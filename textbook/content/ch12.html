Why dissonance wants to move

Hold a dominant seventh chord and wait. The longer it lasts, the more it seems to lean, until the resolution arrives with a sense of release. Then listen to a smaller case: C, E and B♭ together. The B♭ sounds as if it is pressing down, and when it falls to A over an F bass, the pressure is gone.

That push and release, tension and resolution, is the motor of tonal music. Dissonant chords are not mistakes; they are the moments that make the consonant ones feel like arrivals.

Consonance and dissonance in common-practice theory. Two-voice intervals were classified like this:

ClassIntervalsSemitones
perfect consonancesunison, octave, fifth0, 12, 7
imperfect consonancesthirds, sixths3, 4, 8, 9
dissonancesseconds, sevenths, tritone1, 2, 10, 11, 6
dissonant only against the bassperfect fourth5

A dissonance was expected to be prepared or approached by step and to resolve by step, usually down, into a consonance. The standard resolutions are small motions in one voice:

  • a seventh falls to a sixth (7–6) or, in a seventh chord, to the third of the next chord;
  • a fourth above the bass falls to a third (4–3);
  • a ninth falls to an octave (9–8);
  • a diminished fifth closes to a major third and an augmented fourth opens to a minor sixth (Chapter 10).

Why does a dissonance want to move? No single explanation covers everything, and three are commonly given. Each captures part of the story:

  1. Sensory roughness. Close partials of the two notes beat rapidly against each other, and the ear hears the result as rough (Chapter 3). Moving one voice a step can remove most of the roughness.
  2. Voice leading. Every dissonance is one small step away from a consonance, so it is easy to hear where it will go. A sound that implies its own continuation creates expectation.
  3. Learned expectation. Listeners who know a style have heard thousands of resolutions, and their expectations carry the habits of that style. The perfect fourth is a clear case: it is acoustically smooth, yet over the bass it sounds unstable to ears trained on common-practice music.
With harmonic timbres the golden triad is rougher than major, minor and augmented, but smoother than diminished, and its instability is concentrated in the golden fifth. Played on golden-spectrum timbres, which exist only digitally, the order flips and the golden triad becomes the smoothest chord: dissonance depends on timbre. See measuring golden chords and the timbre flip.

Exercise 12.1. For each dissonant interval above the bass, name the consonance it reaches when its upper note moves down by step: a minor seventh, a perfect fourth, a ninth.

Answer

The minor seventh (10 semitones) falls to a sixth: the major sixth (9) if the scale step is a semitone, the minor sixth (8) if it is a whole tone. This is the 7–6 resolution. The perfect fourth (5) falls to a major or minor third (4 or 3), the 4–3. The ninth (14) falls to the octave (12), the 9–8. A second above the bass is the one case usually resolved the other way: the bass falls a step under the held upper note, turning the second into a third (the 2–3 bass suspension).

Passing and neighbor tones

A melody does not have to stay on the notes of the chord beneath it. It can walk between them, step to a neighboring note and come back, or reach ahead. These notes outside the chord are the small dissonances that make melodies flow.

A chord tone belongs to the chord that is sounding; a non-chord tone (or nonharmonic tone) does not. Non-chord tones are classified by how they are approached and left:

A non-chord tone on a weak beat is unaccented and passes almost unnoticed; one on a strong beat is accented and is heard as a dissonance that leans on its resolution. The passing tone F over C in the first bar of the example is unaccented. The suspension and the appoggiatura, the subject of the next section, are the classic accented types.

36-ET adds passing tones that split a semitone into three sixth-tones. A leading tone can rise B → ^B → vC → C through two passing tones a sixth-tone apart. When three voices move together in this way, one holding, one rising and one falling, their passing tones can form golden chords (splitting the semitone).

Exercise 12.2. Over a C major chord, a melody goes G A B C. Which notes are non-chord tones, and what type are they?

Answer

A and B are both non-chord tones over C. Together they fill the gap between the chord tones G and C by step in one direction, so both are passing tones (a "double passing tone"). If the chord changed to G under B, the B would become a chord tone.

Suspensions and appoggiaturas

A suspension is a note that refuses to leave. One chord sounds; then the harmony changes, but one voice holds on to its old note, which now clashes with the new chord. A moment later it gives in and steps down into place. The sound is one of the most expressive in music: a sigh, a delay, a held breath.

An appoggiatura is more forceful. It arrives by a leap onto a dissonant note, right on the beat, and then steps down. Compare the phrase with and without it.

The three stages of a suspension.

  1. Preparation: the note is a consonant chord tone (C in the C major chord).
  2. Suspension: the harmony changes under it, usually on a strong beat, and the held note becomes a dissonance (C over the G bass is a fourth above it).
  3. Resolution: the note falls by step to a chord tone of the new harmony (C falls to B, the third of G).

Suspensions are named by the intervals above the bass at the suspension and the resolution:

The sus chord of popular music (Csus4 = C F G, Csus2 = C D G) is a suspension that need not resolve: the third is replaced, not delayed, and the chord is used as a color in its own right.

The appoggiatura (from Italian appoggiare, "to lean") is accented and unprepared: it is approached by leap and resolves by step, usually in the opposite direction. Because it is not prepared, it is heard as more insistent than a suspension. The cadential six-four of Chapter 10 is often described as a double appoggiatura (or double suspension) over the dominant bass: the sixth and fourth above G fall to the fifth and third.

The golden six-four takes the cadential six-four into 36-ET, but in the other direction: instead of the upper voices falling onto the dominant, they rise through sixth-tones into the tonic, G – vE♭T13/G – vCg/G – C, over the held G. See the golden six-four.

Intervals. In semitones above the bass: 4–3 is $5 \to 4$ (or $5 \to 3$ over a minor chord); 7–6 is $10 \to 9$ or $10 \to 8$, or $11 \to 9$, depending on the qualities; 9–8 is $14 \to 12$ (or $13 \to 12$ for a minor ninth). In each case exactly one voice moves, by one or two semitones.

Exercise 12.3. In the chord progression F – G – C, place a 4–3 suspension in the G chord. Which note of the F chord can be held, and what does it resolve to?

Answer

The fourth above G is C, which is in the F chord (F A C). Hold C from F into G (the suspension), then let it fall to B over G (the resolution), and then B rises to C in the final chord. The preparation (F), the suspension and the resolution give the classic pattern.

Passing chords

Sometimes a whole chord is "passing": it has no job of its own except to connect two other chords smoothly. Listen for three kinds: a six-four chord slipped between a chord and its inversion, a diminished seventh chord on a chromatic bass note, and an augmented triad made by nudging one voice up a semitone.

A passing chord arises when several voices move by step at once: each voice has a passing tone, and together they form a chord. It usually falls on a weak beat and has no harmonic function of its own.

Passing chordProgression in CHow it is made

In 12-ET an evenly spaced passing chord exists only when every voice moves by an even number of semitones (holds and whole steps), so that each voice has a note exactly halfway. A voice that moves by a semitone must move either early or late. That limitation disappears in 36-ET, which has two notes inside every semitone.

In 36-ET every stepwise voice leading has passing chords: each semitone splits into three sixth-steps, each whole tone into three thirds of a tone. The golden system's passing harmony comes in two systems:
  • System 1, the strict path (SongMap ~): every voice moves in equal parts, so the path is unique. There are 288 strict paths through golden chords (240 with tertian endpoints, 12 types up to transposition and reversal); a strict path never has two golden chords in a row, and the partner chord is bronze (168 paths) or silver (72). See the four-path theorem and never twice.
  • System 2, the natural path (SongMap ~g): whole steps may split unevenly. Then 46 chord pairs get two golden passing chords and 181 at least one, including IV → V, which System 1 cannot do: F – vF♯g – ^F♯g – G. See the golden escalator.

Passing chords as averages. If chord $X$ moves to chord $Y$ with voice $i$ moving $d_i$ semitones, a single passing chord halfway is $(X + Y)/2$, which lies on the 12-ET grid only if every $d_i$ is even (held voices and whole steps). In 36-ET, with pitches in sixth-steps, the two chords $$P_1 = \frac{2X + Y}{3}, \qquad P_2 = \frac{X + 2Y}{3}$$ always lie on the grid, because $Y - X = 3d$ is divisible by 3. These are the strict passing chords of System 1, $P_k = X + k\,d$. The golden ones are exactly the cases where $P_1$ or $P_2$ is a transposition of $\{0, 11, 22\}$ (the averaging principle).

Exercise 12.4. Between C (C E G) and A minor (C E A), how many passing chords does 12-ET offer if each voice moves in equal steps? How many does 36-ET offer, and what are they?

Answer

Only G moves, by a whole tone (2 semitones). 12-ET offers one midpoint, C E G♯ (= Caug), the chromatic passing chord. In 36-ET the G moves 6 sixth-steps, 2 per step, so the passing notes are G + 2 = vA♭ and G + 4 = ^A♭, giving C E vA♭ and C E ^A♭. Neither is golden: with only one voice moving, both passing chords keep two notes on the 12-ET layer, while a golden triad has one note on each layer. The 12-ET midpoint G♯ (3 sixth-steps above G) is not on this path.

Tension curves

Listen to a short phrase and imagine drawing a line that rises when the music feels tense and falls when it relaxes. For this phrase, many listeners' lines climb through the middle, peak on the held note over the dominant and on the dominant seventh, and fall at the end. That line is a tension curve.

Music psychologists study tension by asking listeners to report it continuously, for example by moving a slider while the music plays. Several ingredients seem to feed into the result:

  • sensory dissonance, the roughness of each sonority;
  • tonal distance, how far a chord or key is from the tonic in a space of keys and chords;
  • expectation, how strongly the music implies a continuation (a suspension, a dominant, a leading tone);
  • melodic contour, register, loudness and tempo.

Fred Lerdahl's Tonal Pitch Space (2001) built a theory of tonal tension from the first three. It measures distances between chords and keys in a hierarchical pitch space, adds surface dissonance, and accumulates tension through the hierarchical structure of a phrase. Lerdahl and Carol Krumhansl compared the model's predictions with listeners' tension ratings ("Modeling Tonal Tension", Music Perception, 2007) and reported close agreement for the pieces they studied.

The difference is a good summary of this chapter: tension is partly in the sound and partly in what the sound leads you to expect.

Golden passing chords add a new kind of small tension: a sub-chromatic step off the 12-ET grid. On harmonic timbres they sit just above their neighbors in roughness, as passing chords should, and arriving on a Western chord is release. In the author's model a golden home is a local minimum of tension, a shallow rest beside the deep one of C major, so its tension curve dips twice: a little on the golden chord, fully on the Western one (rational visitation). On digital golden-spectrum timbres the measured order flips, the laboratory setting of the timbre flip. Whether golden harmony offers listeners any particular state beyond its sound is an open hypothesis, discussed in Chapter 41.

The roughness model. For two sine partials of frequencies $f_1 < f_2$ and amplitudes $a_1, a_2$, the model used in this book (Sethares' parametrization of the Plomp–Levelt curve, as in reference/golden/explore.py) assigns

$$r(f_1, f_2) = \min(a_1, a_2)\,\big(e^{-3.5\,s\,(f_2 - f_1)} - e^{-5.75\,s\,(f_2 - f_1)}\big), \qquad s = \frac{0.24}{0.0207\,f_1 + 18.96}.$$

A chord's roughness is the sum of $r$ over all pairs of partials of all its notes, each note having partials $k f$ with amplitude $0.88^{k-1}$ for $k = 1, \dots, 8$. With this model the close C major triad on C4 measures 1.319, the canonical value of Chapter 26. The factor $s$ shrinks as $f_1$ grows, so the curve scales with the critical band: the same interval is rougher low in the register than high.
