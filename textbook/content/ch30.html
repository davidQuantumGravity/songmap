Melody in golden7 and golden13

Chapter 13 built Western melody from a handful of ideas: contour, steps and leaps, motifs, phrases that breathe and cadence (Chapter 13). This chapter asks what happens to those ideas when the scale under the melody is not the diatonic but its golden counterpart, golden7: C, C♯, vE, vF, ^G, ^A♭, B.

Listen first to the scale itself, then to a short tune that uses it. The scale has a strange gait. It moves in pairs: a tight semitone, then a wide step about the size of a small minor third, then a semitone again. Four of its seven steps are semitones. A melody in golden7 therefore keeps alternating between leaning (the semitones) and striding (the wide steps), and it has a semitone below its tonic, B, just as C major has.

The tune spells the golden triad, leans from ^A♭ down to ^G, arpeggiates vEg from B, and closes C–B–C. It is played on the digital golden-spectrum bell, the laboratory timbre of Chapter 26, on which the golden triad measures smoother than any Western triad; there the tune sounds settled rather than strange. (No physical instrument has this spectrum; on harmonic instruments C major remains the deepest rest.)

The larger golden scale, golden13, sounds at first like a chromatic scale, every step close to a semitone. Three of its steps are a little smaller. At those three seams the line slips from one 12-ET layer to another.

The two step sizes of golden7

golden7 is the seven-note well-formed scale of the golden chain (Chapter 23): stack three golden sixths up and three down from C and fold them into one octave. In 36-ET sixth-steps its notes are 0, 3, 11, 14, 22, 25, 33 and its steps are 3, 8, 3, 8, 3, 8, 3. In exact tuning the two step sizes are

  • the golden apotome $\varphi^3/4$ = 99.27 c (36-ET: 3 sixth-steps = 100 c), four times;
  • the large step $8/\varphi^{4}$ = 267.64 c, which is two golden apotomes plus a golden limma (36-ET: 8 sixth-steps = 266.67 c), three times.

The large step is within 0.77 c of the septimal minor third 7/6 (266.87 c). That is a numerical coincidence, not part of the construction, but it is a useful picture: the wide steps are much closer in size to a small minor third than to a whole tone.

The closest Western relative of this gait is not the diatonic but the hexatonic scale C, C♯, E, F, G♯, A, whose steps alternate 1 and 3 semitones (Chapter 19). golden7 alternates 1 and 2⅔ semitones and has one extra semitone, so it is not symmetric: it has a single place, around C, where two semitones meet (B–C–C♯).

Contour in golden13

golden13 (C C♯ D vE♭ vE vF vF♯ ^F♯ ^G ^A♭ ^A B♭ B) has ten golden apotomes (99.27 c) and three golden limmas (69.10 c, 36-ET: 2 sixth-steps). The limmas sit exactly where the scale changes layer: D→vE♭, vF♯→^F♯ and ^A→B♭. The layers divide it into runs of semitones: {B♭, B, C, C♯, D} on the 12-ET layer, {vE♭, vE, vF, vF♯} below, {^F♯, ^G, ^A♭, ^A} above. A golden13 melody behaves like a chromatic line with three seams, and a composer can use the seams the way a Western melody uses its two half steps: as places where the line changes color.

Definition. Let $c(j) = 1200\,(j\log_2\varphi \bmod 1)$ be the exact pitch class of chain note $j$. Then golden7 $=\{c(j): -3\le j\le 3\}$ and golden13 $=\{c(j): -6\le j\le 6\}$. In 36-ET chain note $j$ is $25j \bmod 36$.

Proposition (two sizes per generic interval). In golden$n$ for $n\in\{7, 13\}$, each generic interval occurs in exactly two sizes. Status: theorem; it holds for every well-formed scale (Chapter 6), and the table above was computed exhaustively from exact φ.

Proposition (layers of a step). A step of 3 sixth-steps keeps the layer and a step of 8 or 2 changes it, since $3\equiv 0$, $8\equiv 2$ and $2\equiv 2 \pmod 3$. Hence golden7 splits into the layer clusters $\{33,0,3\}$, $\{11,14\}$, $\{22,25\}$ and golden13 into $\{30,33,0,3,6\}$, $\{8,11,14,17\}$, $\{19,22,25,28\}$.

Exact step sizes. golden apotome $\varphi^3/4$: $1200(3\log_2\varphi-2) = 99.27$ c. Golden limma $2^7/\varphi^{10}$: $1200(7-10\log_2\varphi)=69.10$ c. The golden7 large step is chain distance 4 inverted: $1200(3-4\log_2\varphi) = 267.64$ c $= 2\times 99.27 + 69.10$.

Golden motifs and their transformations

A motif is a short shape that a piece can repeat, vary and develop (Chapter 13). The simplest way to hear what golden7 does to a motif is to keep the shape and change the scale: write a tune as scale degrees, play it in C major, then play the same degrees in C golden7. Contour and rhythm survive; every step changes size.

Next, one golden7 motif in five versions: as written, upside down, backwards, moved up the golden chain into a neighbouring golden key, and shifted up one degree inside the scale.

The transplant keeps the motif's generic intervals (counted in degrees) and changes the specific ones (counted in cents). Two degrees are the same pitch in both scales: the tonic C and the leading tone B. This is the whole intersection of C major and C golden7, a fact we use for dialogue in the last section.

Inversion is exact in golden7

Inverting a motif turns every rising interval into a falling one. In a diatonic scale there are two ways to do it. Tonal inversion mirrors the degrees (degree $k$ becomes $-k$), and real inversion mirrors the exact pitches. In C major they disagree: mirrored about C, degree 1 (D) becomes degree −1 (B), but the exact mirror of D about C is B♭. The major scale is symmetric only about D, the Dorian center (Chapter 6).

golden7 is symmetric about its own tonic. The chain runs from −3 to +3 around C, so mirroring about C sends chain note $j$ to $-j$, which is again in the scale: C♯ ↔ B, vE ↔ ^A♭, vF ↔ ^G. So in golden7 tonal and real inversion about the tonic are the same operation. The inverted motif of the second example (C B ^A♭ vF ^A♭ B C) is the exact mirror image of the original (C C♯ vE ^G vE C♯ C), interval for interval, and it never leaves the scale. golden13 has the same property for the same reason.

Two kinds of transposition

  • Real transposition by a chain step moves every note up a golden sixth (25 sixth-steps) or down a golden third. The shape is preserved exactly, and the motif lands in a neighbouring golden key that differs from C golden7 by one note. Up one chain step (to ^A♭ golden7) the note B is replaced by ^A, and the transposed motif in the example uses that ^A. This is the golden counterpart of moving to the dominant key and picking up its F♯ (Chapter 32).
  • Tonal transposition by a scale degree keeps the motif in golden7 but changes interval sizes. The motif C–C♯–vE (small, large) moved up one degree becomes C♯–vE–vF (large, small). Sequences built this way have the uneven, self-varying quality of diatonic sequences.

Retrograde and the rhythmic transformations, augmentation and diminution, work exactly as in Chapter 13 (inversion, retrograde, augmentation): they do not care what scale the notes come from.

One more transformation is new in 36-ET: the golden transform $M_{11}$, multiplication of every pitch class by 11. It turns the three-note sixth-step cluster C ^C vC♯ into the golden triad C vE ^G, so it can turn a narrow, chromatic motif into a golden arpeggio. It is defined and studied in Chapter 33.

Proposition (inversional symmetry). Let $S_n = \{25j \bmod 36 : -m\le j\le m\}$ with $n=2m+1$ (golden3, golden7, golden13, …). Then $-S_n = S_n$, so the inversion $I_0: x\mapsto -x$ maps the scale to itself and fixes the tonic. For golden7 the induced map on degrees is $k\mapsto -k \pmod 7$, so tonal and real inversion about degree 0 coincide. Status: theorem (immediate; checked for golden7 and golden13).

Proposition (chain transposition). $T_{25}S_n$ (the chain window $[-m+1, m+1]$) shares $n-1$ notes with $S_n$; the note at chain position $-m$ leaves and the note at $m+1$ enters. For golden7, $T_{25}$ replaces B (chain −3) by ^A (chain +4) and $T_{11}$ (one chain step down) replaces C♯ by vE♭.

Contrast. The C major set $\{0,2,4,5,7,9,11\}$ (in semitones) is mapped to itself by $x\mapsto 4-x$, that is, inversion about D (pitch class 2), and by no inversion about C.

Counterpoint with golden voices

Western counterpoint rests on a sorting of intervals into perfect consonances, imperfect consonances and dissonances (Chapter 15). That sorting is not a law of nature. It follows from how the partials of harmonic tones line up (Chapter 3), so it changes when the timbre changes.

Here are five dyads on C4 in exact ratios: a perfect fifth, a major third, a golden sixth, a golden third and a golden fifth. They play first on a harmonic organ tone, then on a digital organ tone with the same envelope whose partials are golden (each partial a power of two times a power of the golden ratio; no physical instrument sounds like this). The golden tone is a little rough even on its own, so listen for the order of the five dyads within each timbre, not for one timbre being smoother overall.

Real instruments are harmonic, so the harmonic column describes the music as it is actually played; the golden column describes the digital laboratory, a place to learn golden counterpoint on its own terms before carrying it back to harmonic instruments.

Proposed rules for golden first species

Status: proposals. Golden counterpoint is open research. The rules below are derived from the measurements above and from the Western rules they imitate. They have not been tested with listeners, and other choices are possible.
  1. Consonance tiers (for digital golden-spectrum voices, from the measured gaps in the table):
    • golden perfect: unison, octave, golden sixth (833.09 c) and golden third (366.91 c), chain distance 0 and ±1;
    • golden imperfect: 1001.46, 466.18, 1100.73 and 733.82 c;
    • mild dissonance: 634.55, 932.36, 565.45 c;
    • sharp dissonance: the three steps, 267.64, 198.54 and 99.27 c.
  2. First species uses only perfect and imperfect intervals. Begin and end on the unison or octave.
  3. No parallel unisons, octaves or golden sixths (they fuse; see the coincidence table). Parallel golden thirds fuse almost as much (6 of 11 partials); we tentatively allow them but not in long chains. Whether listeners hear golden thirds as independent voices is an open question.
  4. Perfect intervals are approached by contrary or oblique motion.
  5. Melodic motion prefers steps (99.27 or 267.64 c). Leaps are golden thirds, golden fifths or golden sixths, recovered by step in the opposite direction, as in Western practice.
  6. The golden clausula. End with the two notes that enclose the tonic: C♯ in the lower voice and B in the upper (1001.46 c apart) moving outward to the octave C–C, or B below and C♯ above (198.54 c) moving inward to the unison. Both voices move by a golden apotome. This mirrors the Western clausula, where D and B move to the octave on C.
  7. Timbre clause. These tiers hold for digital golden-spectrum voices, the laboratory setting. On harmonic voices, that is on every real instrument, the golden third and the 466.18 c interval fall into the rougher half of the chain dyads (ranks 9 and 8 of 12), so a golden counterpoint on harmonic instruments is a texture of gentle tensions, best resolved onto Western consonances, rather than a chain of consonances.

A worked example

The cantus firmus (lower voice) is C vE vF ^G vF vE C♯ C, degrees 0 2 3 4 3 2 1 0. The counterpoint is C B ^A♭ C C♯ vE B C. Every vertical interval is perfect or imperfect; both octaves after the first are reached by contrary motion; the only similar motion leads into imperfect intervals (466.18 c, then 1001.46 c); and the piece ends on the golden clausula C♯/B → C/C. A small search program enumerated every counterpoint above this cantus (upper voice at most twelve degrees above the lower, melodic moves of one to three degrees). 121 satisfy rules 1–3 and the clausula ending; 20 of those also satisfy rule 4, counting the golden third as perfect. The example is one of the 20. The rules are restrictive but far from impossible.

Answer

The table predicts the golden timbre: there the golden third ranks 2nd and 466.18 c 4th of the twelve chain dyads, while on harmonic tones they rank 9th and 8th. Your own judgment is the real data here; this is exactly the kind of listening test the proposed rules still need.

Chain distance classifies golden7 dyads. If $a, b \in$ golden7 have chain positions $j_a, j_b\in[-3,3]$, their pitch-class interval is $c(j_b - j_a)$, with $|j_b-j_a|\le 6$. So exactly twelve interval classes (six chain distances, two octave forms each) occur between two golden7 voices, plus the unison. In 36-ET they are 3, 6, 8, 11, 14, 17, 19, 22, 25, 28, 30, 33 sixth-steps.

The roughness model (as in reference/golden/explore.py): for partials $f_1<f_2$ with amplitudes $a_1,a_2$, roughness $\min(a_1,a_2)\,(e^{-3.5 s\Delta}-e^{-5.75 s\Delta})$ with $\Delta = f_2-f_1$ and $s = 0.24/(0.0207 f_1+18.96)$, summed over all pairs of partials of both tones. Dyads on $f_0 = 261.63$ Hz.

Why the golden sixth fuses on golden tones. The golden spectrum $\{2^m\varphi^n\}$ is closed under multiplication by $\varphi$ except at its edges: $\varphi\cdot 2^m\varphi^n = 2^m\varphi^{n+1}$. Every partial of the upper tone with $n+1\le 3$ and below 9 lands on a partial of the lower tone, which gives the 7 coincidences in the table. For the octave the same count comes from $m\mapsto m+1$. A perfect fifth cannot coincide exactly: $2^m\varphi^n$ is rational only when $n=0$, so $3/2 = 2^m\varphi^n$ has no solution in integers, and numerically no pair of these partials comes within 1 c.

The search. Upper voice above the cantus, within the 12 degrees above each cantus note; first and last intervals octaves; last approached by B; vertical intervals in the perfect or imperfect tiers; melodic steps of 1–3 degrees; no parallel octaves or golden sixths. Exhaustive enumeration gives 121 solutions, and 20 when the perfect tier (unison, octave, golden sixth, golden third) must also be approached by contrary or oblique motion.

Call and response between two worlds

Call and response is one of music's oldest forms: a leader sings, a group or a second voice answers (Chapter 15). In this book's two-world harmony it gets a new meaning. The call can come from the rational world, a harmonic-timbre voice in a Western key over Western chords, and the answer from the golden world, a digital golden-spectrum voice in golden7 over golden chords. Each voice measures as consonant in its own timbre; on real, harmonic instruments the rational answer is always the deeper release.

The two phrases rhyme: the answer has the call's rhythm and contour, but every step is bent into golden7. The first answer ends on C♯, the semitone above the tonic, a question; the second ends B–C, a cadence both worlds share.

What two worlds share

The dialogue works because the two scales overlap very little. C major and C golden7 share exactly two notes, C and B. That is close to the maximum. No major or natural-minor scale on any of the 36 tonics of 36-ET shares more than two notes with C golden7, and none shares more than three with C golden13. The reason is the layer structure: a Western scale lies on one layer, and golden7 has at most three notes on any layer, {B, C, C♯}, which no diatonic scale contains all three of (it would need three consecutive semitones).

Ways to answer

Western call-and-response answers by repetition, variation, contrast or completion. The golden world offers specific versions of each. Status: these are compositional proposals, not results.

  • Transplant answer: same degrees, other scale (the example). The answer sounds like the call heard through a prism.
  • Mirror answer: the golden inversion about C, which stays in golden7 exactly. The answer bends where the call rose.
  • Chain answer: real transposition by one chain step, landing in a neighbouring golden key. The answer seems to come from slightly elsewhere.
  • Harmonic answer: the rational voice's chord answered by the golden chord a few sixth-steps away (C by Cg, 2 sixth-steps; Cm by Cg, 3; A♭ by Cg, 4: Chapter 31). The melody can stay; only the world under it changes.
  • Timbre roles: in the digital laboratory, keep the rational voice on harmonic timbres and the golden voice on golden-spectrum timbres, so that each measures as consonant when it speaks. On real instruments both voices are harmonic, the golden answer is the delicate local rest and the rational call or answer the global one.
Call and response is found across many traditions: responsorial and antiphonal singing in liturgical chant, the call-and-response forms of many West and Central African musics, and their descendants in work songs, spirituals, gospel, blues and jazz (Chapter 15). A dialogue between two tuning worlds, as here, is an extension proposed in this book.

Exercise 30.5. Show that C golden13 and C major share exactly three notes, and name them.

Answer

C major lies on layer 0, where golden13 has B♭, B, C, C♯, D. Of these C major contains B, C and D. So the common tones are B, C and D.

Theorem (small overlap). Let $D$ be any major-scale set on any of the 36 tonics of 36-ET (natural minor scales are the same sets). Then $|D\cap \text{golden7}_C|\le 2$ and $|D\cap\text{golden13}_C|\le 3$, and both bounds are attained.

Proof. $D$ lies in one coset $\ell + 3\mathbb{Z}_{36}$ (one layer). golden7 meets the layers in $\{33,0,3\}$, $\{11,14\}$ and $\{22,25\}$. The first is three consecutive semitones (B, C, C♯), and a diatonic set never contains three consecutive semitones, so at most two; the other two layers hold only two notes each. golden13 meets the layers in five, four and four consecutive semitones; a diatonic set contains at most three notes of any four or five consecutive semitones (they span at most a major third). Attainment: C major gives {C, B} and {B, C, D}. An exhaustive check over all 36 tonics confirms both bounds. ∎
