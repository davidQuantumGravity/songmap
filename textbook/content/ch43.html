A song as a text file

Every example in this book was made the same way: someone wrote a short text file, and a program turned it into sound. Here is one of them. Listen first: a four-chord progression on an organ with a bass, played twice, the second time with a glass bell arpeggiating each chord, and at the end of each phrase a dominant chord that slides into the tonic through two passing chords over a held G: one inner voice climbs B – ^B – vC – C one sixth-step (a third of a semitone) at a time, and the top voice climbs D – vE♭ – ^E♭ – E two sixth-steps at a time.

The program is SongMap, written for this project. Nothing in the file says how to make an organ sound, how to voice a chord or how loud anything is in decibels of raw signal: those things live in a fixed engine. The file says only what a musician would say: tempo, key, chords, which instrument plays what, and how the parts are balanced.

The file. This is the complete source of the example:

songmap: 0.2
title: "A first song map"
tempo: 90
meter: "4/4"
key: "C major"
tuning: 36edo
seed: 1
tail_seconds: 3
instruments: {organ: clean/organ, low: clean/bass, bell: clean/glass}
motifs:
  m1: {rel: "R8 3 5 R+ 5 3 R 3"}
sections:
  - {name: a, bars: 4, harmony: "C:1 Am:1 F:1 G:0.5 ~:0.25 ~:0.25", parts: {organ: hold, low: bassline}}
  - {name: b, bars: 4, harmony: "C:1 Am:1 F:1 G:0.5 ~:0.25 ~:0.25", parts: {organ: hold, low: bassline, bell: {motifs: [m1]}}}
  - {name: end, bars: 1, harmony: "C:1", parts: {organ: hold, low: {style: roots, role: bass}}}
mix: {organ: {gain_db: -6}, low: {gain_db: -4}, bell: {gain_db: -9, send: {hall: 0.3}}}
master: clean

It is written in YAML, a plain-text format of keys and values. (SongMap also reads songs written in its own compact language, .sm files, which compile to the same thing; this book's examples all use YAML.) Reading it top to bottom:

FieldMeaning

tempo, meter, key90 beats per minute, four beats per bar, home key C major
tuning12edo or 36edo: which pitch grid the song may use (§43.2)
seedevery random choice the engine makes derives from it, so a render is reproducible
tail_secondssilence after the last bar, for the sound to ring out
instrumentsthe ensemble: part name → instrument/preset

sectionsthe form: each section has a length in bars, a harmony and a part list

partswhich parts play, and in which style (§43.4)
mixper-part gain in decibels and the amount sent to a hall reverb
masterclean: scale the final peak and nothing else (Chapter 44)

Why text. A song as a short text file has practical virtues. It can be read, compared line by line with an earlier version, and changed by a person or by a program; it is small enough for a language model to write and revise; and it is exact: the file, not a mouse gesture, is the score. The engine behind it is written once and improves over time, while each song stays a few lines long. The same file can also drive video: SongMap writes the time and video frame of every section and event to a cues.json file (Chapter 45).

The pipeline. Rendering goes through fixed stages:

StageWhat happens
parsethe YAML is read and validated; errors say what is wrong and, where possible, how to fix it
arrangeharmony strings become a timeline of chords, placeholders become passing chords, styles and motifs become notes
performarticulation sets how long each written note is held; notes are played on the grid as written (a performance layer with groove and humanizing is planned, and the clean examples do not need it)
instrumentseach note is synthesized or played from a recorded sample; the source chooses the voice (Chapter 44)
mix and masterparts are balanced, sent to reverb and summed; the clean master scales the peak
exportaudio, per-part stems, cues.json and a QA report of measured numbers

Checking the file without rendering it prints a summary:

$ songmap check first_song.songmap.yaml
ok: A first song map
  3 sections, 9 bars at 90 BPM 4/4, 0:27.00 (810 frames @ 30 fps)
  parts: organ 13, low 14, bell 32

(A last line, omitted here, gives the number of events and a fingerprint of the expanded note list.) The counts are note events: the organ strikes 13 chords, the bass plays 14 notes, and the bell plays 32 eighth notes in section b.

Timing. With tempo $T$ beats per minute and $b$ beats per bar, one beat lasts $60/T$ seconds and one bar $60b/T$. A token G:0.5 lasts half a bar, $0.5\,b = 2$ beats. The song length is

$$\text{seconds} = \frac{60\,b}{T}\sum_{\text{sections}} \text{bars} + \text{tail}.$$

Here $60\cdot 4/90 = 2.667$ s per bar, $9$ bars $= 24$ s, plus a 3 s tail: 27.00 s, as songmap check reports. Video frames are $\operatorname{round}(\text{seconds}\times\text{fps})$; at 30 fps that is 810.

Frame-exact beats. A beat lands on a whole video frame exactly when $60\cdot\text{fps}/T$ is an integer. At 90 BPM and 30 fps it is $1800/90 = 20$ frames per beat. At 80 BPM it would be 22.5, and songmap check warns that beats do not land on whole frames.

Harmony per section. A section's harmony string is laid along its bars and repeats if it is shorter than the section (progressions loop). In the example each harmony string sums to $1+1+1+0.5+0.25+0.25 = 4$ bars, the section length.

Reproducibility. A render depends only on the file and the engine version: the seed fixes every random choice, and the clean instruments use none (§44.2). The engine's test suite keeps a hash of the complete expanded note list of a set of locked example songs (the ported soundtrack examples and the first golden étude), so any change in their output is detected.

Golden notation in SongMap

The first example writes the averaging principle as chord symbols: C, Cm and Caug, then their average Cg; then C, Cm and Cdim, then their average Cs. It ends by playing Cg and then C(v3,^5): two spellings of the same chord, which sound identical.

The second example is a melody written as scale degrees of the golden diatonic, golden7, over golden chords taken from the same scale. The bell climbs the scale's tones C, vE, ^G, B and the octave, then falls back, mostly by step, and turns around vE before landing on C.

Tuning. tuning: 36edo switches the song to 36-ET, the grid of sixth-steps of 33.33 c (Chapter 24). A 12edo song that uses a sixth-step anywhere is an error whose message names the fix, so a Western piece can never drift off the grid by a typo.

Ups and downs. A sixth-step up is ^, down is v, always written as a prefix (Chapter 24). The same marks work everywhere a pitch can be written:

WhereExamples
letter notes in motifs^c4 (C up, quarter note), ve8 (E down, eighth), veb+ (E♭ down, an octave up)
scale degrees^3, v5/8. (degree 5 down, dotted eighth)
chord-relative tonesv3, ^5
chord roots^C, vEb, ^F#m7
keyskey: ^D minor, key: vE golden13

Chord symbols. On top of the Western qualities (C, Cm, Cdim, Caug, Csus4, C7, Cmaj7, Cm7, Cm7b5, Cdim7, C6, Cm6, C9, Cadd9 and others), 36edo songs have the golden family:

Golden keys. key: C golden7, golden13, silver13 (and golden3, 4, 10, 23) make the golden scales of Chapter 23 available to scale-degree motifs. In golden7, degree 0 is C, 1 is C♯, 2 is vE, 3 is vF, 4 is ^G, 5 is ^A♭, 6 is B and 7 is the octave. The melody of the second example is

tune: {deg: "0/4 2 4 6 | 7/2 6/4 4 | 3/4 2 1 2 | 0/1", octave: 5}

which reads: degree 0 as a quarter note, then degrees 2, 4, 6 (durations are sticky), the octave as a half note, and so on: C5 vE5 ^G5 B5 | C6 B5 ^G5 | vF5 vE5 C♯5 vE5 | C5.

From a name to a frequency. SongMap's pitch unit is the semitone (MIDI number), and in 36edo a pitch may be a multiple of 1/3. A written note with letter, accidental and octave has MIDI number $m$ (C4 = 60), and $u$ ups and $d$ downs move it to $m + (u-d)/3$. In sixth-steps, $s = 3m + u - d$, and with A4 = 440 Hz ($s = 207$),

$$f = 440\cdot 2^{(s - 207)/36}\ \text{Hz}.$$

Example: vE4 has $m = 64$, $s = 191$, $f = 440\cdot 2^{-16/36} = 323.34$ Hz. The exact golden third above C4 (261.63 Hz) is $261.63\cdot 2/\varphi = 323.39$ Hz: the 36-ET note is 0.24 c flat.

Chord qualities as interval lists (in semitones, as the parser stores them):

$$\texttt{g} = \left(0, \tfrac{11}{3}, \tfrac{22}{3}\right),\quad \texttt{g7} = \left(0, \tfrac{11}{3}, \tfrac{22}{3}, 11\right),\quad \texttt{s} = \left(0, \tfrac{10}{3}, \tfrac{20}{3}\right),\quad \texttt{T13} = \left(0, \tfrac{13}{3}, \tfrac{26}{3}\right).$$

An alteration list adds $\pm\tfrac13$ per mark to the named chord tone. For C(v3,^5): $(0, 4, 7) + (0, -\tfrac13, +\tfrac13) = (0, \tfrac{11}{3}, \tfrac{22}{3})$, which is exactly Cg.

Golden scales from the chain. A golden scale is a window of the golden chain, whose generator φ is 25 sixth-steps. golden7 is the chain from index $-3$ to $3$:

$$\{25j \bmod 36 : j = -3,\dots,3\} = \{33, 22, 11, 0, 25, 14, 3\} = \{0, 3, 11, 14, 22, 25, 33\},$$

that is C, C♯, vE, vF, ^G, ^A♭, B. golden13 is the window $-6,\dots,6$, and silver13 is golden13 with every ^ and v swapped.

Passing placeholders: ~ and ~g

F to G, IV to V, is one of the most common moves there is. The example plays it twice, with two passing chords in between each time. The first time the passing chords come from System 1, the strict path: every voice moves in equal steps. The second time they come from System 2, the natural path, where two golden chords in a row carry the music up the "golden escalator". Both were written with placeholders, not chord names: the engine worked out which chords lie on the way.

Placeholders. In a harmony string, a token that begins with ~ is not a chord but a request: "put here the passing chord that the voice leading from the previous chord to the next one picks out". The theory behind it is in Chapter 27 (System 1) and Chapter 28 (System 2).

TokenMeaning
~System 1, the strict path: every voice moves uniformly (a half step as 1+1+1 sixth-steps, a whole step as 2+2+2)
~gSystem 2, the natural path: uneven monotone splits allowed, golden chords preferred
~g7System 2, golden sevenths preferred
~ ~, ~g ~gtwo in a row: both passing chords P1 and P2 of one path
~g@1/3, ~g@2/3one placeholder forced to the first or second position of the path

A single placeholder takes the better of the two chords on the path, ranked golden, then golden over a pedal, then silver or bronze, then planing or Western, then micro-altered. Because progressions loop, a placeholder at the end of a string bridges back to its first chord, as in the first example of this chapter.

The F → G rows show the difference between the systems. On the strict path every voice of F → G moves by a whole step or a half step in equal parts, and the chords in between are a micro-altered diminished triad and an incomplete golden seventh: neither is golden, as the quality theorem predicts for a move between roots a whole step apart (Chapter 27). On the natural path two voices split unevenly, and both passing chords become golden triads on the chromatic root between F and G: the golden escalator of Chapter 28. The two piano rolls show the engine's four lines for each system; they are also exactly what the example's held organ plays.

The passing-path algorithm. For a move $X \to Y$ the engine works entirely in integer sixth-steps.

  1. Voices. Four lines: a bass on the root, in the octave from E2 up, and three upper voices, each in the octave from G3 up. For a triad the upper voices are its three tones, or (with a penalty) the root doubled with the third and no fifth; for a seventh chord, third–fifth–seventh or (with a smaller penalty) third–seventh–root.
  2. Targets. Every assignment of $Y$'s upper tones to the three upper voices is tried. Each voice's displacement $D$ is the nearest-way distance, in $[-18, 18)$ sixth-steps. Assignments in which every voice moves at most a whole step ($|D| \le 6$) are preferred; otherwise the ones with least total motion.
  3. Schedules. Each displacement is split into three moves $(d_1, d_2, d_3)$, one per step of the path $X \to P_1 \to P_2 \to Y$:
    • System 1: $(D/3, D/3, D/3)$, uniform. A sixth-step-sized displacement not divisible by 3 moves as early as evenly possible.
    • System 2: a half step stays $(1,1,1)$; other moves may use any monotone split with each part at most 3 sixth-steps (a semitone).
    • A leap larger than a whole step (often the bass) holds and moves only on the arrival chord: a pedal.
  4. Classification. $P_1$ and $P_2$ are named: golden (g, g7), golden over a pedal, equal-third (silver, bronze), Western, planing (a Western chord off the 12-ET layer) or micro-altered (nearest chord plus alterations).
  5. Choice. Among all candidates the engine prefers, in strict order of priority: more golden sevenths (when ~g7 asks for them); more golden chords, counting golden over a pedal (when ~g asks); more exact golden chords; $P_1 \ne P_2$; fewer parallel fifths or octaves between $X$ and $Y$; less total motion (plus the small penalties for incomplete voicings); and finally more even splits.

For System 1 the uniform schedule makes the path unique once the targets are chosen, and voice by voice

$$P_1 = \tfrac{2X + Y}{3}, \qquad P_2 = \tfrac{X + 2Y}{3},$$

the averaging principle of Chapter 25 (voices that leap are held instead, as the bass is here). For V → I in C the engine's lines, in sixth-steps (C4 = 180), are

Voiced styles

A chord symbol says which notes; a style says how they are played. The example takes one progression (C – Am – F – G, with the strict V → I path at the end) and plays it five ways: held chords, a comping rhythm, steady quarter notes, the top and bottom voices alone as melody and bass, and a gentle arpeggio over a bass in half notes. Listen to the passing chords in each: in the held version they are heard as chords, in the line version as a melody that climbs through sixth-steps.

Styles as data. A style is a small YAML file in the engine. The voiced styles share one idea: the song's chords are voiced once, as four parts in the song's tuning, and each style plays some of those voices in some rhythm. Because the voicing is shared, a top melody is exactly the soprano of the hold chords, and a bassline exactly their lowest voice.

StylePlaysRhythm
holdall four voicesstruck on each chord change, held
compall four voicesx..x.x..x..x.x.. (one character per sixteenth): the downbeat, then 0.75, 1.25, 2, 2.75 and 3.25 beats after it, plus every chord change
pulseall four voicesevery quarter note
topthe soprano, an octave upone note per chord
basslinethe lowest voice, an octave downone note per chord

Other styles are chord-relative patterns rather than voiced parts: arpeggios (gentle8, roll16) and bass figures (roots, half-note roots; root5, root and fifth).

What "voice-led" means here. There are two cases.

  • Ordinary chords are voice-led: the style takes the chord's pitch classes and chooses, among all playable voicings in the range D3–D5, the one that moves least from the previous chord (Derive layer).
  • Passing paths are played as the passing engine computed them. A chord produced by ~, ~g or ~g7, and the two chords at the ends of its path, are played on the engine's own four lines (§43.3): the bass and the three upper voices, ordered low to high. The whole path is moved by one octave shift toward the middle of the part's range, so every sub-chromatic line, slash bass included, is heard exactly as computed. The chord names do not change.

So in the strict F → G example the held organ plays the System 1 roll of §43.3 note for note: the bass climbs F – vF♯ – ^F♯ – G two sixth-steps at a time, so that ^F♯ sounds as the slash bass of vA♭g7(v7)/^F♯, and the arrival G is the engine's G–G–B–G, with the fifth omitted. Because the octave shift is chosen from the three upper voices, the engine's bass can lie below the style's range: here it is F2, and the bassline style, an octave lower still, plays F1. When two passing paths in a progression share a chord (… F ~g ~g G ~ ~ C …), the second path continues from the voicing on which the first arrived, but only if that gives the same passing chords; otherwise it starts from its own standard voicing, so a chord's name never depends on its neighbours.

A habit of the voicer. Outside passing paths, because the voicing cost only mildly penalizes a bass that is not the root, held chords often come out with the fifth in the bass (C as G–C–E–C). Several examples in this book therefore add a separate bass part, either a written bass line or the roots style, for a firm cadence.

Candidate voicings. For a chord with pitch classes $\mathcal P$ and a range $[\ell, h]$ (default D3–D5, MIDI 50–74), a candidate is a set of four distinct grid pitches $c_1 < c_2 < c_3 < c_4$ in the range, each in $\mathcal P$, such that

  • every chord tone is present (the fifth may be dropped when the chord has more tones than voices);
  • neighbouring voices are at most an octave apart, and $c_4 - c_1$ is at most two octaves.

Cost. With $r$ the root, the voicing cost is

$$\kappa(c) = \#\{\text{doubled non-root pitch classes}\} + \begin{cases}0 & c_1 \equiv r\\ 2 & 0 < (c_1 - r) \bmod 12 \le 2\\ 0.5 & \text{otherwise.}\end{cases}$$

Choice. For the first chord (or after N.C.), with center $\mu = (\ell + h)/2 = 62$,

$$c^\ast = \arg\min_c\ \big|\bar c - \mu\big| + 0.1\,(c_4 - c_1) + \kappa(c);$$

for every later chord, with $p$ the previous voicing,

$$c^\ast = \arg\min_c\ \sum_{i=1}^{4} |c_i - p_i| + 0.05\,\big|\bar c - \mu\big| + \kappa(c).$$

Distances are in semitones on the song's grid, so in 36edo a voice can move by $\tfrac13$. The root-position voicing C3–C4–E4–G4 is outside the default range (C3 = 48), and the second-inversion voicing G3–C4–E4–C5 scores $0.75 + 1.7 + 0.5 = 2.95$, the lowest of all candidates, against $3.75 + 1.2 + 0 = 4.95$ for the root-position C4–E4–G4–C5: that is the arithmetic behind the box above.

Passing paths. A placeholder's chord and the two chords at the ends of its path carry the engine's lines $(\ell_0, \ell_1, \ell_2, \ell_3)$ in semitones, bass first, the upper lines ordered by their pitch in the first chord. A four-voice style plays $\ell_i + 12k$, with one integer $k$ for the whole path, fixed by the path's first chord:

$$k = \operatorname{round}\!\left(\frac{\mu - \tfrac13(\ell_1 + \ell_2 + \ell_3)}{12}\right).$$

For F → G the upper lines start at A3, C4, F4 (MIDI 57, 60, 65), whose mean 60.67 is within half an octave of $\mu = 62$, so $k = 0$. The ordinary chords after a path are voice-led from its arrival voicing by the rule above.

Exercises

Exercise 43.1 (reading). How long is a song with tempo: 72, meter: "4/4", two sections of 4 bars and tail_seconds: 2? Do its beats land on whole frames at 30 fps?

Answer

A bar lasts $60\cdot 4/72 = 3.333$ s; 8 bars = 26.67 s; plus 2 s = 28.67 s. $60\cdot 30/72 = 25$ frames per beat, an integer: yes.

Exercise 43.2 (notation). Write the frequency of ^G4 in 36-ET and compare it with the exact golden fifth above C4.

Answer

G4 is MIDI 67, so $s = 202$ and $f = 440\cdot 2^{-5/36} = 399.62$ Hz. The exact golden fifth is $261.63\cdot 4/\varphi^2 = 399.73$ Hz; 36-ET is 0.49 c flat.

Exercise 43.3 (calculation). On the strict path C → A♭ (bass C held), the voices C4, E4 and G3 move to C4, E♭4 and A♭3. Compute $P_1$ and name it.

Answer

In sixth-steps: C4 180 → 180; E4 192 → 189, so $P_1$ = 191 (vE4); G3 165 → 168, so $P_1$ = 166 (^G3). $P_1$ = C, vE, ^G = Cg: the golden triad is one third of the way from C to A♭, exactly as the table above reports.

Exercise 43.4 (writing). Write a harmony string for I – vi – IV – V – I in C in which the IV → V move passes through the golden escalator and the V → I move through the golden six-four, each bar of four beats.

Answer

One solution: "C:1 Am:1 F:0.5 ~g:0.25 ~g:0.25 G:0.5 ~:0.25 ~:0.25 C:1". It resolves to C – Am – F – vF♯g – ^F♯g – G – vE♭T13/G – vCg/G – C.

Exercise 43.5 (spelling). Spell the golden7 scale on D (every note two semitones higher than on C).

Answer

Add 6 sixth-steps to 0, 3, 11, 14, 22, 25, 33: 6, 9, 17, 20, 28, 31, 3, that is D, E♭, vF♯, vG, ^A, ^B♭, C♯.
