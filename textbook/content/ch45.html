The workflow

Everything you have heard in this book, from the first pure sine of Chapter 1 to the golden circle of Chapter 44, came out of one working loop between a person and a machine. The person is the author, who had the idea of a golden Pythagorean scale years before any of this was built. The machine is an AI system that writes and runs code. The longest single product of that loop is the Well-Tempered Golden Clavier, a cycle through all 72 major and minor keys of 36-ET and all 36 golden13 and 36 silver13 scales.

This chapter describes how that loop worked: who did what, how claims were checked, and what the machine could not do. It is written in the same spirit as the rest of the book: it states what happened and labels what is opinion.

Why a machine helped. The golden system is small enough to state on a page and large enough to defeat patient hand calculation. There are 36 pitch classes, 36 golden triads, thousands of four-voice paths between chords, and every claim about them has to hold in every key and both passing systems. A person can conjecture "golden chords pass only between triads of the same quality a third or fifth apart"; a program can check it for every pair. The author's own account (in the Preface) is that the system waited years for exactly this kind of help.

What the loop looked like in practice. An idea, usually the author's, was stated in words. The machine restated it precisely, often as code, and enumerated its consequences. The results came back as tables, theorems with their exhaustive checks, and audio. The author listened, accepted, rejected or redirected. Questions went both ways: the author asked whether there is a natural nine-note golden scale (there is not: nine golden sixths give three step sizes, and the natural sub-scale is golden7); the machine asked which fifth the silver triad should have and what to call T13, and the author decided.

Questions with options. The most useful questions from the machine were not open-ended. They laid out the alternatives with their consequences and asked the author to choose. Examples from the project's design notes:

Stating interpretations back. The author's founding notes were written in musical language ("well-defined ways to expand melodies of voice leading"). The machine turned such phrases into precise rules, in this case the uniform sub-chromatic path of Chapter 27, and then said explicitly that this was its reading, asking whether the author meant it or would weight the choices differently. Interpretations are where a collaboration most easily drifts without either side noticing; writing them down as interpretations is the cure.

What "enumerate" means here. The dictionary of transitions covers 30 chords in C major: the 7 diatonic triads, the 7 diatonic sevenths, and 16 chromatic chords (i, iv, ♭III, ♭VI, ♭VII, ♭II, II, III, VI, V7/V, V7/vi, V7/IV, iiø7, ♭VImaj7, I+, vii°7). The ordered pairs of distinct chords number $30\cdot 29 = 870$, partitioned as

$$\underbrace{7\cdot6}_{\text{triads}} + \underbrace{7\cdot6}_{\text{sevenths}} + \underbrace{2\cdot7\cdot7}_{\text{triad}\leftrightarrow\text{seventh}} + \underbrace{14\cdot16}_{\text{diatonic}\to\text{chromatic}} + \underbrace{16\cdot14}_{\text{chromatic}\to\text{diatonic}} + \underbrace{16\cdot15}_{\text{chromatic}} = 42 + 42 + 98 + 224 + 224 + 240 = 870.$$

For each pair, each system, and every assignment of voices and schedule of moves (Chapter 42), the passing engine builds the candidate paths and classifies their chords. A theorem such as "a strict path never contains two golden chords" is then not argued from examples; it is checked on every path, and where possible also proved by hand (Appendix D).

The census. Over the 870 pairs, System 1 yields 32 exact golden passing chords and 122 golden chords over a pedal; System 2 yields 227 and 299, with 46 pairs passing through two different golden chords and 181 through at least one (Chapter 28).

Verifying instead of trusting

Here is a claim you can check with your own ears. Two chords, in exact ratios on 110 Hz: the golden triad, then the golden seventh. Listen to the beating inside each. The triad's strongest beats are at about 6.1 and 7.6 per second, with fainter, faster ones from about 12.3 up. The seventh adds a beat at about 9.4. An early summary of this project got the relation between these numbers wrong, and the author caught it.

And a second one. The founding vision of the project said that golden chords, built on the most irrational number, must be "the least stable chords". Listen to six triads in close position on middle C, in the order a roughness model ranks them for harmonic tones: sus4, major, minor, augmented, golden, diminished. The golden triad is near the rough end, but it is not last.

The habit. A language model writes fluent, plausible text, and a plausible wrong number reads exactly like a right one. The only defense is a habit: never state a number that was not computed, never state a theorem that was not checked, and say what kind of claim each statement is. The book's three labels, theorem, measurement and hypothesis, come from that habit.

Errors came from every side. The first correction was the author's, of the machine. The second corrected the author's own founding intuition, by measurement. The historical slips are ones that people repeat everywhere. The last three were found by the machine, in the project's own documents and code, only because each claim was checked against the running program before it went into this book.

Rules that worked.

  1. Compute, then write. Every number in this book was produced by code or copied from a computed table.
  2. Check one way, then another. The golden-seventh beats were derived algebraically, then counted by brute force over partial pairs, then seen in the envelope spectrum of the rendered audio.
  3. Exhaust, don't sample. Where a statement is about all chords or all paths, check all of them.
  4. Keep canonical values. One table of exact values (φ = 833.09 c, the golden comma 30.17 c, …) that every chapter copies, so that no rounding drifts.
  5. Label the status. A theorem, a measurement under a model, or a hypothesis.
  6. Let the ear have the last word on taste, and only on taste.

Three independent checks of one claim. The claim: an exact close-voiced Cg7 on harmonic tones beats at $b_1 = f_0\varphi^{-6}$, $b_2 = b_1\cdot 2/\varphi$, $b_3 = b_1\cdot 4/\varphi^2$ and $2b_1$.

1. Algebra. Partial 5 of a tone at $f$ and partial 4 of the tone a golden third above it, at $2f/\varphi$, differ by $f(5 - 8/\varphi) = f(13 - 8\varphi) = f\varphi^{-6}$, using $1/\varphi = \varphi - 1$. Applied to the three consecutive golden thirds of Cg7 (root–third, third–fifth, fifth–seventh), with $f = f_0,\ 2f_0/\varphi,\ 4f_0/\varphi^2$, this gives $b_1, b_2, b_3$. And $8/\varphi^3 = 2 - 2\varphi^{-6}$ (both sides equal 1.8885438199983176 in double precision), so the seventh's fundamental lies $2f_0\varphi^{-6}$ below the root's second partial.

2. Brute force. List every pair of partials (8 per tone) from different chord tones whose frequencies differ by less than 25 Hz, and weight each by the smaller amplitude:

for i, a in enumerate(notes):
    for b in notes[i+1:]:
        for k in range(1, 9):
            for l in range(1, 9):
                df = abs(k*a - l*b)
                if 0.5 < df < 25:
                    beats[round(df, 2)] += min(k**-1.2, l**-1.2)

For Cg7 on 110 Hz the strongest rates are 12.26, 6.13, 7.58, 24.52 and 9.37 Hz; for the triad alone, 6.13, 7.58 and then weaker components at 12.26, 19.84 and 24.52 Hz, with no 9.37. The seventh's own ratio would predict a beat at $b_1\cdot 8/\varphi^3 = 11.58$ Hz: it does not appear.

3. Measurement. In the rendered audio of the stable Cg7 (Chapter 43), the spectrum of the amplitude envelope has peaks at 6.12 and 12.25 Hz (within the 0.125 Hz resolution of an 8 s analysis window), among the largest in the 3–30 Hz band.

Agreement of three methods that share no code is much stronger evidence than any one of them, and much stronger than a fluent sentence.

What a machine cannot judge

Listen to a finished piece from the golden suite, a lullaby that ends every phrase on the golden six-four. Every chord in it was computed, every voice was led by an algorithm, every partial of every tone was placed by a formula. Whether it is beautiful is not in any of those numbers.

No ears. The machine in this project did not listen. It read numbers: loudness, true peak, band balance, chord symbols, beat rates. SongMap was designed so that every render produces such a report, precisely so that a program can check its own work without ears. That makes many errors catchable (a clipped peak, a wrong chord, a beat outside its intended band) and leaves the most important question untouched: how does it sound to a person?

Proxies, and their limits. Some numbers are good proxies for listening. A roughness model predicts, for a given timbre, which of two chords most listeners will call smoother; a beat spectrum predicts which pulsations a chord will produce; a loudness measurement predicts which of two excerpts will seem louder. The project used them constantly. But each proxy is a model with assumptions (a spectrum, a register, a listener with typical hearing), and each answers a narrower question than "is this good?". The roughness of the golden triad on a golden spectrum is a fact under the model; whether a listener prefers that sound is a separate fact, to be found out.

What remained the author's.

  • Taste. Which of the 870 transitions are worth hearing, which voicing flatters a golden chord, how long a passing chord should last. The engine ranks candidates by rules; the rules themselves were the author's taste written down, and the author decided by ear whether the results were right.
  • What to explore. A machine explores where it is pointed, and it is easiest to point it at what is easy to compute. The decision to explore golden passing harmony first, and golden homes second, was a musical choice.
  • Meaning and function. Whether a golden7 progression feels like tonic, predominant and dominant is an open question (Chapter 31). It can be proposed by computation (for example, ranking chords by golden-circle distance from Cg); it can only be answered by listening.
  • Effects on people. Whether golden sound calms, absorbs or delights is a question about listeners, not about signals. No amount of computation answers it; only experiments with people can (Chapter 40).
  • History beyond the sources. A model can produce a confident date or quotation it has never seen. The rule in this book is to state only well-established facts, hedge the uncertain ones and omit the rest.

What the collaboration changed. In the Preface the author estimates that he could have worked the system out himself in under 40 hours at any point in the past decade, but never did; with the machine it took a few minutes of his time and a few hours of computation. What did not change is where the music comes from: the scale, the idea of golden passing chords, the averaging intuition, the demand for clean sound and the final yes or no were all human. The machine made it possible to hear the idea, completely and quickly, and to know which statements about it are true.

Predicates and judgements. Call a predicate any yes-or-no property computable from the rendered signal or the score: "the peak is below −1 dBFS", "every passing chord in this section is golden", "at least 60% of the beat energy lies between 4 and 8 Hz". A machine can verify predicates exactly. A judgement such as "this section is calm" is not a function of the signal alone; it is a property of a listener hearing it. The bridge between the two is empirical: one can measure how well a predicate predicts listeners' judgements, with the blind protocols of Chapter 40, but one cannot derive it. The division of labor in this project follows that line: predicates for the machine, judgements for people, and experiments to connect them.

Exercises

Exercise 45.1 (checking). Verify the identity $8/\varphi^3 = 2 - 2\varphi^{-6}$ by hand, using $\varphi^3 = 2\varphi + 1$ and $\varphi^{-1} = \varphi - 1$.

Answer

$\varphi^{-3} = (\varphi - 1)^3 = \varphi^3 - 3\varphi^2 + 3\varphi - 1 = (2\varphi + 1) - 3(\varphi + 1) + 3\varphi - 1 = 2\varphi - 3$, so $8\varphi^{-3} = 16\varphi - 24$. Also $\varphi^{-6} = (2\varphi - 3)^2 = 4\varphi^2 - 12\varphi + 9 = 4(\varphi + 1) - 12\varphi + 9 = 13 - 8\varphi$, so $2 - 2\varphi^{-6} = 2 - 26 + 16\varphi = 16\varphi - 24$. Equal.

Exercise 45.2 (checking). Why are the rates 1 : 2/φ : 2 not a golden triad? What would the third rate have to be?

Answer

A golden triad is 1 : 2/φ : 4/φ² = 1 : 1.236 : 1.528. The third rate 2 is an octave above the first, not a golden fifth; for a golden triad it would have to be 4/φ² ≈ 1.528 times the first.

Exercise 45.3 (classifying). Label each statement theorem, measurement or hypothesis: (a) Cg = (C + Cm + Caug)/3 in sixth-steps; (b) on a golden spectrum the golden triad is the smoothest triad; (c) golden textures promote absorption.

Answer

(a) theorem (arithmetic: (12+9+12)/3 = 11, (21+21+24)/3 = 22); (b) measurement, under the Sethares roughness model and a particular spectrum; (c) hypothesis.
