Tone rows

Here is a melody that uses each of the twelve notes exactly once before any note returns: C, E, A, B♭, F, E♭, F♯, C♯, D, B, G, A♭. No note is repeated, so none can become a tonic by insistence. Listen to it as a melody first. Then hear its two halves as chords of six notes each. Each half sounds dense and ambiguous, and together they contain all twelve notes.

Such an ordering of the twelve pitch classes is a tone row (or series). In twelve-tone music one row, chosen for a piece, supplies all its melodies and harmonies: the notes are taken in the row's order, horizontally as a tune or vertically as chords, in any octave and rhythm. The row is less a theme than a "genetic code". You rarely hear it plainly, but its intervals shape everything.

Arnold Schoenberg (1874–1951) formulated his "method of composing with twelve tones which are related only with one another" in the early 1920s, after a decade of free atonality. Among the first pieces written wholly with it are the last of the Five Piano Pieces Op. 23 (a waltz) and the Suite for Piano Op. 25 (1921–23). The Austrian composer Josef Matthias Hauer developed a different twelve-tone system at about the same time.

The method solved a problem that free atonality had raised. If any note can follow any note, what keeps music from simply drifting? Schoenberg's answer was to fix an order. Two goals guided it:

  • Equality of the twelve. Because no note recurs before the other eleven have sounded, no pitch class gains the weight of a tonic. This is not a law of acoustics. It is a stylistic choice to avoid tonal implications.
  • Unity. Every melody and chord derives from one source, so the piece's intervals are related throughout, the way a tonal piece's harmonies are related through its key.

Some vocabulary: a row is written as a list of pitch classes, here P0 = ⟨0, 4, 9, 10, 5, 3, 6, 1, 2, 11, 7, 8⟩. Its hexachords are its first and last six notes. Its ordered intervals are the differences between consecutive notes mod 12, here 4, 5, 1, 7, 10, 3, 7, 1, 9, 8, 1. The intervals, not the notes, are what transposition preserves.

Golden mirror. A 36-ET row would order all 36 sixth-step pitch classes. One such row is already familiar: the golden chain, a golden sixth (25 sixth-steps) at a time, $0, 25, 14, 3, 28, \dots$, visits all 36 pitch classes before it returns, because 25 shares no factor with 36. It is a 36-tone row whose ordered intervals are all the same (Chapter 33). You can hear it in the section on all-interval rows.

Definition. A twelve-tone row is a bijection $P: \{0, 1, \dots, 11\} \to \mathbb{Z}_{12}$, written $\langle P(0), \dots, P(11)\rangle$ (order positions to pitch classes). Its interval succession is $\langle P(i+1) - P(i) \rangle_{i=0}^{10}$ mod 12.

Counting. There are $12! = 479{,}001{,}600$ rows, and $11! = 39{,}916{,}800$ of them start on C. Since transposition changes the first note and keeps the intervals, rows up to transposition correspond to interval successions, and there are $11!$ of them.

The 48 forms of a row

A row can be played forwards or backwards, right side up or upside down. Listen to the four basic forms: the prime P0, the inversion I0 (every interval turned the other way), the retrograde R0 (the prime backwards) and the retrograde inversion RI0 (the inversion backwards). They sound like four views of one object: the same intervals, mirrored in pitch or in time.

Each of the four forms can start on any of the twelve notes, which gives $4 \times 12 = 48$ forms. Composers lay them out in a row matrix, a 12 × 12 square. Read the top line left to right for P0, the left column top to bottom for I0, any line right to left for a retrograde, and any column bottom to top for a retrograde inversion. The subscripts name the first note of a P or I form. An R or RI form here carries the subscript of the line it reverses, so it names that form's last note; some authors label retrogrades by their first note instead.

The matrix is built in two steps. Write P0 across the top, then I0 (the negatives, $-x \bmod 12$) down the left side. Each line is then P0 transposed to begin on its left-hand note. The main diagonal is all C: the entry in line $i$, column $j$ is $P_0(j) - P_0(i)$, which is 0 when $i = j$.

Some rows have fewer than 48 distinct forms, because one operation maps the row onto another of its forms. A famous example is the row of Anton Webern's Symphony Op. 21 (1928), A–F♯–G–A♭–E–F | B–B♭–D–C♯–C–E♭. Its second half is the first half backwards, transposed by a tritone. So its retrograde equals its own transposition by a tritone, and it has only 24 distinct forms.

The group of row operations. Transposition $T_n$ and inversion $I_n$ act on pitch classes as in Chapter 20; retrograde acts on order positions, $R(P)(i) = P(11 - i)$. $R$ commutes with every $T_n$ and $I_n$, and $R^2 = 1$, so the row operations form the group $D_{12} \times \mathbb{Z}_2$ of order 48.

Theorem (row classes). The number of rows up to the 48 operations is $9{,}985{,}920$.

Proof (Burnside). Count rows fixed by each operation. No $T_n$ ($n \ne 0$) or $I_n$ fixes a row: a fixed row would need $P(i) + n = P(i)$, or $n - P(i) = P(i)$ for all twelve $i$, but $2x \equiv n$ has at most two solutions. An operation $RT_n$ fixes $P$ iff $P(11-i) = P(i) + n$; applying it twice forces $2n \equiv 0$, and $n = 0$ is impossible ($P(11-i) \ne P(i)$), so $n = 6$. Then the first six notes determine the row, and they must contain one note from each tritone pair: $6!\cdot 2^6 = 46{,}080$ rows. An operation $RI_n$ fixes $P$ iff $P(11-i) = n - P(i)$, which needs $x \mapsto n - x$ to have no fixed points, i.e. $n$ odd (6 values), each giving $6!\cdot 2^6 = 46{,}080$ rows. So

$$\#\text{classes} = \frac{12! + 46{,}080 + 6 \cdot 46{,}080}{48} = \frac{479{,}324{,}160}{48} = 9{,}985{,}920.\quad\square$$

The rows with fewer than 48 forms are exactly those fixed by $RT_6$ (like Webern's Op. 21) or by some $RI_n$ with $n$ odd. Each has 24 forms.

Check. Webern's row is $\langle 9,6,7,8,4,5,11,10,2,1,0,3\rangle$. Its retrograde is $\langle 3,0,1,2,10,11,5,4,8,7,6,9\rangle$, and adding 6 to each note of the prime gives the same list (computed).

Combinatoriality

Play the row against its own inversion, note against note: the prime in the upper voice, and below it the inversion that starts on B. Something special happens at the halfway point. In the first six beats, the two voices together have played all twelve notes, each exactly once, and the same is true of the last six. The two rows interlock so that each half of the passage is a complete chromatic aggregate. No note doubles another and no pitch class is over-weighted.

This property is hexachordal combinatoriality, and Milton Babbitt (1916–2011) named and systematized it in the 1940s and 50s, building on Schoenberg's practice. It depends only on the row's first hexachord $H$: we need a form of the row whose first hexachord is the complement of $H$, the six notes $H$ leaves out.

Here $H$ = {C, E♭, E, F, A, B♭} and the inversion I11 = B G D C♯ F♯ A♭ | F B♭ A C E E♭ begins with {B, G, D, C♯, F♯, A♭}, which is the complement. There are four kinds, by the operation that produces the complementary hexachord:

Prime or inversional combinatoriality is possible for 20 of the 50 hexachord classes (computed). Our row's hexachord is inversionally combinatorial but not prime combinatorial. Schoenberg built many of his later rows on inversionally combinatorial hexachords, pairing a prime with an inversion so that the two together fill out aggregates.

Golden mirror. In 36-ET the natural unit is the half-aggregate of 18 notes. The hexachord theorem carries over: the complement argument of Chapter 20 works for any even number of pitch classes, so an 18-note set and its complement always have the same interval vector. Which 18-note sets are combinatorial, and which of them are useful in golden music, is open. One example is known: the golden chain row is combinatorial at the tritone and under one inversion (Chapter 33).

Definition. A hexachord $H$ is P-combinatorial if $T_n(H) = \mathbb{Z}_{12}\setminus H$ for some $n$, and I-combinatorial if $I_n(H) = \mathbb{Z}_{12}\setminus H$ for some $n$. A row is combinatorial in the same sense if its first hexachord is.

Why it works. If $I_n(H) = H'$ (the complement), the first hexachord of $I_n(P)$ is $I_n(H) = H'$, so the first halves of $P$ and $I_n(P)$ together are $H \cup H' = \mathbb{Z}_{12}$. The second halves are $H'$ and $I_n(H') = H$, another aggregate.

A necessary condition. By the hexachord theorem (Chapter 20), $H$ and its complement always have the same interval vector. Combinatoriality asks for more: that they be in the same set class. So a Z-hexachord can never be P- or I-combinatorial, because its complement is its Z-partner, a different class.

Theorem (computed exhaustively over the 50 hexachord classes). 7 classes are P-combinatorial, 19 are I-combinatorial, 6 are both, and 20 are at least one. One class, (0,1,3,4,5,8), is P- but not I-combinatorial. The six that are both, listed above, are exactly the all-combinatorial hexachords. For our row, $I_{11}$ is the only inversion that works: $11 - \{0,3,4,5,9,10\} = \{11,8,7,6,2,1\}$, the complement.

All-interval rows

A row has eleven intervals between its twelve notes. Can they all be different, so that the row contains each interval from semitone to major seventh exactly once? Yes. Listen to one such row, voiced so that its leaps grow steadily: down a semitone, up a whole tone, down a minor third, up a major third, and so on, opening like a fan until the last leap is eleven semitones. Then hear the row of the first movement of Alban Berg's Lyric Suite for string quartet (1925–26), another all-interval row. Its first six notes are white keys and its last six are the five black keys and B.

At the opposite extreme is a row with only one interval. 12-ET has a famous one, the circle of fifths. 36-ET has its golden counterpart: the golden chain, each note a golden sixth above the last, through all 36 sixth-step pitch classes.

An all-interval row is a row whose eleven ordered intervals are 1, 2, …, 11 in some order. The "wedge" in the example has intervals 11, 2, 9, 4, 7, 6, 5, 8, 3, 10, 1, which is each value once. Berg's row has 11, 8, 9, 10, 7, 6, 5, 2, 3, 4, 1 (computed).

Every all-interval row has one property you can hear: its first and last notes are a tritone apart. The wedge runs from C to F♯, and Berg's row from F to B. The proof is one line, below.

Golden mirror. The wedge idea works in any even equal division. In 36-ET the row $0, 35, 1, 34, 2, 33, \dots$ has 35 different intervals, so 36-ET has all-interval 36-tone rows (checked by computer). The golden chain, with one interval used 35 times, is the other extreme. Between them lies the territory of golden serialism (Chapter 33).

Theorem. In an all-interval row, $P(11) - P(0) \equiv 6 \pmod{12}$.

Proof. The intervals telescope: $P(11) - P(0) = \sum_{i=0}^{10} \bigl(P(i+1) - P(i)\bigr) \equiv 1 + 2 + \dots + 11 = 66 \equiv 6. \ \square$

The wedge in general. For even $n$, the row $w_0 = 0$, $w_k = (-1)^k \lceil k/2 \rceil \bmod n$ (that is $0, -1, 1, -2, 2, \dots$) has successive intervals $(-1)^k k$ for $k = 1, \dots, n-1$. The odd $k$ give the odd residues $n - k$, the even $k$ give the even residues $k$, and all $n-1$ are different. So every even EDO has all-interval rows. For odd $n$ there are none, because the same telescoping sum gives $n(n-1)/2 \equiv 0$, which would make the first and last notes equal.

Counts (computed). Depth-first search over rows beginning with 0, rejecting any repeated pitch class or interval, finds 3,856 rows. No row at all is fixed by a nontrivial $T_n$ or $I_n$, no all-interval row is fixed by any $RI_n$ (computed), and 176 are fixed by $RT_6$. Burnside then gives $(12 \cdot 3856 + 12 \cdot 176)/48 = 1008$ classes under the 48 operations, which matches direct canonicalization.

Serialism in practice

Twelve-tone theory is about rows, but twelve-tone music is about everything else too: rhythm, register, color, phrasing. A row heard plainly, as in this chapter's examples, sounds like an exercise. The same row spread across registers, split between instruments, partly sounded as chords, can sound passionate (Berg), crystalline (Webern) or intricate (Babbitt). If you listen to the composers below, try to forget the rows and follow the gestures.

The Second Viennese School

  • Schoenberg treated the row as a source of themes and harmonies within traditional forms: suites, variations, concertos, the opera Moses und Aron (composed 1930–32, unfinished).
  • Alban Berg (1885–1935) often chose rows with tonal fragments. The row of his Violin Concerto (1935) begins with a chain of thirds that outlines G minor, D major, A minor and E major triads and ends in whole tones. Its music can sound almost Romantic.
  • Anton Webern (1883–1945) favored rows with internal symmetries and few notes per gesture. The row of his Concerto Op. 24 (1934), B–B♭–D | E♭–G–F♯ | A♭–E–F | C–C♯–A, is built from four forms of one three-note cell, set class (0,1,4) (computed). The Op. 21 row above is a palindrome up to transposition.

After 1945: total serialism

Composers of the next generation extended the series idea from pitch to other dimensions. Messiaen's piano study Mode de valeurs et d'intensités (1949) assigned fixed durations, dynamics and attacks to pitches. Pierre Boulez's Structures (book I, 1952, for two pianos) took a series from it and ordered durations, dynamics and attacks by series too. Karlheinz Stockhausen and others in Europe worked similarly. In the United States, Babbitt built a rigorous theory of serial structure for pitch and rhythm, from his Three Compositions for Piano (1947) on. Stravinsky, long the opposite pole of Schoenberg, adopted serial techniques in his late works from the 1950s.

What serialism does and does not claim. The row guarantees equal use of the twelve pitch classes and a web of interval relations. It does not guarantee that a listener perceives the row, and listening experiments have given mixed results on whether listeners can recognize row forms. Its value is as a compositional discipline, like counterpoint's rules, and is judged by the music.
Golden mirror. Golden serialism would order all 36 sixth-step pitch classes, or the 13 of golden13, and could combine rows by the 36-ET symmetries, including the golden transform $M_{11}$ that turns clusters into golden chords. These are experiments, not established practice (Chapter 33).

Invariance. A central tool of serial composition is invariance: segments that two row forms share. If a segment $S$ of $P$ satisfies $T_n(S) = S$ or $I_n(S) = S$ as a set, then the form $T_n P$ or $I_n P$ contains the same notes in the same positions as a set, and the two forms can be linked through it. Combinatoriality is the extreme case where the segment is a whole hexachord and its image is the complement. Webern's Op. 24 row shows the other extreme. Its cells are related by $RI$, $R$ and $I$ operations, so the row's own symmetries reproduce its parts.

Check (computed). The four trichords of the Op. 24 row, {11, 10, 2}, {3, 7, 6}, {8, 4, 5} and {0, 1, 9}, all have prime form (0, 1, 4).

Its retrograde is ⟨6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 5, 4, 3, 2, 1, 0⟩, which is each note of the row plus 6.

Exercise 21.3. Is the chromatic hexachord {0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5} P-combinatorial? I-combinatorial? Find the operations.

Answer

Both: $T_6$ maps it to {6, …, 11}, its complement; $I_{11}$ maps it to {11, 10, 9, 8, 7, 6}, also the complement.

Exercise 21.4. Check that Berg's row F E C A G D A♭ D♭ E♭ G♭ B♭ B begins and ends a tritone apart, and explain why it must.

Answer

F to B is 6 semitones. It must, because its intervals are 1 to 11 in some order, summing to 66 ≡ 6 (mod 12).
