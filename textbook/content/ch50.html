The songs of Chapters 47 to 49 are two to three minutes long, and each is built on one golden technique. This chapter goes the other way: one piece, nearly ten minutes long, that uses almost every technique of Parts VII and VIII and makes them work together. It is through-composed progressive metal with a modal-jazz centre, in 36-ET, and it changes meter from section to section: 4/4, 7/8, 5/4 and 13/16.

One idea carries it, and the title says what it is. "A Comma Higher" starts on C and ends on ^C, one sixth-step higher. A sixth-step is 33.33 cents, the 36-ET version of the golden comma (the exact comma is 30.17 cents, Chapter 23). The last movement climbs that sixth-step by the comma pump of Chapter 34, one note at a time, and then plays the opening metal song again on the new tonic. You can hear the whole piece as one long modulation by less than a third of a semitone.

The piece is printed here in its four movements. Each is cut from the same score, so the notes are those of the full song. Short excerpts isolate the techniques one at a time. Listen to each movement whole first, and ask the question of Chapter 47: does anything sound wrong?

One long piece, a comma higher

A short song can afford one surprise. A long one needs a plan, or the surprises cancel out. This piece has a simple plan. It is a song in a key, the C metal song of the first movement; then a long journey away from that key through golden modes and golden scales; then the same song again, on a tonic one sixth-step higher. The return is the point. When the riff comes back in the last movement, it is the riff you know, and it is not in the key you know.

The four movements also follow one emotional curve. The first is loud and dark: a drop-tuned guitar riff in 7/8 and a verse in F minor. The second is cool and swung: a jazz quartet of horn, electric piano, walking bass and ride cymbal. The third returns to the guitar, in the stranger colours of the golden decatonic, and then stops for a ballad. The fourth climbs. The quiet passages sit far below the loud ones, so the ear has somewhere to rest: the intro is 8.5 dB below the riff that follows it, and the closing coda is more than 15 dB below the last chorus.

A melody ties the movements together. The chorus tune of the first movement, here called the phi theme, comes back as the horn's solo in the second, as the ballad's melody in the third and as the coda's last phrase. Each time it is written in scale degrees, so each time the key it is played in recolours it. Listen for it changing colour while keeping its shape.

The table shows the piece's design: a 12-ET song in C, a golden middle, and the same 12-ET song on ^C. The two outer songs share no note at all. C Phrygian dominant uses only pitch classes on the 12-ET grid, and ^C Phrygian dominant only pitch classes one sixth-step above it. The ear accepts the return anyway, because the middle of the piece has walked it there one note at a time.

Two disjoint copies of one song. In sixth-steps, a 12-ET key uses only residues $\equiv 0 \pmod 3$. Raising every note by one sixth-step maps them to residues $\equiv 1 \pmod 3$, so the key and its comma-lift share nothing; raising by two gives the third layer, residues $\equiv 2$. 36-ET is three 12-ET grids interleaved (Chapter 24), and this piece starts on the first and ends on the second. Its golden chords are what connect them: a golden triad such as Cg = C vE ^G = $\{0, 11, 22\}$ has one note on each grid, so it belongs to none and can lead to any.

I. The door: from a golden pedal into 7/8

The piece opens in a haze. Over a low C held for twelve slow bars, a pad moves between three golden chords and a bell sings the first notes of the phi theme. Nothing here is Western yet. Then two bars do the decisive thing: the golden triad on C bends into C major, and a crash and a 7/8 riff land on that C. The haze becomes a door, and the door opens onto metal.

The riff is in C Phrygian dominant, the mode of flamenco and of much metal: C, D♭, E, F, G, A♭, B♭. It is counted in 7/8 as 2 + 2 + 3, so every bar ends with a longer beat that throws you into the next. After it come a verse in F minor, a pre-chorus in 5/4 (3 + 2) and a chorus in A♭ major. The second chorus ends with two golden surprises: a golden deceptive cadence in the middle, and a golden chord that leads back into the riff.

The guitar is distorted, as metal guitar must be, and that raised a real problem. Ordinary distortion turns golden chords into mud. The next excerpt plays the same power chords three ways: a 12-ET power chord through ordinary distortion; golden power chords through the same distortion; and the golden ones through the piece's own guitar, which distorts each note separately.

Now the whole movement.

The golden pedal (Chapter 37). Over a held C only three golden triads contain C: Cg, ^A♭g and vFg. They are the intro's whole vocabulary, so the pedal never clashes with what moves above it. The coda of the fourth movement does the same over ^C.

The door (Chapter 35). Every major triad has a nearest golden triad, its golden projection: C major projects to Cg. Played the other way, the projection becomes an entrance. From Cg to C two voices move by one sixth-step each and the root stays.

The chord the door opens onto is not just any C major. In F harmonic minor, C major is the dominant, V, and F harmonic minor is the Western key nearest to C golden7 (Chapter 35). So the golden intro resolves the way a dominant resolves: the verse arrives in F minor.

The second chorus (Chapter 29). In A♭ major the chorus heads for E♭, the dominant, and is deflected: E♭ – vEg – Eg – Fm, the golden deceptive cadence, its two golden chords on the half-beats between V and vi. The last half-bar turns A♭ into Cg, a golden mediant that slides back into the C riff.

Why ordinary distortion fails. A distortion that clips the mixed signal creates new tones at the sums and differences of the frequencies it receives. A 12-ET fifth is nearly the ratio 3 : 2, so its difference tone lands almost exactly an octave below the root and reinforces it. That is why power chords sound massive. A golden fifth is not near any simple ratio, so its difference tones fall between the notes and sound like rumble. The piece's guitar therefore distorts each string separately, before the notes are mixed. Each note gets the grit of distortion, and no two notes are clipped together.

The difference tones. Let the root have frequency $f$ and the fifth $rf$. The strongest new tone from clipping the sum is at $(r - 1)f$. For the 12-ET fifth, $r = 2^{7/12} \approx 1.4983$ and $(r-1)f \approx 0.4983f$, 5.9 cents below the octave under the root: almost a doubling. The golden triad's fifth is 22 sixth-steps, $r = 2^{22/36} \approx 1.5274$, so $(r-1)f \approx 0.5274f$, 92 cents above that octave: nearly a semitone off, and beating against the root's own partials. Distorting each note on its own creates only the harmonics of that note, $kf$ and $krf$, which keep the chord's shape.

Exercise 1. Write Cg and C major in sixth-steps and find how far each voice moves through the door.

Answer

Cg = $\{0, 11, 22\}$ and C = $\{0, 12, 21\}$. The root stays, vE rises to E ($11 \to 12$) and ^G falls to G ($22 \to 21$): two voices, one sixth-step each, in contrary motion.

Exercise 2. At 138 beats per minute, counting quarter notes, how long is one bar of 7/8? How long are its three beats, 2 + 2 + 3?

Answer

A quarter note lasts $60/138 \approx 0.435$ s, so an eighth is about 0.217 s and a bar of seven eighths about 1.52 s. The beats last 0.43 s, 0.43 s and 0.65 s: the long last beat is the "limp" that drives the riff.

II. Blue gold: modal jazz through the golden modes

The guitar stops and a jazz quartet takes over: horn, electric piano, walking bass and a swung ride cymbal at a fast 168 beats per minute. Its head is a modal tune in the manner of Miles Davis's "So What", which sits on one Dorian chord for eight bars and then lifts the whole tune a semitone. Here the tune starts in C Dorian, the most familiar Western jazz mode. On its second pass it moves into C golden7, the golden scale that plays Dorian's role. On its third it lifts the whole golden mode by one sixth-step. On its fourth it comes home to C Dorian.

Then horn and guitar trade four-bar phrases through all seven modes of golden7, one after another, from the brightest to the darkest. The phrase stays the same, written in scale degrees, so you hear only the mode change. The excerpt below takes three of the seven: the brightest, golden7 itself (the golden Dorian), and the darkest.

The darkest mode has no golden chord on its tonic, which makes it unstable. The music leaves it through the golden jazz cadence: the familiar ii – V – I with golden chords slipped onto the weak halves of each bar.

Three kinds of modal change happen in the head, and the book has a chapter for each.

  • C Dorian → C golden7 keeps the tonic and changes the scale. golden7 on C is the 3|3 mode, the golden counterpart of Dorian in its place among the modes (Chapter 36). The chords change from Cm7 and F7 to Cg7 and vFg7.
  • C golden7 → ^C golden7 keeps the scale and moves every note at once by one sixth-step: a lift (Chapter 34). It is the golden counterpart of "So What"'s semitone lift, three times smaller.
  • ^C golden7 → C Dorian drops back by a sixth-step and back into 12-ET at the same time.

The seven modes. Each mode is the same seven-note scale heard from another tonic. Moved to C and played brightest first, each lowers one note of the one before by 5 sixth-steps (166.67 cents), the golden counterpart of the diatonic modes, where each step lowers one note by a semitone.

The two darkest modes have no golden triad on their tonic, so their tonic chords are strange and open. That makes them good places to leave from. Dg7 belongs to the darkest mode, and it is also the first golden chord of the golden jazz cadence (Chapter 29). So the trade's last chord pivots straight into the cadence home to Cmaj7.

Golden giant steps (Chapter 37) close the movement: C – Cg – A♭ – A♭g – E – Eg – C, three major triads a major third apart with a golden chord after each. The horn holds the note that each pair shares.

Why one phrase can recolour itself. The horn's phrase is written as scale degrees 0 to 7, not as notes. In SongMap, a section's key, for example key="C golden7_51", maps degrees to notes, so the same phrase is re-spelled in each mode. Inside a golden7 mode no two notes are closer than a semitone (3 sixth-steps), so a degree line cannot rub against the mode's own chords. The rub checker confirms that no two notes a sixth-step or two apart overlap anywhere in the movement.

Exercise 3. From the 3|3 mode to the 1|5 mode, which notes drop, and by how many sixth-steps?

Answer

3|3 → 2|4 lowers B to ^A ($33 \to 28$), and 2|4 → 1|5 lowers ^G to vF♯ ($22 \to 17$). Each drop is 5 sixth-steps, 166.67 cents in 36-ET (168.37 cents in the exact golden system).

III. Decatonic: odd meters and three keys at once

The guitar comes back with a new riff in 7/8. It sounds related to the first movement's riff and also strange, because it uses a ten-note golden scale, the golden decatonic, and it alternates between the two five-note scales hidden inside it: one bar in one pentatonic, one bar in the other. Under it the chords walk the golden circle, a golden triad on each step of the golden chain.

Midway comes golden planing. The guitar plays a plain C major tune and then the same tune with a golden triad under every note, so the chord slides in parallel with the line. You hear one melody. On paper there are three keys at once.

Then a bridge in 13/16, counted 3 + 3 + 3 + 2 + 2, so the bar stumbles into its own end. Then a guitar solo round the whole golden circle and back. Then everything stops for a ballad at half the tempo: the phi theme on keys and bell, in C golden7, ending on the softest cadence in the piece.

The golden decatonic (Chapter 36). golden7 has a limp: semitone, wide step, semitone, wide step. Split each wide step of 8 sixth-steps into 3 + 5 and you get the "35" decatonic, C C♯ D vE vF vF♯ ^G ^A♭ ^A B, whose steps are only semitones and steps of 5 sixth-steps. The scale splits into two interleaved golden pentatonics, and the riff uses them in turn:

  • bar 1: C D vF ^G ^A;
  • bar 2: C♯ vE vF♯ ^A♭ B.

In SongMap the scale is the mode golden10_36: three chain notes below the tonic and six above, in the naming of Chapter 36.

Golden planing (Chapter 37). A golden triad on every note of a C major line puts the roots in C major, the thirds 11 sixth-steps higher and the fifths 22 sixth-steps higher. Those thirds spell vE major, the fifths ^G major. The planed line is exactly three-key polytonality.

The bridge (Chapter 37) resolves into Cg twice from opposite sides: from vEg, a golden leading-tone resolution, and from C♯g, the golden Neapolitan, which falls onto the tonic by a semitone in every voice. The ballad ends the same way from below, Bg → Cg, every voice rising a semitone.

The golden circle walk under the riff and the solo, Cg – ^A♭g – vFg – C♯g – ^Ag – vF♯g – Dg, moves each root up a golden sixth. It is the first half of the comma pump of the next movement. The solo walks it out and back, and the last movement takes it all the way.

Three keys with no common note. In sixth-steps C major is $\{0, 6, 12, 15, 21, 27, 33\}$, all $\equiv 0 \pmod 3$. Adding 11 and 22 gives vE major and ^G major, whose notes are $\equiv 2$ and $\equiv 1 \pmod 3$. The three sets are disjoint, so no note is shared between the line, its thirds and its fifths, yet each triad is the same golden shape $\{r, r + 11, r + 22\}$.

Exercise 4. How long is a bar of 13/16 at 138 quarter notes per minute, and how does it compare with the 7/8 bar?

Answer

A sixteenth lasts $60/138/4 \approx 0.109$ s, so 13 of them take about 1.41 s. The 7/8 bar is 14 sixteenths, about 1.52 s. The bridge bar is one sixteenth shorter than the riff bar, so it arrives early, which is the stumble you hear.

IV. Thirteen steps: the comma pump

The last movement is a climb. Thirteen golden chords follow one another, each keeping two notes of the one before and raising the third by a semitone. Over them the lead sings the rising voice. Nothing jumps, yet after thirteen steps the music is on a new tonic, ^C, a sixth-step above where the piece began. Then the drums come back and the metal song of the first movement starts again: riff, verse, pre-chorus, chorus, all a comma higher.

Listen to the return with the first movement in your ear. The riff is the same riff, and the chorus is the same chorus, and yet the key is not the same: every note is a third of a semitone sharp of where you heard it seven minutes ago. Whether you notice depends on your memory for absolute pitch. Most listeners hear only that the music has come home, and perhaps that home is a little brighter. The last chorus repeats the golden deceptive cadence, now on the new grid, and the coda returns to the haze of the opening over a held ^C, ending with a golden Picardy third: ^Cm – ^Cm(^3) – ^Cg – ^C.

The comma pump (Chapter 34) walks the tonic along the golden chain: each new root is a golden sixth above the last. Chapter 34 does it with golden13 keys, raising one scale note per step. The piece does it with golden triads on the same thirteen tonics. Between neighbouring triads two notes are shared and the third rises a semitone:

The path goes nearly once round the circle in leaps of a golden sixth and closes one sixth-step past its start. The harmony arrives at ^C without any jump at all. Every move is a semitone in one voice, and the lift accumulates out of sight.

The summit. The return is the first movement transposed by one sixth-step: ^C Phrygian dominant, ^F harmonic minor, ^A♭ major. The last chorus has the golden deceptive cadence on the new grid, ^E♭ – Eg – ^Eg – ^Fm, and it is the loudest passage of the piece, 2.8 dB above any other section. The coda is the golden pedal of the intro on ^C, with the golden Picardy of Chapter 29: minor, minor with its third raised a sixth-step, golden, major.

Why thirteen steps lift by one sixth-step. A golden sixth is 25 sixth-steps in 36-ET, and thirteen of them make $13 \times 25 = 325 = 9 \times 36 + 1$: nine octaves and one sixth-step. In the exact system thirteen golden sixths overshoot nine octaves by the golden comma, $1200 \log_2(\varphi^{13}/2^9) \approx 30.17$ cents (Chapter 23); 36-ET rounds it to 33.33 cents.

Exercise 5. Check that ^A♭g keeps two notes of Cg and raises the third by a semitone.

Answer

Cg = $\{0, 11, 22\}$. ^A♭ is 25, so ^A♭g = $\{25, 36, 47\} \equiv \{25, 0, 11\}$ = ^A♭, C, vE. C and vE are kept and ^G (22) rises to ^A♭ (25), 3 sixth-steps, a semitone.

Exercise 6. The summit's riff is the first riff a sixth-step higher. Show that the two riffs share no pitch class.

Answer

C Phrygian dominant in sixth-steps is $\{0, 3, 12, 15, 21, 24, 30\}$, all $\equiv 0 \pmod 3$. Adding 1 gives residues $\equiv 1 \pmod 3$, so no pitch class is shared.

How it was made, and how it was checked

A piece this long tests the music engine as much as the theory. Three things had to be added to SongMap to write it, and each can be heard.

  • Meters per section. Each section sets its own meter and grouping: 7/8 as 2 + 2 + 3, 5/4 as 3 + 2, 13/16 as 3 + 3 + 3 + 2 + 2. The drums and the riffs follow the grouping, so the odd meters groove instead of merely counting.
  • Golden modes as keys. A section can be in "C golden7_51" or "C golden10_36", so one melody written in scale degrees recolours itself from section to section.
  • A guitar that distorts note by note, so golden chords keep their shape under heavy distortion (the excerpt in the first section).

What was checked. The score passes the engine's structural check: 41 sections, 320 bars, 9 minutes 48 seconds including a six-second reverb tail. The automated ear (Chapter 46) reports no clashes, crowding or repetition problems. The rub checker finds no two notes a sixth-step or two apart overlapping anywhere. Every golden chord is written out by hand; none is left for the passing engine to choose.

Loudness. The whole piece measures −14.5 LUFS integrated, the usual streaming level, with a −1.0 dB true peak and no clipped samples. It was mastered without glue compression, so the quiet passages stay quiet. The loudness of the sections tells the shape of the piece:
