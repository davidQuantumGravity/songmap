Additive synthesis

Every tone in this book's examples was built from nothing but sine waves. Listen to one being assembled. A pure sine at 220 Hz sounds first: smooth, colorless, a little hollow. Then, every 1.2 seconds, another sine joins at the next whole multiple of the frequency, 440, 660, 880 Hz and so on, each softer than the last. The pitch never changes, but the tone grows brighter and buzzier until, with eight partials, it begins to resemble a reedy organ stop. Finally the same eight partials return with their upper members much softer: the same pitch, a rounder color.

Building tones from sines. Fourier's idea (Chapter 2) says that a steady periodic tone is a sum of sines at whole multiples of its fundamental. Additive synthesis runs the idea backwards: choose the frequencies and amplitudes of the partials, add the sines, and you have the tone. It is the most transparent way to make sound: every component is known exactly, and nothing is present that was not put there.

The clean instrument that renders this book's audio is an additive synthesizer with a handful of controls:

A rolloff of 1 means each doubling of the partial number halves the amplitude, a slope of about −6 dB per octave of the spectrum; a rolloff of 2 gives −12 dB per octave. The sawtooth wave has rolloff exactly 1, which is why the first part of the example drifts toward a buzz.

The clean tone. For a note of fundamental $f_0$ and velocity $v$, with partial ratios $r_1 < r_2 < \dots$ (harmonic: $r_k = k$), the instrument computes

$$x(t) = \frac{E(t)\,G(v)}{\sum_{k=1}^{K} k^{-\rho}}\ \sum_{k=1}^{K} a_k \sin\!\big(2\pi f_0 r_k t + \phi_k\big)\, e^{-6.9078\, t / T_k},$$

where

  • $a_k = k^{-\rho}$ (times the even weight for even harmonic $k$), with $\rho = \max(\text{rolloff} - \text{vel\_bright}\cdot(v - 0.7),\ 0)$;
  • $\phi_k = \pi k^2 / K$ are fixed phases (§44.2);
  • $T_k = \text{decay}/(1 + \text{damp}\,(k-1))$, and the decay factor is omitted when decay is 0 (sustained tones); since $\ln 1000 = 6.9078$, partial $k$ is 60 dB down after $T_k$ seconds;
  • $E(t)$ is the raised-cosine envelope and $G(v)$ a velocity gain;
  • partials at or above $0.45\times 48\,000 = 21\,600$ Hz are skipped, to stay below the Nyquist frequency (24 kHz) and avoid aliasing.

No clipping by construction (theorem). Each term is at most $a_k$ in absolute value, so by the triangle inequality the inner sum is at most $\sum a_k \le \sum k^{-\rho}$, and $|x(t)| \le E(t)\,G(v)$. The normalization makes the loudest possible alignment of the partials fit exactly.

The sawtooth. The Fourier series of a sawtooth of period $1/f_0$ is $\frac{2}{\pi}\sum_{k\ge1} \frac{(-1)^{k+1}}{k}\sin(2\pi k f_0 t)$: amplitudes $1/k$, i.e. rolloff 1.

Clean, stable tones

Two things are easy to get wrong when building a tone from scratch, and both matter for hearing harmony precisely. First, the edges. A tone that switches on and off instantly clicks; a short straight ramp still ticks; a smooth raised-cosine fade is silent. Listen to the same short note with each.

Second, stability. Many synthesizer sounds are made "warm" by letting each voice drift slightly in pitch and wobble with vibrato. That is pleasant in a pop mix, but it destroys exactly what this book listens to. The example plays an exact golden seventh twice: once with every partial perfectly steady, so its beats at about 6, 7.6, 9.4 and 12.3 per second interlock in a clear pattern; and once with a few cents of slow drift and a gentle vibrato on each voice. The beat pattern dissolves into a generic shimmer.

What "clean" means. The clean instrument has, by design, no vibrato, no pitch drift, no detuned copies, no chorus, no noise, no filter and no saturation. Every partial is an exact sine at a fixed frequency. As a result, any beating in a chord comes only from the tuning of its notes, and any roughness only from the interaction of their partials. When the book says "listen to the beats of the golden seventh", the beats are the chord's, not the synthesizer's.

Edges: raised-cosine envelopes. Each note fades in and out along half a cosine curve, $\tfrac12 - \tfrac12\cos(\pi t/t_a)$, instead of a straight line. The curve starts and ends with zero slope, so the waveform never has a corner, and corners are what the ear hears as clicks.

Fixed phases. Each partial starts at a fixed phase, $\pi k^2/K$, a formula due to Manfred Schroeder (1970). It has two purposes: it avoids randomness (the same file always renders to the same samples), and it avoids starting every partial at its peak at once. Schroeder's formula was designed to give a low crest factor (peak over RMS level) for a sum of equal-amplitude partials. For the falling spectra of real presets that advantage is absent, as this measurement shows (peak over RMS of one period, sampled finely; the random column is the mean of 20 draws of uniform random phases, numpy seeds 0–19, and single draws vary by about ±2 dB):

Why drift destroys beats. A beat is a slow, regular change in the phase relation of two nearly equal partials. If the partials themselves wander in frequency by as much as the beat rate, the relation is no longer regular. In the drifting example, the 8th partial of the 110 Hz voice (880 Hz) swings by about ±5 Hz under a ±10-cent vibrato, comparable to the beat rates of 6–12 Hz. Measured on the envelope of the rendered signal (its 10 ms RMS, 8 s with a Hann window, amplitude spectrum), the components at the four golden beat rates 6.13, 7.58, 9.37 and 12.26 Hz stand about 350, 310, 51 and 750 times above the median of the 3–30 Hz band for the stable chord, and only about 3.7, 3.0, 1.2 and 7.2 times for the drifting one. (The largest envelope component in that band, in both versions, is at 26 Hz: the difference of the two lowest fundamentals, 110 and 135.97 Hz, heard as roughness rather than as a countable beat.)

Crest factor. $\mathrm{CF} = 20\log_{10}\big(\max|x| / \sqrt{\overline{x^2}}\big)$. A single sine has $\mathrm{CF} = 20\log_{10}\sqrt2 = 3.01$ dB. For $K$ equal partials all starting at their peak (cosine phase) the peak is $K$ times one amplitude while the RMS is $\sqrt{K/2}$ times it, so $\mathrm{CF} = 10\log_{10}(2K)$: 12.04 dB for $K = 8$, 15.05 dB for $K = 16$, as measured.

Smoothness and clicks (theorem). The spectrum of an envelope edge decays with frequency according to how smooth the edge is. If the envelope jumps (an abrupt gate), its spectrum falls as $1/f$, about −6 dB per octave, and spreads energy far above the tone: a click. If it is continuous with a corner (a linear ramp), the spectrum falls as $1/f^2$, −12 dB per octave. If it and its slope are continuous (the raised cosine, whose derivative $\tfrac{\pi}{2t_a}\sin(\pi t/t_a)$ is zero at both ends), the spectrum falls as $1/f^3$, −18 dB per octave, and the edge is inaudible at ordinary ramp times.

Drift in numbers. A pitch change of $c$ cents multiplies every frequency by $2^{c/1200}$. For partial $k$ of fundamental $f_0$ the frequency moves by $k f_0 (2^{c/1200} - 1)$: for $k = 8$, $f_0 = 110$ Hz and $c = 10$, that is 5.10 Hz, against 0.64 Hz for the fundamental. Upper partials carry the beats, and upper partials drift the most.

Designing golden timbres

A harmonic tone puts its partials at 1, 2, 3, 4 times the fundamental. Nothing forces that choice on a synthesizer. The next example builds a tone whose partials are golden: the fundamental, then φ times it, then twice it, then φ², and so on. It does not sound like an ordinary instrument: more like a bell or a struck glass, with a slightly unearthly ring.

No physical instrument makes such a tone. Strings, winds and voices have (nearly) harmonic spectra; a golden spectrum exists only digitally. It is a laboratory tone, and the next example is a laboratory experiment. A just major triad and an exact golden triad, first on a harmonic tone, then on the golden tone. On the harmonic tone, the major triad is the calm one and the golden triad shimmers uneasily. On the golden tone the ranking reverses: the golden triad is now the calmer of the two, and the major triad the rougher.

Why it works: matching timbre to tuning. William Sethares (Tuning, Timbre, Spectrum, Scale, 1998) showed how to choose a timbre so that a given tuning's intervals become smooth: put the partials where the intervals' other partials will land. Golden chords are built from golden intervals, so a golden spectrum lines the partials up. The count below is exact (computed for ten partials per tone, exact ratios):

Coincident partials fuse; near misses beat and make roughness. On the golden spectrum the golden triad has twelve exact coincidences and no near misses at all, while the major triad has none and twelve near misses. The Sethares roughness model gives the same verdict in numbers, on C4 (Chapter 26): with harmonic overtones major 1.32 and golden 1.50; with golden overtones golden 2.08 against major 3.17 and minor 3.26. That is the timbre flip: the timbre decides which chord the model ranks smoothest. Compare the numbers only within one timbre: 2.08 on the golden spectrum is not "rougher" than 1.50 on the harmonic one in any meaningful sense, because the two spectra have different partials to interact.

A laboratory, not an instrument. On the digital golden spectrum the exact golden triad is a strict local minimum of roughness: every triad within 5–30 c of it is rougher, by 0.11–0.46. On harmonic tones, the spectra of all real instruments, it is not: roughness and harmonic entropy fall steadily from the golden triad toward just major, and C major remains the smoothest chord and the deepest rest (Chapter 26). In the author's model the golden triad is a delicate local home even there, a place one can dwell; that is a musical claim to test by listening, not something these models show. The golden spectrum is a way to immerse oneself in golden harmony on its own terms, and then to bring what was learned back to harmonic instruments.

Designing your own. A golden timbre has the same other controls as any clean tone. A golden bell (clean/glass) uses ten golden partials, rolloff 1.3 and a 6 s decay with the upper partials fading faster; a golden pad (clean/golden_pad) uses rolloff 1.4 and a slow 2.5 s attack. Fewer partials make a purer, more flute-like golden tone; a lower rolloff a brighter, more metallic one. The essential choice is the set of ratios.

Coincidence theorem (exact tuning). Let the chord be $1 : 2/\varphi : 4/\varphi^2$ on a golden spectrum $\{2^m\varphi^n\}$. A partial of the golden third is $\tfrac{2}{\varphi}\cdot 2^m\varphi^n = 2^{m+1}\varphi^{n-1}$, which is a partial of the root whenever $n \ge 1$. A partial of the golden fifth is $\tfrac{4}{\varphi^2}\cdot 2^m\varphi^n = 2^{m+2}\varphi^{n-2}$, a root partial whenever $n \ge 2$. So most partials of the upper notes land exactly on partials of the root (with a finite set of partials, those near the top of the list may have no partner).

The major third never coincides (theorem). A coincidence between the root and a just third would need $2^a\varphi^b = 5/4$ for integers $a, b$. If $b \ne 0$, $\varphi^b = F_b\varphi + F_{b-1}$ (with Fibonacci numbers extended to negative indices) is irrational, so $2^a\varphi^b$ is irrational; if $b = 0$, $2^a = 5/4$ is impossible. The same argument excludes 3/2 and 6/5. On a golden spectrum, rational intervals other than octaves never fuse.

In 36-ET. The 36-ET golden third is 0.24 c flat of $2/\varphi$, so its exact coincidences become near coincidences: at a partial frequency of 1 kHz the two partials differ by $1000\,(2^{0.24/1200} - 1) = 0.14$ Hz, a beat every seven seconds, heard as a slow breathing rather than roughness.

Mixing for clarity

The last stage of making a track, mixing and mastering, usually adds processing: compression to even out loudness, limiting and saturation to make it louder and denser. This book's audio uses none of it, and the example shows why. First, two pure tones beating six times a second, clean and then through a fast compressor matched to the same loudness: the compressor follows the beat and pushes its peaks down, so the beat becomes shallower. Then a pure major third, clean and then through a saturation curve: new pitches appear that were never played, 330 Hz (a fourth below the lower note) and 660 Hz (a fifth above it).

Gain staging. In a SongMap song, each part has a gain in decibels (gain_db) and an optional send to a hall reverb (send: {hall: 0.3}). For dry demonstrations the examples omit the reverb; for musical examples a send of 0.2–0.4 adds space without blurring the harmony. Balance is set by these gains, not by processing.

What processing does to designed sound (measurements). The example's numbers:

  • Compression. The 6 Hz beat of 220 + 226 Hz has a depth of about 15.8 dB (95th against 10th percentile of its 10 ms RMS envelope). A fast compressor (threshold −30 dB, 8:1, attack 2 ms, release 40 ms) reduces it to about 9.9 dB. A slow glue setting like the default master's barely changes it (about 15.2 dB with 3:1, 30 ms, 200 ms).
  • Saturation. The tanh curve used by the project's original prototype master (drive 1.6) turns 440 + 550 Hz into a signal with components at 330 Hz and 660 Hz about 20 dB below the notes (19.6 dB in a spectrum of the rendered example), plus others above the notes, such as $2\cdot 440 + 550 = 1430$ Hz at the same level. These are real combination tones created by the processor, not by the ear, and they would contaminate exactly the experiments of Chapter 26.

So the clean master does one thing: it scales the whole mix so that its highest peak sits at −1 dBFS, leaving every partial and every beat as the instruments made them.

Loudness for comparisons. Because the clean master normalizes peaks, not loudness, two examples can differ in perceived loudness. When loudness matters, as in the blind comparisons of Chapter 41, the excerpts are matched by measured loudness: SongMap's QA report computes integrated loudness by the ITU-R BS.1770 method.

The clean master. $y = x \cdot 10^{-1/20} / \max_{t,c}|x_c(t)|$, a single gain for the whole file.

BS.1770 integrated loudness (as implemented in SongMap's QA, at 48 kHz):

  1. K-weighting. Each channel passes through two second-order filters: a high-frequency shelf boost of about 4 dB and a high-pass that discounts the lowest frequencies, modeling the ear's sensitivity.
  2. Blocks. Mean squares $z_{c,j}$ of the weighted channels over 400 ms blocks, overlapping by 75% (a new block every 100 ms). Block loudness $\ell_j = -0.691 + 10\log_{10}\sum_c G_c z_{c,j}$, with $G_c = 1$ for left and right.
  3. Gating. Discard blocks below −70 LUFS (absolute gate); compute the mean of the rest; discard blocks more than 10 LU below that mean (relative gate).
  4. Integrated loudness $= -0.691 + 10\log_{10}$ of the mean of $\sum_c G_c z_{c,j}$ over the surviving blocks, in LUFS.

Matching two excerpts to loudness $L^\ast$ multiplies each by $10^{(L^\ast - L)/20}$.

Where the new tones come from (theorem). For small inputs $\tanh u = u - u^3/3 + \dots$. With $u = a\cos\omega_1 t + b\cos\omega_2 t$, the cube contains the terms $\tfrac34 a^2 b\cos\big((2\omega_1 - \omega_2)t\big)$ and $\tfrac34 a b^2\cos\big((2\omega_2 - \omega_1)t\big)$. Any odd-order nonlinearity therefore creates the third-order intermodulation tones $2f_1 - f_2$ and $2f_2 - f_1$: for 440 and 550 Hz, exactly 330 and 660 Hz.

Exercises

Exercise 44.1 (calculation). A clean tone has 8 harmonic partials with rolloff 2. What fraction of its normalization sum does the fundamental contribute?

Answer

$\sum_{k=1}^{8} k^{-2} = 1.5274$, so the fundamental's share is $1/1.5274 = 0.655$, about two thirds.

Exercise 44.2 (listening). In the envelopes example, which edge is louder as a click, the start or the end of the abrupt note? Why might they differ?

Answer

They can differ because the click's size depends on the waveform's value at the moment of the jump: a jump from or to a value near zero clicks less than a jump near a peak. A raised-cosine edge removes the jump whatever the phase.

Exercise 44.3 (proof). Show that on a golden spectrum the golden seventh $8/\varphi^3$ above a root has partials $2^{m+3}\varphi^{n-3}$, and say which of them coincide with root partials.

Answer

$\tfrac{8}{\varphi^3}\cdot 2^m\varphi^n = 2^{m+3}\varphi^{n-3}$. It is a root partial (of the form $2^{m'}\varphi^{n'}$ with $n' \ge 0$) exactly when $n \ge 3$.

Exercise 44.4 (calculation). Two tones of 300 and 400 Hz pass through a saturator. At which frequencies do the third-order intermodulation tones appear, and what musical relation do they have to the input?

Answer

$2\cdot 300 - 400 = 200$ Hz and $2\cdot 400 - 300 = 500$ Hz. With the input a fourth (4:3), the new tones make the frequencies 200, 300, 400, 500 Hz: harmonics 2–5 of 100 Hz.

Exercise 44.5 (design). You want a golden pad on which the golden seventh chord is as smooth as possible. Which setting matters most: the attack, the rolloff, or the spectrum? Why?

Answer

The spectrum: golden partials make the chord's partials coincide exactly (§44.3). Rolloff changes how much the upper partials, and their few near misses, contribute; attack changes only the onset.
