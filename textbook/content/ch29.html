Chapters 27 and 28 found the golden chords that live between ordinary chords. This chapter puts them to work. Each section takes a familiar Western move (the authentic, plagal and deceptive cadences of Chapter 10, the chromatic mediants, stepwise progressions and modal mixture of Chapter 11) and shows the golden version that the two systems guarantee. The Western chord at each end stays home; the golden chords are passing colors, short and on weak beats. Every technique is first defined dry, on an organ, and then heard in a short phrase.

The principle throughout is the one of Chapter 27: a golden passing chord is a weighted average of its neighbours that happens to land on the golden chain. Where System 1 (~) provides one, it is unique and its root is fixed by the root rules; where roots move by step, System 2 (~g) provides two.

The golden authentic cadence

The authentic cadence V → I is the strongest close in tonal music: the leading tone rises to the tonic and the bass falls a fifth. Its golden version keeps all of that and inserts, over the held dominant bass, the golden triad a sixth-step below the tonic. In its three-chord form you hear V, a brief shimmer that already sounds like the tonic seen through frosted glass, and then I. In its four-chord form the bronze partner comes first.

Combined with the plagal glow and the escalator it gives a full golden cadence, I – IV – V – I with four golden passing chords and every voice moving by sixth-steps:

Definition. The golden authentic cadence is V – vIg/V – I (three-chord form) or V – v♭IIIT13/V – vIg/V – I (four-chord form). In C: G – vCg/G – C, or G – vE♭T13/G – vCg/G – C. In SongMap: G:1 ~:1 C:2 or G:1 ~:0.5 ~:0.5 C:2.

The golden chord vCg = vC ^E♭ G has the dominant's root G as its fifth, so the bass belongs to it: it is a golden triad in second inversion over the dominant, the golden counterpart of the Western cadential six-four. Its function is that of a shadow: a golden chord a sixth-step below a diatonic root, leading up into it (Chapter 27). Everything about it is forced by the theory: G and C are major triads a fourth apart, so by the quality theorem there is exactly one strict golden passing chord, and by the root rule it stands on the upper root C, shifted down (v), nearer C (position 2 of 3).

The classical constraints carry over unchanged: passing chords on weak beats, common tones held (G in the tenor), no parallel fifths or octaves at the arrival, a bass pedal under the passing chords, and the leading tone rising through the golden layers rather than skipping them. In V7 → I on the natural path the seventh F rises to G through F♯ in an inner voice (Chapter 28).

The golden cadence transposes like any cadence. In E♭ it is B♭ – vF♯T13/B♭ – vE♭g/B♭ – E♭; in A it is E – vCT13/E – vAg/E – A. The golden chord is always vIg over the dominant.

The cadence in sixth-steps, upper voices only (T, A, S): $X = (165, 177, 186)$ (G3 B3 D4), $d = (0, +1, +2)$, so $P_2 = X + 2d = (165, 179, 190)$, pitch classes {21, 35, 10} = {G, vC, ^E♭} = vCg. As an average, $P_2 = (X + 2Y)/3$ with $Y = (165, 180, 192)$ (G3 C4 E4): $(177 + 360)/3 = 179$ and $(186 + 384)/3 = 190$. The single placeholder ~ picks the more golden of the two System 1 chords (an exact golden triad ranks above an equal-third chord), which is why G:1 ~:1 C:2 yields vCg/G rather than vE♭T13/G.

Golden plagal glow

The plagal cadence IV → I, the "amen" at the end of a hymn, is softer than the authentic one: no leading tone, the tonic's C held throughout as a common tone. Its golden version keeps the held C and bends the other two voices through a golden triad on vF. Going up from I to IV the golden chord leans toward IV; coming home from IV it colors the amen.

Definition. The golden plagal glow is I – vIVg – IV and its reverse; the golden plagal amen is IV – vIVg/IV – I. In C the golden chord is vFg = vF ^A♭ C (pitch classes 14, 25, 0). I → IV and IV → I are two major triads a fourth apart, so the quality theorem guarantees exactly one golden passing chord, and the root rule puts it on the upper root F (F lies a fourth above C), shifted down: vFg, nearer F.

In the dictionary voicing the soprano falls a semitone (F → vF → ^E → E, falling class), the tenor falls a whole tone (A → ^A♭ → vA♭ → G, rising class) and C is held: one voice per class, so the golden chord appears, at 1/3 as the root rule predicts, and never-twice rules out a second. Combining both directions gives the golden plagal-authentic cadence I–IV–I–V–I: C – vA♭T13/C – vFg/C – F – vFg/F – Fmmaj7(v3,^7) – C – vCg/C – Cmmaj7(v3,^7) – G – vE♭T13/G – vCg/G – C, with vFg and vCg mirroring each other around the tonic.

The minor plagal progression I – IV – iv – I has no exact golden chord in its middle step: F → Fm is a mixed-quality pair on the same root, so the quality theorem excludes it (and iv → I, Fm → C, is mixed quality too). The dictionary's minor plagal path has one exact golden chord, vFg/C, in its first step.

vFg is the root-rule chord for the pair (C, F): with the third and fifth of C rising to F's root and third (E → F, G → A) and C held, the upper voices of I → IV move $d = (0, +1, +2)$, which is the six-four motion again, now one fourth higher. Indeed transposing the authentic cadence's upper voices G B D → G C E up a fourth gives C E G → C F A, and its golden chord vCg up a fourth (15 sixth-steps) is $\{35+15, 10+15, 21+15\} = \{14, 25, 0\}$ = vFg. The plagal glow and the golden six-four are the same path type.

The golden deceptive cadence

In a deceptive cadence the dominant promises the tonic and delivers vi instead: G → Am in C. The bass steps up instead of leaping, and the music is pulled sideways into minor. Because the roots move by a whole step, System 2 turns this into an escalator, and the two golden chords sit on A♭, the flat sixth degree. You hear the Romantic color of ♭VI just before the minor chord arrives, as if the deception were announced in advance.

Definition. The golden deceptive cadence is V – v♭VIg – ♭VIg – vi: in C, G – vA♭g – A♭g – Am. In SongMap, G:1 ~g:0.5 ~g:0.5 Am:2.

The leading tone still rises B → ^B → vC → C, exactly as in the authentic cadence; what changes is the bass, which rises by a whole step split 2 + 1 + 3 and carries the golden roots. On the strict path V → vi has no golden chord at all (roots a second apart, mixed quality). It is the uneven split of System 2 that makes the deception golden.

The same move works on any V → vi: in G major it is D – vE♭g – E♭g – Em, in A major E – vFg – Fg – F♯m. The pop-axis progression I–V–vi–IV, realized in the dictionary, contains it: C – vCg/C – Cmmaj7(v3,^7) – G – vA♭g – A♭g – Am – Am(^5) – vFg(^^3)/A – F, with two exact golden chords, both in the deceptive step.

Pitch classes: vA♭g = {23, 34, 9} ($23 + 11 = 34$, $23 + 22 = 45 \equiv 9$); A♭g = {24, 35, 10}. In sixth-steps the four voices (B, T, A, S) run $(129, 165, 177, 186) \to (131, 167, 178, 189) \to (132, 168, 179, 190) \to (135, 171, 180, 192)$. The schedules are 2 + 1 + 3 for the bass and tenor (G → A), 1 + 1 + 1 for the alto (B → C, a half step) and 3 + 1 + 2 for the soprano (D → E). The middle step is $+1$ in every voice, so, as in the escalator of Chapter 28, $P_2$ is $P_1$ planed by one sixth-step.

Mediant gold

A chromatic mediant joins two major chords whose roots are a third apart: C to A♭, C to E, C to A, C to E♭. Such moves share one common tone and shift the other two voices by a half step or a whole step, and they are the signature of Romantic and film-score harmony (Chapter 11). Every one of them has exactly one golden passing chord on the strict path, so golden harmony slots into this style with no adjustment at all.

Read the C → A♭ row as an average: Cg sits one third of the way from C to A♭ (and two thirds of the way back), with the root C held, the third falling E → vE and the fifth rising G → ^G. That gives the golden mediant neighbour I – Ig – ♭VI – Ig – I (C – Cg – A♭ – Cg – C), in which Cg is approached and left by the shortest possible motions.

Minor mediants qualify too, with the minor root rules: Cm → Em through vCg, Cm → E♭m through ^Bg, and the fifth-related minor pair Cm → Fm through Cg. The same golden chord Cg therefore lies on C ↔ A♭ in the major world and on Cm ↔ Fm in the minor world: it is a pivot between them, which the last section uses for modal mixture.

In Western harmony chromatic mediants are often explained through the Tonnetz: C → A♭ and C → E keep one common tone and move the other two voices by semitones in opposite directions (Chapter 10); C → A and C → E♭ keep one common tone and move one voice a semitone and one a whole tone in the same direction. In every case the two moving voices are in different motion classes and the common tone supplies the third class, so the admissibility condition of Chapter 27 is met. That is the structural reason every chromatic mediant has room for a golden chord.

Check of the minor-third rows. C → A (C E G → C♯ E A): C → C♯ (+1), E held, G → A (+2); the classes are rising, hold, falling, so the move is admissible, and $P_1 = X + d = (1, 12, 23)$ = ^C E vA♭ = ^Cg ($1 + 11 = 12$, $1 + 22 = 23$). C → E♭ (C E G → B♭ E♭ G): the stepwise assignment is C → B♭ (−2), E → E♭ (−1), G held; classes rising, falling, hold. $P_2 = X + 2d = (0 - 4, 12 - 2, 21) \equiv (32, 10, 21)$ = vB ^E♭ G, and $10 + 11 = 21$, $10 + 22 = 32$: the golden triad on ^E♭. All four rows are instances of the quality theorem (major pairs, roots 3 or 4 semitones apart), listed in classify.py §E.

The golden staircase and the Andalusian

Climb the major scale's chords one step at a time, I, ii, iii, IV, V, vi, and approach each new chord through two golden chords on the chromatic step below it. That is the golden staircase. Every whole step becomes an escalator; the one half step, iii → IV, cannot be golden (the half-step theorem), so there the harmony simply glides.

The Andalusian cadence descends instead: Am – G – F – E. Two descending escalators, then the half step F → E, which can only plane, landing on the Phrygian close.

Golden Andalusian. vi – ♭VIg – v♭VIg – V – ^♯IVg – v♯IVg – IV – vIV – ^III – III: in A minor (C major's relative), Am – A♭g – vA♭g – G – ^F♯g – vF♯g – F – vF – ^E – E, with four exact golden chords. The descending escalators are the ascending ones reversed (vi → V is the reverse of V → vi, V → IV of IV → V). The last step is the half-step theorem in action: F major to E major in parallel motion, every voice falling a semitone, passes through vF and ^E, the same chord shape on the v and ^ layers (Chapter 28).

Western stepwise progressions (Chapter 11) rely on the scale's two half steps for their sense of direction. In the golden staircase the half step is precisely where the golden color disappears, so the diatonic scale's asymmetry is audible as an absence.

Why every escalator in the staircase is on the chromatic root between: in each whole-step move the bass carries the golden roots and moves by the System 2 schedule chosen for it, for example C → vC♯ → C♯ → D (2 + 1 + 3) in I → ii and D → ^D → E♭ → E (1 + 2 + 3) in ii → iii. Its two intermediate pitches lie on or next to the chromatic note between the roots (vC♯ and C♯ around C♯; ^D, two sixth-steps below E♭, and E♭ itself), and the golden chords are built on them. This is a description of the dictionary's realizations (Chapter 28: 30 of the 36 whole-step pairs of its major and minor triads get two golden chords), not a theorem about all voicings.

Modal mixture and the golden Picardy

Modal mixture borrows chords from the parallel minor: C major darkened to C minor and back (Chapter 11). The golden triad on C sits between the two, closer to C major than to C minor, so it makes a soft hinge: C – Cg – Cm – Cg – C. Turn the idea around and you get a golden version of the Picardy third, the minor piece that ends on a major chord. The third of the final chord climbs from minor to major through every sixth-step in between.

The golden centroid. By the averaging principle (Chapter 25), $$\mathrm{Cg} = \tfrac{1}{3}(\mathrm{C} + \mathrm{Cm} + \mathrm{Caug}):\quad \text{third } \tfrac{12+9+12}{3} = 11,\quad \text{fifth } \tfrac{21+21+24}{3} = 22.$$ Cg is 2 sixth-steps from C (third down one, fifth up one) and 3 from Cm (third up two, fifth up one). As a pivot it belongs to both worlds: it is the strict passing chord of C ↔ A♭ (major pair) and of Cm ↔ Fm (minor pair).

Golden modal mixture: I – Ig – i, C – Cg – Cm (and back). The dictionary also has it as a four-chord form: i – Ig – vIVg/I – IV, Cm – Cg – vFg/C – F, two golden chords between C minor and F major.

Golden Picardy: i – I(^m3) – Ig – I, written in SongMap Cm Cm(^3) Cg C. The third climbs 9 → 10 → 11 → 12 sixth-steps above C, one sixth-step per chord; the fifth stays on G except at Cg, where it touches the golden fifth ^G as an upper neighbour.

The Picardy path is not a uniform path from Cm to C: a uniform path would move the third $+1$ per chord and hold the fifth, giving Cm – Cm(^3) – C(v3) – C, where C(v3) = C vE G = (11, 21). The golden Picardy replaces C(v3) by Cg, moving the fifth up one sixth-step and back; the third's motion is uniform, the fifth's is a neighbour figure. Of the 16 three-chord averages tabulated in GOLDEN_MATH.md §5, the Picardy visits (9, 21), (10, 21), (11, 22) and (12, 21). The centroid statement is exact: on a common root, the only multisets of three Western triads (major, minor, augmented, diminished) that average to (11, 22) are {major, minor, augmented} and {augmented, augmented, diminished} (proof in Appendix D).

Answer

Exercise 29.2 (root rules). Which golden chord passes strictly from E♭ major to G major, and at which position? Check with classify.py §E.

Answer

Major pair, roots a major third apart, upper root G (G lies a major third above E♭), shift 0: Gg, nearer G, at 2/3. §E lists ♭III → V as Gg@2/3.

Exercise 29.3 (deceptive). Why is there no golden chord on V → vi on the strict path, while there are two on the natural path?

Answer

Strict: G major and A minor are of mixed quality with roots a second apart, excluded by the quality theorem. Natural: the bass and tenor split their whole step 2 + 1 + 3 and the soprano 3 + 1 + 2, which puts the three upper voices on three different layers at both passing chords (the escalator mechanism).

Exercise 29.4 (averaging). Show that Cm(^3) = C ^E♭ G is the average of C major and two C minor triads, and find an average of three triads on C that equals C(v3) = C vE G.

Answer

Third (12 + 9 + 9)/3 = 10, fifth (21 + 21 + 21)/3 = 21. For (11, 21): (12 + 12 + 9)/3 = 11 and (21 + 21 + 21)/3 = 21, so C vE G = (C + C + Cm)/3 (maj + maj + min).

Exercise 29.5 (listening). In the staircase example, does the planed step Em → F sound smoother or rougher than the golden escalators around it? Describe the difference.

Answer

A listening question with no single answer. The planed step keeps one chord shape and slides it, so it tends to sound smooth but colorless; the escalators change chord type twice (Western, golden, golden, Western), which most listeners hear as a brief shimmer.
