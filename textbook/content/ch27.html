In Western harmony a passing chord is a chord that exists because voices are on their way somewhere (Chapter 12). In 36-ET the voices can travel in smaller steps than a semitone, so between two ordinary chords there is room for chords that 12-ET does not have. This chapter asks the simplest possible version of the question "which chords appear on the way?" and answers it completely. The rule is strict: every voice moves at a constant speed. Under that rule the golden triad turns out to be a very particular kind of passing chord, with exact laws about where it can appear, which root it has, and what stands next to it. This is System 1, written ~ in SongMap. The next chapter relaxes the rule (System 2), and Chapter 29 turns both into cadences and progressions.

Splitting the semitone

Play a C, then the C♯ above it. Between them lies a single semitone, the smallest step of the piano. In 36-ET that step is three steps long: C, then a C raised by a sixth-step (^C), then a C♯ lowered by a sixth-step (vC♯), then C♯. Each small step is a third of a semitone, about a third of the distance your ear is used to calling "the next note". A whole step, C to D, becomes three double steps: C, vC♯, ^C♯, D.

Listen to one voice doing this. The motion sounds like a slow slide that has been cut into equal slices. Nothing in it is a new interval in the melodic sense; it is still a half step or a whole step, only walked more slowly.

Now imagine three or four voices doing this at once, each crossing its own half step or whole step in three equal slices. The chords that appear at the first and second slices are the subject of this chapter.

Recall the units of Chapter 24. A sixth-step is 1/36 of an octave, 33.33 c. A semitone is 3 sixth-steps and a whole tone is 6. We write pitches as integers in sixth-steps (C4 = 180, because MIDI note 60 times 3) and pitch classes as integers mod 36 (C = 0, ^C = 1, vC♯ = 2, C♯ = 3, and so on). The layer of a pitch class $p$ is $\lambda(p) = p \bmod 3$:

LayerPitch classesOffset from 12-ETSpelling
00, 3, 6, …, 330 cC, C♯, D, …
11, 4, 7, …, 34+33.33 c^C, ^C♯, ^D, …
22, 5, 8, …, 35−33.33 cvC♯, vD, vE♭, …

Every Western chord lives on one layer; ordinary 12-ET music lives entirely on layer 0 (Chapter 24). A voice that moves up a semitone one sixth-step at a time visits layers 0, 1, 2, 0. A voice that moves up a whole step two sixth-steps at a time visits 0, 2, 1, 0. That simple bookkeeping, which layer each voice is on at each moment, is the key to everything below.

This chapter mirrors the Western passing chord (Chapter 12) and four-part voice leading (Chapter 14). There, voices move by semitones and whole tones and a passing chord is whatever sounds while they move. Here the same voices move through 36-ET and the passing chords can be chords that 12-ET cannot spell.

Definitions. A voicing of a chord in $v$ voices is a vector $X \in \mathbb{Z}^v$ of absolute pitches in sixth-steps; its pitch-class set is $\{x_i \bmod 36\}$. Voice order is voice identity: the $i$-th voice is one singer. A voice moves stepwise between $X$ and $Y$ if $|y_i - x_i| \le 6$ (at most a whole tone). The layer map $\lambda:\mathbb{Z}_{36}\to\mathbb{Z}_3$, $\lambda(p) = p \bmod 3$, is a group homomorphism, so $\lambda(p+q) = \lambda(p)+\lambda(q)$. A chord is single-layer if all its pitch classes have the same layer; Western chords on the 12-ET grid are single-layer on layer 0.

The uniform path

Take the most common progression in tonal music, V to I in C: G major to C major. In the usual four-part setting the bass leaps G to C and the upper voices move by the smallest steps: B rises to C, D rises to E, G stays. Now let the upper voices walk those steps in sixth-steps, all at the same pace, while the bass holds G until the arrival. Two new chords appear in the middle. To many ears the first sounds like a slightly sour, widened chord; the second has a strange warmth, as if C major were arriving early and out of focus. That second chord is a golden triad.

The rule. Fix a voicing of the two chords, $X$ and $Y$, with every voice moving at most a whole tone. Let $d_i = (y_i - x_i)/3$ be voice $i$'s motion in semitones, so $d_i \in \{-2,-1,0,1,2\}$. The uniform path moves every voice by $d_i$ sixth-steps per chord:

$$P_k = X + k\,d, \qquad k = 0, 1, 2, 3,$$

so $P_0 = X$, $P_3 = Y$, and the two passing chords are $P_1$ and $P_2$. A half step is split 1 + 1 + 1 sixth-steps, a whole step 2 + 2 + 2, a held note 0 + 0 + 0. Once the voicing is fixed the path is unique; that is what "strict" means.

The barycentric reading. Since $X + kd = X + \tfrac{k}{3}(Y - X)$,

$$P_k = \frac{(3-k)\,X + k\,Y}{3}, \qquad P_1 = \frac{2X+Y}{3},\quad P_2 = \frac{X+2Y}{3}.$$

Each passing chord is a weighted average of its neighbours, voice by voice: $P_1$ is two thirds $X$ and one third $Y$, $P_2$ the reverse. This is the averaging principle of Chapter 25 applied along a progression. In V → I the alto B (pitch class 33) moves to C (36 ≡ 0): $P_1 = (2\cdot 33 + 36)/3 = 34$, which is ^B, and $P_2 = (33 + 2\cdot 36)/3 = 35$, which is vC.

Motion classes. Start on layer 0 (any Western chord). After $k$ steps, voice $i$ sits on layer $k\,d_i \bmod 3$. Only $d_i \bmod 3$ matters, so the five possible motions fall into three classes:

ClassMotion $d$ (semitones)Layer of $P_1$Layer of $P_2$Example

The names describe the layer, not the direction of travel: a voice that rises a semitone and a voice that falls a whole tone both land on the ^ layer first, so they are in the same class. Listen to the three classes one at a time on the path C → Cg → ^E♭T13 → A♭, where the root holds, the third falls a semitone and the fifth rises a semitone:

Lemma (layers along a path). If $X$ lies on layer 0, voice $i$ of $P_k$ lies on layer $\lambda(x_i + k d_i) = k\,d_i \bmod 3$. Proof. $\lambda$ is additive and $\lambda(x_i) = 0$. ∎

So the class of $d_i$ is its residue mod 3: residue 0 is $\{0\}$ (within $|d_i|\le 2$), residue 1 is $\{+1,-2\}$ (rising), residue 2 is $\{-1,+2\}$ (falling). At $k=2$ the layers are doubled mod 3, which swaps the rising and falling layers, as in the table.

Admissibility. By the layer theorem (Chapter 25), a golden triad $\{r, r+11, r+22\}$ has its notes on layers $r$, $r+2$, $r+1$: one on each. Three voices on a uniform path from layer 0 can produce a golden triad at $P_k$ only if they occupy three different layers there, that is, only if the voice leading has exactly one voice of each class: one hold, one rising, one falling. This necessary condition already rules out most progressions: any move in which two voices share a class (for example, two voices both rising a semitone) has no golden passing chord.

The four-path theorem

Turn the question around. Start from a golden triad, Cg = C vE ^G, and ask: from which Western chords can a uniform path lead into it? The answer is that there are exactly four ways in (with the golden chord first on the path), and you can hear all four. In each one the root C holds, the third arrives from E or E♭, and the fifth arrives from G or G♯.

Three of the four destinations are ordinary triads. The fourth, C F F♯, is not a chord anyone would call home, which is why golden passing harmony in practice uses the other three kinds of path.

Fix a golden triad and a position (first or second passing chord). The admissibility condition decides which voice holds: it is the voice whose note is on the layer of the starting chord, layer 0. Each of the other two voices has exactly two motions in its class (a semitone one way or a whole tone the other), so there are $2 \times 2 = 4$ paths. Over all 36 golden triads and both positions that makes $36 \times 2 \times 4 = \mathbf{288}$ ordered paths. Of these, 240 have Western triads at both ends (major, minor, augmented, diminished, and in one type a suspended fourth); the other 48 end on a non-tertian set such as {C, F, F♯}.

Transposing by a semitone keeps every layer, and reversing a path swaps positions 1 and 2. Up to these symmetries there are exactly 12 path types. This is the complete list (golden chord at position 1; the reversed path has it at position 2):

"Root layer" is the layer of the golden chord's root: when it is 0 the root holds (Cg = C vE ^G, C held); when it is 1 the third holds (^Cg = ^C E vA♭, E held); when it is 2 the fifth holds (vC♯g = vC♯ ^E A♭, A♭ held). How to read a row: in the second row, C major moves to A♭ major with C held, E falling a semitone to E♭ and G rising a semitone to A♭. One third of the way along, the chord is C vE ^G = Cg; two thirds along it is C ^E♭ vA♭, an equal-third triad with thirds of 13 sixth-steps (bronze, T13).

Theorem (four-path theorem). Let $G = (r, r+11, r+22)$ be a golden triad in three voices and $k \in \{1,2\}$. There are exactly four motion vectors $d \in \{-2,\dots,2\}^3$ such that $X = G - k\,d$ lies on layer 0. All 4 have three distinct pitch classes at both ends. Hence there are $36\cdot 2\cdot 4 = 288$ ordered uniform paths with a golden passing chord, and they form 12 orbits of size 24 under semitone transposition and reversal.

Proof. $X$ on layer 0 means $k\,d_i \equiv \lambda(g_i) \pmod 3$ for each $i$; since $k$ is invertible mod 3, the residue of each $d_i$ is fixed. The layers $\lambda(g_i)$ are $r, r+2, r+1$, all different, so exactly one $d_i$ has residue 0 (forcing $d_i = 0$) and the other two have residues 1 and 2, with two choices each ($\{1,-2\}$ and $\{-1,2\}$). That gives 4. Two notes of $X$ cannot coincide: $x_i - x_j = (g_i - g_j) - k(d_i - d_j)$ with $g_i - g_j \equiv \pm 11$ or $\pm 14 \pmod{36}$ and $|k(d_i-d_j)| \le 8$, so the difference is never $\equiv 0$; the same argument with $3-k$ applies to $Y$. Semitone transposition adds 3 to every note and preserves layers; reversal maps $(X, P_1, P_2, Y)$ to $(Y, P_2, P_1, X)$ and so swaps $k=1$ with $k=2$. No path is fixed by a non-trivial symmetry (transposition changes the pitch classes; reversal changes $k$), so every orbit has $12 \times 2 = 24$ elements and there are $288/24 = 12$ orbits. (Counting triples of golden triad, position and motion vector counts each path exactly once because no path has two golden passing chords; that is the never-twice theorem of the next section but one, whose proof does not use this one.) ∎

The exact golden triads in the table, as pitch classes: Cg = {0, 11, 22} = C vE ^G; ^Cg = {1, 12, 23} = ^C E vA♭; vC♯g = {2, 13, 24} = vC♯ ^E A♭. In the root-layer-1 rows the held voice is the third (E), and in the root-layer-2 rows it is the fifth (A♭). The table is printed by reference/golden/classify.py, section A; 240 of the 288 paths have Western triads (including Esus4) at both ends. The full proof is repeated in Appendix D.

The quality theorem and the root rules

Which ordinary progressions hide a golden chord on the strict path? Not V to vi, not I to ii, not any move by a step. The answer is surprisingly tidy: two major chords, or two minor chords, whose roots are a third or a fourth apart. I to IV, I to V, and every chromatic mediant (C to A♭, C to E, C to A, C to E♭) qualify. When they do, there is exactly one golden chord on the way, and its root is predictable.

Quality theorem. Between two major or minor triads, a strict golden passing chord exists only when both triads have the same quality (major–major or minor–minor) and their roots are a minor third, a major third, or a fourth/fifth apart (equivalently a sixth, a sixth, or a fifth/fourth the other way). It never exists for roots a second or a tritone apart, and never between a major and a minor triad. Each qualifying ordered pair has exactly one golden passing chord. Equivalently, in the language of Chapter 10: the qualifying pairs are exactly the triads two P/L/R moves apart (from C: PL gives A♭, PR E♭, LP E, LR G, RP A, RL F), 24 × 6 = 144 ordered pairs.

Root rules, major pairs. The golden chord stands on the upper root, the root that lies a third or a fourth above the other, shifted by the interval, and it lies nearer that chord (position 1 if that chord comes first, position 2 if it comes second):

The chromatic mediants all qualify, which is why golden harmony fits so naturally into Romantic and film-score mediant writing (Chapter 29). classify.py §E lists the 68 ordered pairs, in C major plus common chromatic chords, that have a strict golden passing chord.

Theorem (quality theorem and root rules). Among all ordered pairs $(A, B)$ of distinct major or minor triads, those connected by a uniform stepwise path (three voices, any assignment of chord tones to voices) with a golden passing chord are exactly the 144 same-quality pairs with roots 3, 4 or 5 semitones apart (in either direction), and each has exactly one such golden chord, at the position and root given by the root rules.

Status: theorem, proved by a finite case check. Two independent checks establish it. (1) Reading the 12-type table: its major-to-major rows are C → A♭ (roots a major third), C → A (minor third) and C♯ → A♭ (fourth); its minor-to-minor rows are Cm → Fm (fourth), C♯m → Em (minor third) and C♯m → Fm (major third). No row joins a major and a minor triad, and none joins triads a second or a tritone apart. Each row is one orbit of 24 ordered pairs, so the six rows give $6 \times 24 = 144$ ordered pairs, one golden chord each, and the golden roots in the rows are exactly the root rules (for example C♯ → A♭: the upper root C♯ lies a fourth above A♭, and the golden chord is vC♯g at position 1, nearer C♯). The remaining Western-ended rows (three augmented–diminished rows and C♯dim → Esus4) account for the other $4 \times 24 = 96$ of the 240 paths. (2) A direct enumeration over all $24 \times 23$ ordered pairs of major and minor triads, all voice assignments and all stepwise motions finds golden passing chords for exactly 144 pairs, each with exactly one golden chord. A full write-up is in Appendix D.

Never twice, and the bronze partner

On a strict path two passing chords sound between the endpoints, and at most one of them is golden. The other one, when the endpoints are major or minor triads, is always the same kind of chord: a triad made of two equal thirds of 13 sixth-steps (about 433 c each), wider than a major third. We call it bronze. Hear it alongside its relatives on the equal-third line: diminished, silver, golden, augmented, bronze. Each chord's thirds are one sixth-step wider than the last.

Never twice. No uniform path has both passing chords golden. A golden chord can open or close the middle of a strict transition, never fill it.

Partner theorem. On every strict path between Western triads, the passing chord that is not golden is an equal-third triad $T_n = \{0, n, 2n\}$: bronze ($n = 13$, C ^E vA) in 168 of the 240 paths, and silver ($n = 10$, C ^E♭ vG) in the other 72. Silver appears only on paths between an augmented and a diminished triad (three of the 12 types); every path between major or minor triads has a bronze partner.

One caution for four-part writing: the partner theorem is about three moving voices. When SongMap realizes C:1 ~:0.5 ~:0.5 A:2 in four parts with a held bass, the second passing chord is labelled vC♯g7(v3,^5,^7)/C, because the bass C sits under the three upper voices. The upper voices themselves still follow the table.

Golden sevenths on strict paths are rare. In four voices only four types (up to transposition and reversal; 96 paths in all) reach a golden seventh between Western chords: C♯m7 → ^Cg7 → ^G T13 → Cmaj7, C♯m7 → vC♯g7 → A♭ T13 → C♯maj7, and two from an augmented triad (one note doubled) to a diminished-seventh chord, Caug → ^Cg7 → … → C♯dim7 and Caug → vC♯g7 → … → A♭dim7. Golden sevenths become common on natural paths (Chapter 28).

Theorem (never twice). No uniform stepwise path in three voices from a layer-0 chord has both $P_1$ and $P_2$ golden.

Proof. Suppose $P_1 = X + d$ is the golden triad $G$ with root $r$. By the lemma, voice $i$ of $P_1$ is on layer $\lambda(g_i) = d_i \bmod 3$, and voice $i$ of $P_2 = P_1 + d$ is on layer $2d_i \equiv -\lambda(g_i)$. So the voices carrying G's root, third and fifth (layers $r$, $r+2$, $r+1$) sit in $P_2$ on layers $-r$, $-r+1$, $-r+2$. If $P_2$ is a golden triad with root $r'$, its root, third and fifth are on layers $r'$, $r'+2$, $r'+1$, and matching layers forces one of three role assignments. If $r' \equiv -r$: the root voice stays the root and the third voice becomes the fifth, so $r + d_0 = r'$ and $r + 11 + d_1 = r' + 22$, giving $d_1 - d_0 \equiv 11 \pmod{36}$. If $r' \equiv -r+1$: the third voice becomes the root and the root voice becomes the third, giving $d_0 - d_1 \equiv 22$. If $r' \equiv -r + 2$: the fifth voice becomes the root and the third voice the third, giving $d_1 - d_2 \equiv 22$. But $|d_i - d_j| \le 4$, so none of these congruences can hold. ∎

An exhaustive check confirms it and extends it to four voices (with doublings) and golden sevenths: among all uniform stepwise paths from layer-0 chords, none has two golden passing chords (triads or sevenths).

Theorem (partner). On the 240 golden paths with Western triads at both ends, the non-golden passing chord is $T_{13}$ in 168 paths and $T_{10}$ in 72. Proof. Read the $P_2$ column of the 10 Western-ended types: seven are T13 (bronze) and three, exactly the augmented-to-diminished types, are silver; each type has 24 paths, so $7 \times 24 = 168$ and $3 \times 24 = 72$. ∎ (Verified by classify.py §C.)

The equal-third line. $T_n = (0, n, 2n)$ is an affine function of $n$, so the points $T_9, \dots, T_{13}$ are equally spaced on one line in $\mathbb{R}^3$, and $T_{12} - T_{11} = T_{11} - T_{10} = (0, 1, 2)$. Hence Caug → Cg → Cs → Cdim is the uniform path with $d = (0, -1, -2)$: the first row of the four-path table.

Worked example: the golden six-four

The most important single result of System 1 is a cadence. In Western harmony the cadential six-four (Chapter 10, Chapter 14) puts a C major triad over the dominant bass G, then lets its upper notes fall to the dominant before the final tonic: C/G → G → C. The golden six-four does the opposite. It starts on the dominant, keeps G in the bass, and lets the leading tone and the second degree rise through two passing chords. The second of them is a golden triad a sixth-step below the tonic, sitting on the dominant bass: the tonic arriving early, out of focus.

In a musical phrase the golden six-four is short and on a weak beat; the ear hears V, a shimmer, and I.

  • $P_2$ = vCg/G (vIg/V): the upper voices vC ^E♭ G form the golden triad on vC (35, 10, 21: thirds of 11). Its fifth is G, the bass, so the bass belongs to the golden chord; the label "/G" names a golden triad in second inversion, a golden six-four in the literal sense.
  • The leading tone rises B → ^B → vC → C and the second degree rises D → vE♭ → ^E♭ → E. Both voices move up, but in different classes (half step and whole step), which is exactly what admissibility requires; the held G supplies the third class.

The barycentric reading makes vCg concrete: it is $(X + 2Y)/3$ voice by voice, one third V and two thirds I. The root rule predicts it too: G and C are major triads a fourth apart, so the golden chord is on the upper root C (C lies a fourth above G), shifted down a sixth-step, nearer C.

Three-chord form. A single placeholder, G:1 ~:1 C:2, keeps only the more golden of the two passing chords: G – vCg/G – C. This is the golden authentic cadence of Chapter 29.

In sixth-steps (MIDI × 3), with voices (B, T, A, S):

$$X = (129, 165, 177, 186),\quad d = (0, 0, +1, +2),\quad Y_{\text{upper}} = X + 3d = (\cdot, 165, 180, 192),$$

and the bass moves 129 → 144 only on the arrival. So $P_1 = (129, 165, 178, 188)$ and $P_2 = (129, 165, 179, 190)$. Reducing mod 36: $P_1 = \{21, 34, 8\}$ = {G, ^B, vE♭}, and $8 + 13 = 21$, $21 + 13 = 34$: the triad $T_{13}$ rooted on 8 = vE♭. $P_2 = \{21, 35, 10\}$ = {G, vC, ^E♭}, and $35 + 11 = 46 \equiv 10$, $35 + 22 = 57 \equiv 21$: the golden triad rooted on 35 = vC. Within the three upper voices this is the row "(−1, −2, 0) … vC♯g" of the four-path table transposed down a semitone (C → vCg → vE♭T13 → G, the fifth G held) and then reversed, which is why the golden chord comes second.

In exact golden tuning vCg would be vC · $2/\varphi$ · $4/\varphi^2$, i.e. its third 366.91 c and its fifth 733.82 c above vC; 36-ET renders them as 11 and 22 sixth-steps (366.67 c and 733.33 c).

Exercise 27.1 (layers). A voice moves down a whole tone from E to D on a uniform path. Name its four pitches and their layers.

Answer

E (12, layer 0), ^E♭ (10, layer 1), vE♭ (8, layer 2), D (6, layer 0). Motion $d = -2$ is in the rising class: it reaches the ^ layer first.

Exercise 27.2 (admissibility). F major moves to G major with every voice rising a whole tone (F→G, A→B, C→D). Can either strict passing chord be golden?

Answer

No. All three voices have $d = +2$, the falling class, so each passing chord lies on a single layer: the path is F, vF♯, ^F♯, G, every chord a planed F major triad. A golden triad needs one voice in each class. (System 2 does find golden chords here: Chapter 28.)

Exercise 27.3 (root rules). Predict the strict golden passing chord and its position for E♭ → C, for Am → Em and for E → A♭ (E and A♭ = G♯ are a major third apart).

Answer

E♭ → C: major, minor third, upper root E♭ shifted ^: ^E♭g, nearer E♭, so at position 1/3. Am → Em: minor, fifth/fourth, lower root (the root a fourth below the other) is E: Eg, nearer Em, so at 2/3. E → A♭: major, major third, upper root A♭ (G♯ lies a major third above E), shift 0: A♭g, nearer A♭, at 2/3. (classify.py §E lists III → ♭VI as A♭g@2/3.)

Exercise 27.4 (barycenter). Using $P_1 = (2X+Y)/3$, compute the first passing chord of Cm → Fm with voices C→C, E♭→F, G→A♭, and identify it.

C: (0+0+0)/3 = 0; E♭→F: (2·9+15)/3 = 11; G→A♭: (2·21+24)/3 = 22. So $P_1 = \{0, 11, 22\}$ = Cg.

Exercise 27.5 (listening). In the four-paths example, which destination sounds most like a resolution of Cg, and which least? Relate your answer to the partner chord in each path.

Answer

Answers vary; this is a listening question, not a theorem. Many listeners hear Fm and A♭ as the clearest arrivals (bronze partners, major or minor endpoints) and the cluster C F F♯ as the least, with Cdim in between (silver partner, diminished endpoint).

Exercise 27.6 (proof). Show that on a uniform path from a layer-0 chord, a passing chord in which all voices are in one class is the starting chord transposed by $k$ or $2k$ sixth-steps only when all $d_i$ are equal. What is it in general?

Answer

If all $d_i$ are equal to $d$, $P_k = X + kd$ is $X$ transposed by $kd$ sixth-steps: planing. If the $d_i$ are in one class but unequal (some $+1$, some $-2$), $P_k$ is single-layer but not a transposition of $X$; it is a Western chord shape on layer $k d_i \bmod 3$ whose intervals differ from $X$'s by multiples of 3 sixth-steps, for example a different 12-ET chord moved onto the ^ layer.
