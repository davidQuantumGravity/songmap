Sources

This bibliography lists only the sources that the chapters actually cite, grouped by topic, each with a link to where it is discussed. An author, a year and a title are given only where the chapter states them and they are certain; where the book names an author and a year without a title, none is supplied here. No page numbers, editions or identifiers are given. Musical works (Bach's Well-Tempered Clavier, Wagner's Tristan und Isolde, Debussy's preludes and so on) are discussed in the chapters and are not listed as sources. Nothing on this page is a quotation.

Ancient and medieval theory

  • Philolaus (5th century BCE): describes the ratios of the fifth-generated scale, including the tone 9/8 and the limma 256/243 (§5.5).
  • Plato, Timaeus (c. 360 BCE): the same ratios (§5.5).
  • Euclid, Elements, Book VI: division of a line in extreme and mean ratio (§23.1).
  • Ptolemy (2nd century CE): a division of the fourth into 16/15, 9/8 and 10/9 (§4.1).
  • Boethius, De institutione musica (early 6th century CE): carried Greek tuning theory and the terms limma, apotome and comma into medieval Europe (§5.5, §42.1).
  • Anonymous, Musica enchiriadis and Scolica enchiriadis (9th century): the first written polyphony, parallel organum (§42.1).
  • Fibonacci, Liber Abaci (1202): the sequence whose ratios approach φ (§23.1).

Renaissance and Baroque treatises

  • Luca Pacioli, De divina proportione (1509) (§23.1).
  • Pietro Aron (1523): a description usually read as quarter-comma meantone (§7.1).
  • Heinrich Glarean, Dodecachordon (1547): the Aeolian and Ionian modes (§6.5).
  • Gioseffo Zarlino, Le istitutioni harmoniche (1558): the senario and the just thirds (§4.1, §42.2).
  • Zhu Zaiyu (1584) and Simon Stevin (c. 1605): the first exact computations of equal temperament (§7.3).
  • John Napier (1614): logarithms (§1.3).
  • Marin Mersenne, Harmonie universelle (1636): popularized equal temperament; Mersenne's laws of strings, published in the 1630s (§7.3, §2.1).
  • Christiaan Huygens: the 31-fold division of the octave (17th century) and the synchronization of pendulum clocks (1665) (§7.5, §41.3).
  • Nicholas Mercator: the 53-fold division (17th century) (§5.5).
  • Andreas Werckmeister, Musicalische Temperatur (1691): well temperaments, among them Werckmeister III (§7.2).
  • Jean-Philippe Rameau, Traité de l'harmonie (1722): chord roots, inversion and the fundamental bass (§9.2, §10.2, §42.2).
  • Johann Joseph Fux, Gradus ad Parnassum (1725): species counterpoint and the rules of motion (§14.2, §15.1).
  • Leonhard Euler, Tentamen novae theoriae musicae (1739): the gradus suavitatis and the lattice of fifths and thirds (§3.3, §4.3).
  • Jean le Rond d'Alembert (1747): the wave equation for the string; Daniel Bernoulli: a string's motion as a sum of its modes (§2.1).

Acoustics, hearing and timbre

  • Joseph Fourier, work on heat (published 1822): sums of sines (§2.3).
  • Heinrich Wilhelm Dove (1839): binaural beats (§41.1).
  • Georg Ohm (1843): the ear's insensitivity to phase (§2.3).
  • Hermann von Helmholtz, On the Sensations of Tone (1863): dissonance as roughness (§3.2).
  • Ernst Weber and Gustav Fechner (19th century): the logarithmic law of perception; Fechner (1876) on golden rectangles (§1.3, §16.4).
  • Carl Stumpf (late 19th century): tonal fusion (§14.2).
  • Harvey Fletcher and Wilden Munson (1933): equal-loudness contours (§1.2).
  • Reinier Plomp and Willem Levelt (1965): consonance ratings of pure-tone pairs and the critical band (§3.2, §17.3).
  • Manfred Schroeder (1970): low-crest-factor phases (§44.2).
  • Ernst Terhardt (1970s): virtual pitch (§3.3).
  • John Grey (1977), "Multidimensional perceptual scaling of musical timbres" (§17.1).
  • Brian Glasberg and Brian Moore (1990): the equivalent rectangular bandwidth (§3.2).
  • Albert Bregman, Auditory Scene Analysis (1990) (§13.2, §17.2).
  • Paul Erlich (1990s): harmonic entropy (§3.4, §26.1).
  • James Tenney: Tenney height (§3.3).
  • William Sethares, Tuning, Timbre, Spectrum, Scale (1998): roughness for arbitrary spectra (§3.2), and matching timbre to tuning, the basis of the book's golden-spectrum tones (§3.5, §17.3, §26.2, §44.3).
  • ITU-R BS.1770: integrated loudness measurement (§44.4).

Perception, cognition and culture

  • Robert Zajonc (1968): the mere-exposure effect (§42.4).
  • W. Jay Dowling: experiments on melodic contour and memory (§13.1).
  • Carol Krumhansl and Edward Kessler (1982): probe-tone ratings of tonal fit (§10.1).
  • Leonard Meyer, Explaining Music (1973): gap-fill (§13.2).
  • Eugene Narmour (1990): the implication-realization model (§13.2).
  • David Huron (1996): the melodic arch in a corpus of folk-song phrases (§13.1).
  • Paul von Hippel and David Huron (2000), "Why do skips precede reversals?" (§13.2).
  • David Huron (2001), "Tone and voice: a derivation of the rules of voice-leading from perceptual principles" (§14.2).
  • Fred Lerdahl and Carol Krumhansl (2007), "Modeling Tonal Tension", Music Perception (§12.5).
  • Josh McDermott and colleagues (2016, Nature): consonance preferences of the Tsimane' (§42.4).

Harmony, form and analysis

  • Carl Friedrich Weitzmann (1853): the augmented triad (§18.3).
  • Arthur von Oettingen and Hugo Riemann (19th century): the Tonnetz (§4.3).
  • Hugo Riemann, Vereinfachte Harmonielehre (1893): tonic, dominant and subdominant functions (§10.3).
  • Heinrich Schenker, Der freie Satz (1935) (§13.5).
  • Olivier Messiaen, Technique de mon langage musical (1944): the modes of limited transposition (§19.3).
  • Arthur Berger (1963): the name "octatonic", in an article on Stravinsky (§19.2, §22.2).
  • Arnold Schoenberg, Fundamentals of Musical Composition (published 1967) (§16.2).
  • Ernő Lendvai, Béla Bartók: An Analysis of His Music (1971), and Hungarian publications from the 1950s: the golden section and the axes in Bartók (§16.4, §22.2).
  • Allen Forte, The Structure of Atonal Music (1973): set-class names and the Z-relation (§20.1, §20.5).
  • John Rahn (1980): the prime-form convention (§20.1).
  • Milton Babbitt (1940s–50s): hexachordal combinatoriality and the hexachord theorem (§20.4, §21.3).
  • Charles Rosen, Sonata Forms (1980) (§16.3).
  • Fred Lerdahl and Ray Jackendoff, A Generative Theory of Tonal Music (1983) (§16.1).
  • Jeff Pressing (1983): the parallel between rhythms and scales (§16.1).
  • Roy Howat (1983), "Bartók, Lendvai and the principles of proportional analysis", Music Analysis; and Debussy in Proportion (1983) (§16.4, §23.1).
  • Pieter van den Toorn, The Music of Igor Stravinsky (1983) (§22.2).
  • Christopher Longuet-Higgins and Christopher Lee (1984): a measure of syncopation (§16.1).
  • David Lewin (from the 1980s), Brian Hyer and Richard Cohn (1990s): neo-Riemannian theory, hexatonic poles and the Weitzmann region (§4.4, §10.5, §18.3).
  • Jack Douthett and Peter Steinbach (1998): the "Cube Dance" graph (§14.4).
  • William Caplin, Classical Form (1998): period and sentence (§16.2).
  • Fred Lerdahl, Tonal Pitch Space (2001) (§12.5).
  • Godfried Toussaint (2005): Euclidean rhythms (§16.1).
  • Dmitri Tymoczko (2006), "The Geometry of Musical Chords", Science; and A Geometry of Music (2011) (§9.4, §14.4, §25.3).
  • James Hepokoski and Warren Darcy, Elements of Sonata Theory (2006) (§16.3).
  • Clifton Callender, Ian Quinn and Dmitri Tymoczko (2008), "Generalized voice-leading spaces", Science (§14.4).

Orchestration and the composers' own writings

  • Hector Berlioz (1844), a treatise on orchestration; Nikolai Rimsky-Korsakov, Principles of Orchestration (published 1913) (§17.2).
  • Ferruccio Busoni, Sketch of a New Esthetic of Music (1907): third-tones and their combination into sixth-tones (§22.6, §24.1).
  • Henry Cowell, New Musical Resources (written around 1919, published 1930): tone clusters (§22.3).
  • Julián Carrillo: Sonido 13 (§22.6).
  • Ivan Wyschnegradsky (1932): a manual of quarter-tone harmony (§22.6).
  • Harry Partch, Genesis of a Music (1949): 43-tone just intonation (§22.6).
  • Hugues Dufourt (1979): the label "spectral music" (§22.4).

Tuning theory and notation

  • Erv Wilson (1970s): moments of symmetry (§6.2, Appendix D).
  • Norman Carey and David Clampitt (1989): well-formed scales (Appendix D).
  • Thorvald Kornerup: the "golden meantone"; Heinz Bohlen: an 833-cent scale on the φ interval (§23.1).
  • Kite Giedraitis: the "ups and downs" notation for equal divisions of the octave (§24.3, §45.1).

Mathematics

  • Moritz Stern (1858) and Achille Brocot (1861): the Stern–Brocot tree (§6.3).
  • Adolf Hurwitz (1891): φ is the hardest number to approximate by fractions (§23.1, §41.2).
  • Hugo Steinhaus (conjecture); Vera Sós, Stanisław Świerczkowski and János Surányi (proofs, late 1950s): the three-gap theorem (§6.1, Appendix D).
  • Andrey Kolmogorov (1954), Jürgen Moser (1962) and Vladimir Arnold (1963): KAM theory; John Greene (1979): the residue criterion and the robustness of the golden torus (§41.3).

Brain rhythms and beats

  • Hans Berger (1929): the first human EEG (§41.2).
  • Gerald Oster (1973), "Auditory beats in the brain", Scientific American (§41.1).
  • Roopun and colleagues (2008, Frontiers in Neuroscience): ratios near φ between cortical rhythms (§41.2).
  • Pletzer, Kerschbaum and Klimesch (2010), "When frequencies never synchronize: the golden mean and the resting EEG", Brain Research: the golden-ratio EEG hypothesis (§41.2).
  • Garcia-Argibay, Santed and Reales (2019): a meta-analysis of binaural-beat studies (§41.1).
