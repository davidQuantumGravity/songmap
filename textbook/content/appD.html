This appendix collects the proofs behind the theorems of Parts II, VI and VII. Each result is labelled by how it was established. A proof is a complete argument. A finite check is an exhaustive computer enumeration of a finite, precisely defined set of cases, which is also a proof, but one you verify by rerunning code rather than by reading; the scripts are named. Where both exist, both are given. Results that depend on a voicing model or a listening model are measurements, and they are not proved here.

Notation: $\alpha$ is a generator as a fraction of an octave ($\log_2(3/2)$ for the fifth, $\log_2\varphi$ for the golden sixth); $\{x\}$ is the fractional part; cents are $1200\log_2$; pitch classes in 36-ET are elements of $\mathbb{Z}_{36}$ (sixth-steps), and the layer of $p$ is $\lambda(p) = p \bmod 3$.

Tuning and scales

D.1 The generators are irrational

Proposition. $\log_2(3/2)$ and $\log_2\varphi$ are irrational.

Proof. If $\log_2(3/2) = m/n$ with integers $n \ge 1$, then $3^n = 2^{m+n}$, impossible since the left side is odd and the right side even (or equal to 1 only if $n = 0$). If $\log_2\varphi = m/n$, then $\varphi^n = 2^m$. Here $\varphi = (1+\sqrt5)/2$ has conjugate $\psi = (1-\sqrt5)/2$ and norm $\varphi\psi = -1$, so the norm of $\varphi^n$ is $(-1)^n$, while the norm of the rational number $2^m$ is $2^{2m}$. Hence $2^{2m} = \pm 1$, so $m = 0$ and $\varphi^n = 1$, forcing $n = 0$. ∎

Consequently no number of fifths, or of golden sixths, ever equals a whole number of octaves: every chain has a comma.

D.2 Limma, apotome and comma

Pythagorean. The limma is $2^8/3^5 = 256/243$ (90.22 c), the apotome $3^7/2^{11} = 2187/2048$ (113.69 c), the whole tone $9/8$ (203.91 c). Then limma × apotome $= 3^2/2^3 = 9/8$ and apotome ÷ limma $= 3^{12}/2^{19}$, the Pythagorean comma (23.46 c). In units of octaves with $\alpha = \log_2(3/2)$: tone $= 2\alpha - 1$, limma $= 3 - 5\alpha$, and the diatonic octave is $5(2\alpha - 1) + 2(3 - 5\alpha) = 1$ exactly.

Golden. With $g = \log_2\varphi$: golden apotome $\varphi^3/4$, i.e. $3g - 2$ (99.27 c); golden limma $2^7/\varphi^{10}$, i.e. $7 - 10g$ (69.10 c). Their ratio is $$\frac{\varphi^3/4}{2^7/\varphi^{10}} = \frac{\varphi^{13}}{2^9},$$ the golden comma (30.17 c): thirteen golden sixths minus nine octaves. The golden13 scale has 10 apotomes and 3 limmas, and $10(3g-2) + 3(7-10g) = 1$ exactly, for every value of $g$. The golden7 scale has 4 golden apotomes and 3 large steps of $2(3g-2) + (7 - 10g) = 3 - 4g$ (267.64 c), and $4(3g-2) + 3(3-4g) = 1$.

The 36-comma. $\varphi^{36}/2^{25}$ is $1200(36g - 25) = -8.75$ c: thirty-six golden sixths fall 8.75 c short of 25 octaves. 36-ET tempers it out by setting the golden sixth to 25 steps, 833.33 c, which is 0.24 c above $\varphi$ (833.09 c). 13-ET sets it to 9 steps, $9/13 \cdot 1200 = 830.77$ c, 2.32 c below.

D.3 The three-gap theorem

Steinhaus conjectured it; the first proofs were published in the late 1950s by Sós, by Świerczkowski and by Surányi. The proof below follows the classical argument by cases.

Setting. Let $\alpha \in (0,1)$ be irrational and $N \ge 2$. Place the $N$ points $x_k = \{k\alpha\}$, $k = 0, 1, \dots, N-1$, on the circle $\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}$ (distinct, since $\alpha$ is irrational). Among $k = 1, \dots, N-1$ let $u$ be the index of the smallest $x_k$ and $v$ the index of the largest, and put $$a = x_u, \qquad b = 1 - x_v.$$ So $x_k + a \equiv x_{k+u}$ and $x_k + b \equiv x_{k-v}$ (mod 1) whenever those indices exist. Note $a \ne b$, since $a = b$ would make $(u+v)\alpha$ an integer.

Lemma 1. $u + v \ge N$.

Proof. If $u + v \le N-1$, then $x_{u+v} \equiv a - b$. If $a > b$ this is a point in $(0, a)$, contradicting the minimality of $a$; if $a < b$ it is a point in $(1-b, 1)$, contradicting the maximality of $x_v = 1 - b$. ∎

So the index set $\{0, \dots, N-1\}$ splits into three consecutive ranges $[0, N-u)$, $[N-u, v)$, $[v, N)$, the middle one possibly empty.

Theorem (three-gap). The gap from $x_k$ to the next point (in the increasing direction) is $a$ if $k < N-u$, it is $a+b$ if $N - u \le k < v$, and it is $b$ if $k \ge v$. Hence the $N$ gaps take at most three lengths, $a$, $b$ and $a+b$, occurring $N-u$, $N-v$ and $u+v-N$ times.

Proof. In each case we name a point at the claimed distance and show that no point lies strictly between. Write $t \in (0,1)$ for the distance from $x_k$ forward to a hypothetical point $x_j$ in that open arc.

  • Case $k < N-u$. The point $x_{k+u} = x_k + a$ exists. Suppose $t \in (0, a)$. If $j > k$, then $x_{j-k} = t < a$ with $1 \le j-k \le N-1$: contradiction. If $j < k$, put $m = k - j$; then $x_m = 1 - t$, and since $m \le k < N - u$ the index $m + u \le N-1$ exists, with $x_{m+u} = x_m + a - 1 = a - t \in (0, a)$: contradiction.
  • Case $k \ge v$. The point $x_{k-v} = x_k + b$ exists. Suppose $t \in (0, b)$. If $j < k$, then $x_{k-j} = 1 - t > 1 - b$: contradiction with the maximum. If $j > k$, put $m = j - k \le N - 1 - k \le N - 1 - v$; then $x_m = t$ and $x_{m+v} = t + 1 - b \in (1 - b, 1)$: contradiction.
  • Case $N-u \le k < v$. The index $i = k + u - v$ satisfies $N - v \le i \le u - 1$, so it exists, and $x_i = x_k + a + b$. Suppose $t \in (0, a+b)$ with $j \ne i$. If $j > k$: $j - k \le N-1-k \le u-1$, so $t = x_{j-k} \ge a$, and $t \ne a$ because $j - k \ne u$; thus $t \in (a, a+b)$, and the index $u - (j-k) \in [1, u-1]$ has $x_{u-(j-k)} = a - t + 1 \in (1-b, 1)$: contradiction with the maximum. If $j < k$: $m = k - j \in [1, v-1]$, so $x_m = 1 - t \in (1 - a - b, 1)$ and $x_m < 1 - b$ (as $m \ne v$); then $v - m \in [1, v-1]$ has $x_{v-m} = (1-b) - x_m \in (0, a)$: contradiction with the minimum. ∎

Corollary (sum rule). Adding all gaps, $(N-u)a + (N-v)b + (u+v-N)(a+b) = va + ub = 1$.

D.4 Well-formed scales and the Stern–Brocot path

Theorem. The scale of $N$ notes $\{0, \alpha, \dots, (N-1)\alpha\}$ (mod 1) has exactly two step sizes if and only if $N = u + v$, and this happens exactly when $N$ is the denominator of a fraction on the Stern–Brocot path of $\alpha$. In that case there are $v$ steps of size $a$ and $u$ steps of size $b$.

Proof. By the three-gap theorem there are two sizes iff the middle range is empty, i.e. $u + v \le N$, i.e. (Lemma 1) $u + v = N$. Write $a = u\alpha - p$ and $b = q - v\alpha$ with integers $p, q$, so $p/u < \alpha < q/v$. The sum rule gives $v(u\alpha - p) + u(q - v\alpha) = uq - vp = 1$. Fractions $p/u < q/v$ with $uq - vp = 1$ are neighbours in the Stern–Brocot tree (each node of the tree is the mediant of its two nearest ancestors, which form such a pair, and every such pair in $[0,1]$ arises this way); the path from the root to $\alpha$ passes through a node exactly when its two ancestors bracket $\alpha$. So $(p+q)/(u+v) = (p+q)/N$ lies on $\alpha$'s path.

Conversely let $p/u < \alpha < q/v$ with $uq - vp = 1$ and $N = u + v$. We show that $u$ and $v$ are the record indices for $N$ points. Any $1 \le k \le N-1$ and any integer $r$ can be written $(k, r) = s(u, p) + t(v, q)$ with integers $s, t$, because the matrix with rows $(u,p)$ and $(v,q)$ has determinant $\pm 1$. Then $k\alpha - r = sa - tb$ and $k = su + tv$. If $s, t \ge 1$ then $k \ge N$; if $s, t \le 0$ then $k \le 0$; so $s$ and $t$ have opposite signs or one is zero. If $0 < k\alpha - r \le a$: $t = 0$ gives $s \ge 1$ and $k\alpha - r = sa$, so $s = 1$ and $k = u$; $s = 0$ gives $k\alpha - r = -tb < 0$ for $t \ge 1$; $s \ge 1, t \le -1$ gives $sa + |t|b > a$; $s \le -1, t \ge 1$ gives a negative value. So $u$ is the unique minimizer. The same argument with the roles of $a$ and $b$ exchanged shows $v$ is the maximizer. Hence $u + v = N$ and the scale has two step sizes. ∎

D.5 Best approximations

The continued fractions are $\log_2(3/2) = [0; 1, 1, 2, 2, 3, 1, 5, \dots]$ and $\log_2\varphi = [0; 1, 2, 3, 1, 2, 3, 2, \dots]$. Convergents of the second are $1/1, 2/3, 7/10, 9/13, 25/36, 84/121, \dots$: 9/13 is why thirteen golden sixths nearly make nine octaves, and 25/36 is why 36-ET carries the golden chain so well. (The number $\varphi$ itself has the all-ones continued fraction $[1; 1, 1, \dots]$; that is a statement about $\varphi$, not about $\log_2\varphi$.)

Golden chords

D.6 Identities of the golden ratio

Let $\varphi = (1+\sqrt5)/2$ and $\psi = (1-\sqrt5)/2 = -1/\varphi$; both satisfy $x^2 = x + 1$. With the Fibonacci numbers $F_0 = 0, F_1 = 1, F_{n+1} = F_n + F_{n-1}$:

Lemma. $\varphi^n = F_n\varphi + F_{n-1}$ and $\psi^n = F_n\psi + F_{n-1}$ for $n \ge 1$; hence $F_{n+1} - F_n\varphi = \psi^n$.

Proof. Induction: $\varphi^{n+1} = F_n\varphi^2 + F_{n-1}\varphi = (F_n + F_{n-1})\varphi + F_n$. The same holds for $\psi$. Then $F_{n+1} - F_n\varphi = F_n + F_{n-1} - F_n\varphi = F_n(1 - \varphi) + F_{n-1} = F_n\psi + F_{n-1} = \psi^n$, using $1 - \varphi = \psi$. ∎

In particular $\varphi^3 = 2\varphi + 1 = 2 + \sqrt5$, so $\varphi^{-3} = \sqrt5 - 2 \approx 0.23607$ and $\varphi^{-6} = (\sqrt5-2)^2 = 9 - 4\sqrt5 \approx 0.055728$. With $n = 6$: $13 - 8\varphi = \psi^6 = \varphi^{-6}$.

Proposition (the golden seventh below the octave). $8/\varphi^3 = 2 - 2\varphi^{-6}$.

Proof. Put $x = \varphi^{-3} = \sqrt5 - 2$. Then $x^2 + 4x - 1 = (9 - 4\sqrt5) + (4\sqrt5 - 8) - 1 = 0$, so $1 - x^2 = 4x$, i.e. $2 - 2x^2 = 8x$, which is the claim. ∎ Numerically $8/\varphi^3 = 1.8885438\ldots$ (1100.73 c), and the gap below the octave is $2\varphi^{-6} = 0.11146$. This is why a close golden seventh on harmonic tones beats at $2f_0\varphi^{-6}$ against the root's octave; the base beat $f_0(13 - 8\varphi) = f_0\varphi^{-6}$ comes from the Fibonacci approximation $13/8 \approx \varphi$. At $f_0 = 110$ Hz: $f_0\varphi^{-6}\times(1, 2/\varphi, 4/\varphi^2) = 6.13, 7.58, 9.37$ Hz and $2f_0\varphi^{-6} = 12.26$ Hz. (The derivation of which partials beat is in Chapter 26; the beat spectrum itself depends on the timbre and is a measurement.)

D.7 Golden intervals in 36-ET

The golden third $2/\varphi$ is 366.91 c, the golden fifth $4/\varphi^2$ 733.82 c, the golden seventh $8/\varphi^3$ 1100.73 c. 36-ET renders them as 11, 22 and 33 sixth-steps (366.67, 733.33 and 1100 c). Each golden third is 0.24 c wider than 11 sixth-steps, so the errors add up linearly: 0.24, 0.49 and 0.73 c. The 36-ET golden seventh is exactly the 12-ET major seventh.

D.8 The layer theorem

Theorem. A golden triad $\{r, r+11, r+22\}$ has one note on each layer. A golden seventh $\{r, r+11, r+22, r+33\}$ has its root and seventh on the root's layer and one note on each of the other two.

Proof. $\lambda$ is additive, $11 \equiv 2$, $22 \equiv 1$ and $33 \equiv 0 \pmod 3$, so the layers are $\lambda(r), \lambda(r)+2, \lambda(r)+1$ (all different) and, for the seventh, also $\lambda(r)$. ∎ A Western chord, built from multiples of 3 sixth-steps, lies on a single layer.

D.9 The averaging principle: uniqueness of the centroids

On a common root write triads as (third, fifth) in sixth-steps: major (12, 21), minor (9, 21), augmented (12, 24), diminished (9, 18).

Proposition. The only multisets of three of these triads whose average is golden (11, 22) are {major, minor, augmented} and {augmented, augmented, diminished}; the only ones whose average is silver (10, 20) are {major, minor, diminished} and {augmented, diminished, diminished}.

Proof. Golden needs third sum 33; the thirds are 12 or 9, so exactly two chords have third 12 (major or augmented) and one has third 9 (minor or diminished). The fifth sum must be 66. Listing the six cases: (maj, maj, min) 63; (maj, maj, dim) 60; (maj, aug, min) 66; (maj, aug, dim) 63; (aug, aug, min) 69; (aug, aug, dim) 66. Silver needs third sum 30, so one chord from {maj, aug} and two from {min, dim}, and fifth sum 60: (maj, min, min) 63; (maj, min, dim) 60; (maj, dim, dim) 57; (aug, min, min) 66; (aug, min, dim) 63; (aug, dim, dim) 60. ∎

Universality. Every 36-ET pitch is an average of three 12-ET pitches: if $p = 3m + e$ with $e \in \{0, 1, 2\}$, then $p = (3m + 3m + (3m + 3e))/3$. Applied voice by voice, every 36-ET chord is an average of three 12-ET chords, so the principle's musical content lies in averaging related chords.

D.10 The equal-third line

$T_n = (0, n, 2n)$ is affine in $n$, so $T_9$ (diminished), $T_{10}$ (silver), $T_{11}$ (golden), $T_{12}$ (augmented) and $T_{13}$ (bronze) are equally spaced on one line, with $T_{n+1} - T_n = (0, 1, 2)$. Bronze is golden reflected through augmented: $2T_{12} - T_{11} = T_{13}$. Stacking thirds of $n$ sixth-steps returns after $36/\gcd(n, 36)$ notes: 4 for $n = 9$, 18 for 10, 36 for 11, 3 for 12, 36 for 13. Only 11 and 13 are coprime to 36, so only golden and bronze thirds pass through all 36 pitch classes.

Passing harmony

D.11 Setting

A voicing in $v$ voices is $X \in \mathbb{Z}^v$ (sixth-steps); voice order is voice identity. A uniform stepwise path is $P_k = X + k\,d$, $k = 0, \dots, 3$, with $d \in \{-2, \dots, 2\}^v$, so $Y = X + 3d$ and each voice moves at most a whole tone. $P_k = ((3-k)X + kY)/3$. If $X$ is on layer 0, voice $i$ of $P_k$ is on layer $k d_i \bmod 3$; the residue of $d_i$ mod 3 is its class: hold (0), rising (+1, −2), falling (−1, +2). All triad results below are for three voices; the enumerations are in reference/golden/classify.py (sections A–G), and the extra checks named "new" were run for this book with the same definitions.

D.12 Admissibility and the four-path theorem

Admissibility. If $P_k$ is a golden triad then $d$ has one voice in each class. Proof. The three notes of $P_k$ are on three different layers (D.8), and voice $i$ is on layer $k d_i$; since $k \in \{1, 2\}$ is invertible mod 3, the residues $d_i \bmod 3$ are all different. ∎

Four-path theorem. For each golden triad $G$ and $k \in \{1, 2\}$ there are exactly four $d$ with $X = G - kd$ on layer 0; all have three distinct pitch classes at both ends; the 288 resulting paths form 12 classes of 24 under semitone transposition and reversal. Proof: Chapter 27, Derive layer. In brief: the residue of each $d_i$ is forced; residue 0 allows only $d_i = 0$ and residues 1 and 2 allow two values each; distinctness follows because golden intervals ($\pm 11$, $\pm 14$ mod 36) cannot equal displacement differences of size at most 8; and no path is fixed by a non-trivial symmetry. Of the 288 paths, 240 have Western triads at both ends (one type ends on a suspended fourth) and 48 end on non-tertian sets (finite check: classify.py §A).

D.13 Never twice

Theorem. No uniform stepwise path from a layer-0 chord in three voices has both passing chords golden.

Proof. Let $P_1 = G$ have root $r$, so voice $i$ is on layer $\lambda(g_i) \equiv d_i$, and $P_2 = G + d$ puts it on layer $2d_i \equiv -\lambda(g_i)$. If $P_2$ is golden with root $r'$, matching layers leaves three role assignments, each forcing one of $d_1 - d_0 \equiv 11$, $d_0 - d_1 \equiv 22$, $d_1 - d_2 \equiv 22 \pmod{36}$ (details in Chapter 27). Since $|d_i - d_j| \le 4$, none holds. ∎

Finite check. classify.py §B finds 0 such paths among the 288. A new enumeration over every layer-0 voicing in three voices and in four voices (doublings allowed, transposed so the first voice is C), every $d \in \{-2,\dots,2\}^v$, and golden triads or golden sevenths at both passing positions, also finds 0.

In short: the layers of the three voices are permuted between $P_1$ and $P_2$, so the voices would have to change roles, and every role change requires two voices' displacements to differ by 11 or 22 sixth-steps mod 36, far more than the at most 4 available.

D.14 The partner theorem

Theorem. On the 240 Western-ended golden paths, the other passing chord is an equal-third triad: $T_{13}$ (bronze) in 168 paths and $T_{10}$ (silver) in 72; silver occurs exactly on the paths between augmented and diminished triads. Proof. By the four-path theorem it suffices to inspect the 10 Western-ended types of the table in Chapter 27: seven have a T13 partner and three (Caug → Cg → Cs → Cdim, Caug → ^Cg → vC♯s → C♯dim, Caug → vC♯g → ^C♯s → Ddim) a silver one. Each type has 24 paths. ∎ (Finite check: classify.py §C.)

D.15 The quality theorem and the root rules

Theorem. Over all ordered pairs of distinct major or minor triads, a uniform stepwise three-voice path (any assignment of chord tones to voices) has a golden passing chord exactly for the 144 pairs of the same quality whose roots are 3, 4 or 5 semitones apart in either direction, and then for exactly one golden chord, placed as follows. Major pairs: on the root lying a third or a fourth above the other, shifted 0 (major third), ^ (minor third) or v (fourth), nearer that chord. Minor pairs: on the root lying a third or a fourth below the other, shifted 0 (fourth), v (major third) or vv (minor third), nearer that chord.

Status: established by finite check, in two independent ways. (1) From the 12-type table: the six rows with major or minor triads at both ends are C → A♭, C → A, C♯ → A♭ (major; roots a major third, minor third, fourth apart) and Cm → Fm, C♯m → Em, C♯m → Fm (minor; fourth, minor third, major third). Each row is an orbit of 24 ordered pairs, giving 144, and the golden roots in the rows (Cg, ^Cg, vC♯g; Cg, ^Cg, vC♯g at position 1) are the root rules for those six relations; reversal moves the chord to position 2, keeping it "nearer" the same triad. Because every golden path appears in the table, no other pair has one. (2) A direct enumeration of all $24 \cdot 23 = 552$ ordered pairs of major and minor triads, all voice assignments, all $d$ and both positions, found exactly 144 pairs with a golden passing chord, all of the same quality with roots 3, 4 or 5 semitones apart, each with exactly one golden chord (new check; it agrees with classify.py §E on the 68 pairs listed there).

Golden sevenths (finite check). With four voices, 96 uniform paths join Western chords through a golden seventh, in 4 types up to transposition (classify.py §D).

D.16 The asymmetry theorem

Theorem. No uniform stepwise path in three voices between two different golden triads has a single-layer passing chord. In particular no path golden → Western → golden exists.

Proof (new). Let $G$ have root $r$ and let $P_k = G + kd$ be single-layer, on layer $L$. Then $\lambda(g_i) + k d_i \equiv L$ for every voice, so the $d_i$ have three different residues mod 3. The end chord $G' = G + 3d$ keeps every voice on its layer. If $G'$ is a golden triad with root $r'$, its root, third and fifth lie on layers $r'$, $r'+2$, $r'+1$, and since layers are preserved, either (a) $r' \equiv r$ and each voice keeps its role, which gives $3d_0 = 3d_1 = 3d_2 \pmod{36}$, hence $d_0 = d_1 = d_2$; or (b) $r' \equiv r+1$: the fifth voice becomes the root, the root voice the third and the third voice the fifth, which gives $3(d_0 - d_2) \equiv 33$ and $3(d_1 - d_2) \equiv 33$, so $d_0 = d_1 = d_2 - 1$; or (c) $r' \equiv r + 2$: the third voice becomes the root and the fifth voice the third, giving $d_2 = d_1$. In every case two of the $d_i$ are equal, contradicting the three different residues. ∎

Finite check. classify.py §G finds 0 paths golden → Western → golden. A new enumeration also finds 0 single-layer passing chords between distinct golden triads, and 0 Western passing chords on four-voice uniform paths between golden sevenths (and golden triads with a doubled voice).

D.17 System 2: layer patterns and the half-step theorem

Schedules. On a natural path a half step is 1 + 1 + 1, and a whole step is any $(a, b, c)$ with entries in $\{0, 1, 2, 3\}$ summing to 6 (ten schedules). The layer pair at $(P_1, P_2)$ of a rising whole step is $(a \bmod 3, (a+b) \bmod 3)$, which takes every value except $(1, 2)$: that pair needs $a = 1$ and $a+b \in \{2, 5\}$, but $c = 6 - a - b \le 3$ forces $a + b \ge 3$, so $b = 4$, impossible. A falling whole step misses $(2, 1)$.

Half-step theorem. If every voice moves $+3$ (or every voice $-3$), $P_k = X \pm k$ is $X$ planed, single-layer, not golden. Lemma. If two voices of different pitch class both move $+3$ (or both $-3$), neither passing chord is a golden triad; it can be a golden seventh only if the two voices are 3 (equivalently 33) sixth-steps apart. Proofs: Chapter 28, Derive layer.

Finite check (new). For every ordered pair of triads with qualities in {major, minor, diminished, augmented} and roots a semitone apart, all three-voice assignments (doublings allowed), stepwise displacements and System 2 schedules were enumerated. No pair of major or minor triads has a golden passing chord; some pairs involving a diminished or augmented triad do (for example Bdim → C through vCg).

Z36

D.19 Subgroups and layers

$\mathbb{Z}_{36}$ is cyclic, so it has exactly one subgroup of each order $d$ dividing 36, namely $\langle 36/d \rangle$. The orders are 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 9, 12, 18, 36. The subgroup $\langle 3 \rangle$ of order 12 is 12-ET; its three cosets $3\mathbb{Z}_{36}$, $1 + 3\mathbb{Z}_{36}$, $2 + 3\mathbb{Z}_{36}$ are the three layers, and $\lambda$ is the quotient map $\mathbb{Z}_{36} \to \mathbb{Z}_{36}/\langle 3\rangle \cong \mathbb{Z}_3$. (Appendix A reviews groups and cosets.)

  • $M_{13}\{0, 1, 2\} = \{0, 13, 26\}$: the cluster becomes the bronze triad.
  • In $\mathbb{Z}_{12}$, $M_7\{0, 1, 2\} = \{0, 7, 14\} = \{0, 2, 7\}$.
  • $11^2 = 121 \equiv 13$, $11^3 \equiv 13 \cdot 11 = 143 \equiv 35 = -1$, so $M_{11}^2 = M_{13}$, $M_{11}^3$ is the inversion $p \mapsto -p$, and $M_{11}$ has order 6. $13^2 = 169 \equiv 25$ and $13^3 \equiv 1$: $M_{13}$ has order 3. (New observations, checked by computation.)
  • Because $11 \cdot 3 = 33 \equiv -3$, $M_{11}$ acts on the 12-ET layer as inversion: it sends C major $\{0, 12, 21\}$ to $\{0, 24, 15\}$ = F minor. Because $13 \cdot 3 = 39 \equiv 3$, $M_{13}$ fixes every 12-ET pitch class. On layers, $\lambda(11p) = 2\lambda(p)$ (swapping layers 1 and 2) and $\lambda(13p) = \lambda(p)$. (New observations.)
  • Since $11 \cdot 13 \equiv -1$, $M_{11}$ sends bronze to the inverted cluster: $M_{11}\{0, 13, 26\} = \{0, 35, 34\}$.

D.21 The golden chain is a 36-tone row

The golden sixth is 25 sixth-steps, and 25 is a unit, so $k \mapsto 25k$ $(k = 0, \dots, 35)$ is a bijection of $\mathbb{Z}_{36}$: the golden chain visits every pitch class once, a 36-tone row (Chapter 33). It begins 0, 25, 14, 3, 28, 17, 6, 31, 20, 9, 34, 23, 12, that is C, ^A♭, vF, C♯, ^A, vF♯, D, ^B♭, vG, E♭, ^B, vA♭, E. Since $25 \equiv -11$, the chain of golden sixths is the chain of golden thirds read backwards, and the golden circle $G_r \to G_{r+11}$ visits all 36 golden triads because $\gcd(11, 36) = 1$.
