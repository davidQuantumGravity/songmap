Chapter 13 described Western melody with a few ideas: contour, steps and leaps, motifs and their transformations, phrases that breathe and close (Chapter 13). Chapter 30 carried them into golden7 and golden13 (Chapter 30), and Chapter 36 laid out the whole family of golden scales, from the golden triad spread out as a scale to the golden decatonic and golden13 (Chapter 36). This chapter is a melodist's handbook to that family. Its first half asks what each scale gives a melody: which steps, which leaps, which chords a line can outline, which notes lean. Its second half lets the scale change while the melody goes on: melodies that hold on to the notes two scales share, melodies that modulate one note at a time, a melody that starts on three notes and unfolds into thirteen, and melodies that cross between golden keys and C major. Every count, intersection and interval below was computed by enumeration (reference/golden/melody.py prints every table; GOLDEN_BLENDING.md §16 summarizes them). The recipes and the melodies are proposals, and statements about how they sound are hypotheses for the ear to test.

The palette: what each golden scale gives a melody

Start with one tune and six scales. The tune is written only as scale degrees (it climbs by skips to the sixth note of the scale, falls back, and closes with a turn below the tonic), and it is played in golden3, golden4, the golden pentatonic, golden7, the decatonic and golden13, always over the golden triad Cg. In golden3 the tune can only leap: its three notes are the triad itself, so it sounds like a bugle call. golden4 adds one note and with it one tight semitone. The golden pentatonic has no semitone at all and sounds open, almost like a folk pentatonic stretched a little. golden7 limps: tight semitone, wide step, tight semitone. The decatonic walks more evenly, and golden13 creeps, every step a semitone or less.

The same contour survives in every version, as Chapter 13 predicts for any order-preserving change of scale. What changes is the gait, and with it what the melody can do: lean on a semitone, stride across a golden third, or spell a golden chord.

A few remarks the table cannot make.

  • Leaning tones. The semitone pairs are where a melody can lean, the way the Western leading tone leans to the tonic. Every scale from golden7 up has a semitone on both sides of C: B below (a golden apotome, 99.27 c) and C♯ above. golden3, golden4 and the pentatonic have no leaning tone at C at all; their melodies close by leap or by whole tone.
  • Clusters. From golden7 up, a semitone step keeps the 36-ET layer and every other step changes it, so each scale falls into semitone clusters, one per layer (Chapter 30): golden7 into {B C C♯}, {vE vF}, {^G ^A♭}; the 35 decatonic into {B C C♯ D}, {vE vF vF♯}, {^G ^A♭ ^A}; golden13 into runs of five, four and four. Stepwise lines lean inside a cluster and stride between clusters.
  • Chords to outline. A scale of $n$ consecutive chain notes holds $n - 2$ golden triads and $n - 3$ golden sevenths. A melody in golden7 can spell five golden triads, in the decatonic eight, in golden13 eleven. The pentatonic holds none, which makes it the most interesting case (next section).
  • The hybrids and silver13 are not chain windows, so they have more step sizes. Their sixth-step "steps" (C–^C, vA–A, 33 c) sound as inflections of one note rather than as motion, so in a melody they work as bends or ornaments, not as steps. The golden Ionian is the one hybrid with a familiar gait: it is C major with E and G bent (Chapter 38), and we will meet it again as the natural road home.
Western mirror. The diatonic gives a melody two step sizes (90 and 204 c in Pythagorean tuning) and the pentatonic two (204 and 294 c); the leaps that matter are the third, the fourth and the fifth (Chapter 13). In the golden palette the leaps that matter are the golden third, the golden fourth and the golden fifth, and the step sizes of the scales from the pentatonic up run from 267.64 c (pentatonic, golden7) down to 69.10 c (golden13).

Theorem (the $n - k$ rule). Let $W = \{25j \bmod 36 : \ell \le j \le \ell + n - 1\}$ be a window of $n$ chain notes. For $1 \le k \le n - 1$ the pairs of notes $k$ links apart are $(j, j + k)$ with $\ell \le j \le \ell + n - 1 - k$: exactly $n - k$ of them, and each spans the pitch-class interval $1200\{k\log_2\varphi\}$ cents (36-ET: $25k \bmod 36$ sixth-steps). For $n \le 18$ different $k$ give different interval classes, so the interval-class vector of $W$ is $(n-1, n-2, \dots, 1)$ placed on the classes of $k = 1, 2, \dots, n-1$. (Theorem; the vectors of golden3, golden4, golden7, 35, 53, golden10 and golden13, and the pentatonic's below, were also computed directly and agree.)

Corollary (no fourths). The 36-ET fourth and fifth (15 and 21 sixth-steps) occur at chain distance $k$ with $25k \equiv \pm 15 \pmod{36}$, i.e. $k \equiv \pm 15$. A window contains them only if $n \ge 16$. In exact tuning chain distance 15 is $1200\{15\log_2\varphi\} = 496.35$ c. Semitones (chain 3) need $n \ge 4$, whole tones (chain 6) $n \ge 7$, minor thirds (chain 9) $n \ge 10$ and major thirds (chain 12) $n \ge 13$.

The pentatonic. The golden pentatonic is a window of five golden fifths (chain positions $-2, 0, 2, 4, 6$), so its pairs are at chain distances 2, 4, 6, 8, occurring 4, 3, 2 and 1 times: golden fifths or fourths (466.18/733.82 c), 267.64 c, whole tones (198.54 c) and 535.28 c (computed).

Melody in the golden pentatonic

The golden pentatonic C D vF ^G ^A is a chain of five golden fifths: ^G, C, vF, ^A, D, each a golden fifth below the last. Like the black keys, it has no semitone. Unlike the black keys, it has no golden third either, and so it contains no golden chord. A melody in it floats. The first example walks a melody along the chain and lets the harmony walk with it: Cg, vFg, ^Ag, Dg and back. Each chord shares exactly one note with the next, and on the way out the melody keeps that note across each change.

The second example puts the golden pentatonic next to its Western relative. A tune in C D E G A over C, Am and G is followed by the same scale degrees in C D vF ^G ^A over golden chords. The contour is the same; the third, fourth and fifth degrees have moved up (E to vF, G to ^G, A to ^A), so the step D–E has widened and the step E–G has narrowed, both to the same 268 c.

Steps, leaps and the reversed proportions

The golden pentatonic has two step sizes: the whole tone (198.54 c, twice) and golden7's wide step (267.64 c, three times; within 0.77 c of 7/6). The Western major pentatonic has the opposite proportions: three whole tones (203.91 c) and two minor thirds (294.13 c). Chapter 36 found this reversal; for a melodist it means that a stepwise golden-pentatonic line moves mostly by its wide step, the small minor third, where a Western one moves mostly by whole tones.

The five modes

The golden pentatonic has five modes, one on each note. Moved to C, they are windows of five golden fifths containing C. We name them as in Chapter 36 by the number of chain notes below and above the tonic ($b|a$), counting on the chain of golden fifths. Each mode lowers one note of the brighter one by a golden limma (69.10 c, 2 sixth-steps), exactly as the decatonic modes do, because the two scales have the same chroma (Derive).

The pentatonic contains no golden triad: a golden triad spans three consecutive chain notes, and the pentatonic takes only every other one. What it does contain is four golden fifths, and each golden fifth belongs to exactly one golden triad:

Writing pentatonic melodies

The Western pentatonic's habits transfer, with the golden fifth as pillar (proposals):

  • Frame with the golden fifth. Open with C–^G or end a phrase on ^G over Cg; the golden fifth is the pentatonic's strongest interval and names its chord.
  • Fill leaps with steps. A leap of a golden fourth (C up to vF, or vF up to ^A) is followed by steps back; the gap-fill habit of Chapter 13 applies unchanged.
  • Use contours that stay open. Without a leaning tone at C, closure comes from the harmony (Cg, then C major), from the metre, and from a descent through D or ^A to C. Ending on D or ^A over Dg or ^Ag sounds open: these are the notes farthest from C on the chain (six and four links).
  • Let the melody choose the chord by its golden fifth, and move the harmony along the chain (one golden fifth per change) so that the melody's held note is the common tone.
Western mirror. A Western pentatonic melody can outline a chord directly: C D E G A contains the triads C and Am, and C–E–G is a pentatonic arpeggio. A golden pentatonic melody cannot; it can only outline golden fifths. In that respect it behaves like a melody in fourths and fifths, the open outline of much folk and early music, whose thirds are supplied by the accompaniment. The contour ideas of Chapter 13 (arch, gap-fill) and its motif ideas (Chapter 13) apply unchanged.

Proposition (chroma of the pentatonic). Moving the window $\{-2, 0, 2, 4, 6\}$ one golden fifth up the chain (to $\{0, \dots, 8\}$) removes chain position $-2$ (^G) and adds 8 (vG). These are 10 links apart, and $25 \cdot 10 \equiv -2 \pmod{36}$: the note falls a golden limma, exactly $1200(7 - 10\log_2\varphi) = 69.10$ c. The decatonic's chroma is the same chain distance (Chapter 36), so the pentatonic and the decatonic share their chroma. In exact tuning the steps are chain distance 4 inverted, $1200(3 - 4\log_2\varphi) = 267.64$ c, and chain 6, $1200(6\log_2\varphi - 4) = 198.54$ c; their difference is the limma. (Theorem.)

Proposition (no golden triad, one triad per golden fifth). A golden triad is a chain window $\{r, r-1, r-2\}$ (root, third, fifth), which contains positions of both parities; the pentatonic contains only even positions. A golden fifth $\{j, j-2\}$ lies in a golden triad only as root and fifth, of the triad rooted at $j$, whose third $j - 1$ is odd: a note of pentatonic B. (Theorem.)

Proposition (transposition). Transposing pentatonic A by $s$ sixth-steps shifts its chain positions by $c(s)$ (Chapter 34). It keeps $0$ notes if $c(s)$ is odd and $\max(0, 5 - |c(s)|/2)$ if $c(s)$ is even: 4 for a golden fifth, 3 for two, and none for a golden third, a semitone or a limma. (Checked for all 36 $s$.)

Melody in golden7 and the decatonic

golden7 has four leaning pairs: B–C, C–C♯, vE–vF and ^G–^A♭. The first example uses all four. ^A♭ falls to ^G, C rises to C♯ and the first phrase stops there, a semitone above the tonic; then vF falls to vE, and the second phrase ends with B rising to C. The two endings are meant as golden7 versions of a half close and a full close, and on harmonic tones the final release from Cg to C major is the deepest rest of all.

golden7 reads the same upside down around C, so any golden7 phrase has an exact mirror image that stays in the scale. The second example plays a two-bar phrase and then its mirror. Even the chords mirror: Cg becomes vFg and C♯g becomes vEg.

The decatonic walks more smoothly. The third example takes one small motif, a golden triad with the scale note just above its root, and plays it on four successive chain steps without changing a single interval; then the scale forces a change, and the line turns into a chromatic turn, D C B C, a Western figure the decatonic contains intact.

Two step sizes, three clusters

Both scales have two step sizes. In golden7 they are far apart (99.27 and 267.64 c), so every step is either a lean or a stride; in the decatonic they are close (99.27 and 168.37 c), so a stepwise line flows. Their generic intervals, counted in scale steps, each take two sizes:

Tendency tones

The leaning pairs are the obvious candidates for tendency tones. In golden7 every golden triad has a chord tone with a semitone neighbour in the scale (Cg: C with B and C♯, vE with vF, ^G with ^A♭), and the decatonic adds a second neighbour inside each cluster (C♯–D, vF–vF♯, ^A♭–^A). Two leans approach the tonic: B rising (the golden apotome below C) and C♯ falling (the apotome above). Which of them closes more strongly, and whether either works as a leading tone in the Western sense, is open (Chapter 31). The examples treat B → C as the full close and a stop on C♯ as a half close, a proposal.

Contour: arches and waves

The arch of Chapter 13 works directly. In golden7 the natural arch climbs by a golden triad (C vE ^G), touches the leaning neighbour above (^A♭) and returns. A wave suits golden7's gait especially well: lean, stride, lean, stride, so that the line alternates a semitone with a wide step and swings between two clusters. The first example's opening, C vE vF ^G ^A♭ ^G, is such a wave. In the decatonic, waves are gentler. Its two interleaved pentatonics alternate note by note, so every step crosses from one to the other and every skip of one note stays inside one.

Inversion is exact, and it mirrors the harmony

golden7 and golden13 are symmetric about C (Chapter 30), so an inverted motif stays in the scale with every interval exactly mirrored. The harmony can mirror too: inversion about C maps each golden triad to a golden triad (Cg ↔ vFg, C♯g ↔ vEg, ^A♭g fixed), because a golden triad's two golden thirds turn into two golden thirds. The second example uses this. Its phrase C C♯ vE ^G vE | vF ^A♭ vF C♯ over Cg, C♯g becomes C B ^A♭ vF ^A♭ | ^G vE ^G B over vFg, vEg.

The decatonic is not symmetric about C. Inverted about C, the 35 decatonic becomes the 53 decatonic: D, vF♯ and ^A turn into B♭, ^F♯ and vE♭. So a 35 motif played upside down about C lands in the other reading of the scale, a ready-made colour change. The 35 decatonic is symmetric only about the point halfway between C and C♯.

Sequences up the chain

A real sequence moves a motif by a golden sixth up (or, the same pitch classes, a golden third down): one chain step. The scale moved one chain step keeps all its notes but one (next sections), so the sequence stays in the scale as long as the motif avoids the note that would leave. In the 35 decatonic (chain −3 to 6) a motif on the golden triad rooted at chain $r$ with the scale note above its root uses chain $r - 2$ to $r + 3$, so the real sequence works for the roots C, ^A♭, vF and C♯ (chain 0 to 3). At ^Ag it would need ^B♭, which is not in 35, and the melody must bend into a tonal sequence, keeping the shape but changing one interval. The third example does exactly this.

Western-like motifs in the decatonic

A Western cell can appear exactly only inside one layer cluster, where all intervals are 12-ET intervals. golden7's cluster B C C♯ holds only the chromatic neighbour figures. The decatonic's cluster B C C♯ D holds four consecutive semitones: the turn D C B C, the double neighbour C C♯ C B C, the chromatic fill B C C♯ D, and even a transposed BACH motif (C B D C♯). It does not hold do-re-mi: a major third needs 12 chain links. Of all the three-note cell shapes of C major within a fourth, exactly the two made of a semitone and a whole tone fit into the 35 decatonic: semitone then whole tone (B C D, as in C major) and whole tone then semitone (A B C in C major, found in 35 as B C♯ D) (computed).

Western mirror. Chapter 13's transformations (inversion, retrograde, augmentation) all work in golden7 and the decatonic. The golden differences: inversion about the tonic is exact in golden7 (in C major it is exact only about D), it maps 35 to 53 in the decatonic, and chain sequences change exactly one note of the scale, as a Western sequence by fifths does.

Proposition (degree chords of the decatonic). A decatonic step changes the chain position by $+3$ (small step, 3 sixth-steps: $25 \cdot 3 \equiv 3$) or by $-7$ (large step: $25 \cdot (-7) \equiv 5$). Three consecutive steps reach chain $-1$, a golden third, exactly when they contain one large and two small steps ($-7 + 3 + 3 = -1$). The cyclic step word 5 3 3 5 3 3 5 3 3 3 has ten windows of three; nine contain exactly one 5, and the window 3 3 3 gives chain $+9$, the minor third. A degree chord 1-4-7 is golden when both of its three-step spans are golden thirds: 8 of the 10. (Theorem.)

Proposition (inversion of windows). The chain window $[\ell, h]$ is mapped to itself by $j \mapsto \ell + h - j$, which in pitch is $x \mapsto 25(\ell + h) - x$. For golden7 ($\ell + h = 0$) this is inversion about C; for 35 ($[-3, 6]$, $\ell + h = 3$) it is $x \mapsto 3 - x$, the axis between C and C♯; inversion about C maps $[-3, 6]$ to $[-6, 3]$, which is 53. Inversion about C maps the golden triad on chain $r$, $\{r, r-1, r-2\}$, to $\{-r, -r+1, -r+2\}$, the golden triad on chain $-r + 2$: Cg (0) ↔ vFg (2), vEg ($-1$) ↔ C♯g (3), ^A♭g (1) fixed. (Theorem.)

Proposition (real chain sequences). $T_{25}$ maps the window $[\ell, h]$ to $[\ell + 1, h + 1]$: the note at chain $\ell$ leaves and $h + 1$ enters, $h + 1 - \ell = n$ links away. A motif on chain positions $[p, q]$ stays in the scale for $m$ real steps iff $q + m \le h$. For 35 and the motif $\{r-2, \dots, r+3\}$ this is $r + 3 \le 6$. (Theorem.)

Common-tone subsets: holding one scale inside another

A melody does not have to use every note of its scale, and a melody that avoids a few notes can belong to two scales at once. In the next example the melody uses only six notes, C vE vF ^G ^A♭ B. They are the six notes C golden7 shares with golden7 a golden third higher, on vE. Under the first half the chords belong to C golden7 (C♯g uses the C♯ that only C golden7 has); under the second half they belong to vE golden7 (^Gg uses its vE♭). The melody does not change scale; the key changes underneath it.

Scales inside scales

On a fixed tonic the golden scales nest along the chain: golden3 ⊂ golden4 ⊂ golden7 ⊂ the decatonic ⊂ golden13, and pentatonic A ⊂ 35 ⊂ golden13. Two facts about the decatonic's two readings are worth memorizing:

  • 35 ∩ 53 = golden7 and 35 ∪ 53 = golden13 (on the same tonic).
  • golden13 minus 35 is the golden triad vE♭g (vE♭ ^F♯ B♭); golden13 minus 53 is Dg (D vF♯ ^A).

So a golden7 melody lies inside both decatonics, and a melody in either decatonic lies inside golden13; a 35 melody that avoids D, vF♯ and ^A lies in golden7, and so in 53 as well.

Pivot cells, ranked

Call a short melodic cell that lies in two scales a pivot cell, the melodic counterpart of a pivot chord. Ranked by how much they hold (size first, then a golden triad, then golden thirds and fifths, then semitones; computed over the golden scales on C and on their neighbours, and C major, C minor, A minor, F and G major):

  1. Chain neighbours share $n - 1$ notes and $n - 3$ golden triads (golden13 on C and on vE: 12 notes, 10 triads). Almost any melody is a pivot.
  2. A whole golden triad. A golden triad lies in $n - 2$ keys of golden-$n$: Cg is in 5 golden7 keys (on C, vE, ^G, ^A♭ and B), 8 decatonic keys and 11 golden13 keys. A melody that arpeggiates Cg can be heard in any of them. Between golden13 keys a limma apart the shared part is exactly one golden triad (next section).
  3. A golden fifth or golden third, shared, for instance, by pentatonic A and golden7 (C vF ^G).
  4. The semitone pair B–C, the whole of golden7 ∩ C major, and the three-note cell B C D of 35 ∩ C major.
  5. A single note.
Western mirror. Western modulation by a pivot chord uses a chord diatonic in both keys (Chapter 11); a pivot cell is its melodic counterpart. C major and G major share six notes, so almost any C major phrase that avoids F is a pivot into G. The golden keys a chain step apart are in the same position (golden7: six common notes), while a golden key and a Western key share at most two to four notes, so the melodic hinge between the worlds is always a short cell.

Theorem (window overlap). The golden-$n$ windows on C and on $s$ are chain windows $[\ell, \ell + n - 1]$ and $[\ell + c, \ell + c + n - 1]$ with $c = c(s) \equiv 13s$ folded to $[-18, 18)$; their intersection has $\max(0, n - |c|)$ chain positions. For $n \le 18$ the two windows together span at most $n + 18 \le 36$ consecutive positions, which are distinct pitch classes, so the windows cannot also meet the other way round the 36-note chain and the count is exact (for larger $n$ it can fail). (Theorem; checked for $n$ = 3, 4, 7, 10, 13 and all 36 $s$. The pentatonic rule is in the previous section.)

Proposition (keys containing a golden triad). The triad on chain $r$ occupies $[r - 2, r]$; a window of $n$ contains it for $n - 2$ placements. With $n = 7$ and $r = 0$ the windows start at $-6, \dots, -2$: the golden7 keys centred on chain $-3$ to 1, i.e. B, ^G, vE, C, ^A♭. (Theorem.) No Western scale contains two notes of a golden triad, because the triad has one note on each layer (computed: 0 of the 216 major, minor, harmonic, melodic and pentatonic scales).

The Western bounds are Chapter 30's small-overlap theorem (golden7 at most 2, golden13 at most 3 with major and natural minor), extended by computation to 35 and 53 (3) and to harmonic and melodic minor, which can reach 4 with golden13 because they contain the cell semitone–whole tone–semitone (A♯ B C♯ D in B minor), which no major scale contains: golden13's layer-0 run B♭ B C C♯ D holds it.

Changing systems: melodic modulation and the growing scale

Now let the scale move. The first example walks golden7 up the chain: C golden7, then ^A♭ golden7, vF golden7 and C♯ golden7, then back. Each step changes exactly one note, and the melody plays the new note at once: ^A in place of B, vF♯ in place of ^G, D in place of vE.

The second example grows a scale. It begins on the three notes of the golden triad, adds ^A♭ (golden4), then B, vF and C♯ (golden7), then ^A, vF♯ and D (the decatonic), then vE♭, ^F♯ and B♭ (golden13), and releases into C major.

The third example alternates the decatonic's two readings on C: the same phrase in 35 and then in 53, where three notes are each a golden limma higher. It is the golden counterpart of playing a phrase in major and then in minor.

The last example changes golden13 by a golden limma. golden13 on C and golden13 on vC♯ share exactly three notes, and they form a golden triad, vE♭g. The melody walks to that triad in C, sings on it, crosses into the new key, and comes back through the same three notes.

The comma pump as a melody

golden13 is the extreme case. Thirteen chain steps take C golden13 to ^C golden13, raising one note by one sixth-step at each step, in chain order B♭, ^F♯, vE♭, B, ^G, vE, C, ^A♭, vF, C♯, ^A, vF♯, D (Chapter 34). As a melodic process (a proposal): give each step one bar, and let the melody touch the raised note in that bar, preferably as a held or repeated note so that the ear compares it with its memory of the old pitch. After thirteen bars the melody is in a key a sixth-step higher, without ever having moved more than one note at a time. A thirteen-step walk needs a longer piece than the short examples here; the apotome pump (three steps, C → ^A♭ → vF → C♯ golden13: B♭ → ^B♭, ^F♯ → vG, vE♭ → E♭) is a compact version.

Alternating scales

Two alternations stay on one tonic.

  • 35 ↔ 53. On C the two decatonic readings differ in three notes, each a golden limma apart: D, vF♯, ^A in 35 against vE♭, ^F♯, B♭ in 53. What they share is golden7. So a piece can switch between them while the harmony stays in golden7 (Cg, C♯g, vEg, vFg, ^A♭g), as the example does. The Western counterpart is the switch between C major and C minor: three notes, each a semitone apart (E A B against E♭ A♭ B♭), with the four notes C D F G in common. In both cases the change is three mode steps, each moving one note by the chroma.
  • Pentatonic A ↔ pentatonic B. The two halves of the decatonic share no note and are a golden third apart. A phrase in A answered in B (Chapter 36's double-pentatonic example) is a complete change of notes inside one scale, the golden counterpart of answering a white-key pentatonic phrase (C D E G A) with a black-key one.

The golden-third key cycle

Moving a golden key a golden third up is one chain step down, so it too changes one note: in golden7 C♯ → vE♭ (+5), in the decatonic D → vE♭ (+2), in golden13 D → vD (−1). Three golden thirds make 33 sixth-steps, a semitone below the start, and 36 of them visit every key. A melodic sequence that rises by golden thirds therefore stays almost entirely in common notes: in golden13 twelve of thirteen notes survive each step. Harmonized by the projections C → Cg → vEg → vE, it is the golden-third key cycle of Chapter 37 sung.

A limma key change that keeps a golden triad

Between golden13 keys a golden limma apart the common part is exactly a golden triad: C → vC♯ keeps vE♭g (vE♭ ^F♯ B♭), C → ^B keeps Dg (D vF♯ ^A). The ten notes that change are the 35 decatonic (upward) or the 53 decatonic (downward), each replaced by a note a sixth-step away (Chapter 36). For a melody this is the cleanest key change in the system: sing the kept triad, and the key can move underneath by a limma in either direction. In C the kept triad vE♭g lies at one edge of the scale (chain −6 to −4), four chain steps from Cg; in vC♯ golden13 it lies at the opposite edge, six chain steps from the new tonic vC♯g. The example reaches it from Cg through Bg, crosses to B♭g and vC♯g, and returns.

Western mirror. Western modulation to a neighbouring key changes one note by an apotome (C major → G major: F → F♯), and the pentatonic, diatonic and chromatic scales nest along the chain of fifths at the well-formed sizes 5, 7 and 12 (2, 3, 5, 7 and 12 in pure fifths; Chapter 6). The golden system has the same mechanics on a different chain: one note per step, by the chroma, and nesting at 3, 4, 7, 10 and 13. Its peculiarities are that the growing scale gains its first semitone already at four notes (the chain of fifths needs six, F to E), and that its smallest key change, the sixth-step, shares no note at all.

Proposition (chroma of a chain step). Moving a window of $n$ chain notes up one link removes position $\ell$ and adds $\ell + n$; the change is chain distance $n$, i.e. $25n \bmod 36$ sixth-steps (folded to the nearest representative) and $1200(n\log_2\varphi - m)$ cents, with $m$ the integer nearest to $n\log_2\varphi$: $n = 3$: $+99.27$; $n = 7$: $-168.37$; $n = 10$: $-69.10$; $n = 13$: $+30.17$; for the pentatonic (generator two links, window span 8) the change spans 10 links, $-69.10$. (Theorem; checked by the script.)

Proposition (growing scale). The window $[\ell, h]$ around the triad $[-2, 0]$ has two step sizes iff its size $n = h - \ell + 1$ is a well-formed size of the golden chain (2, 3, 4, 7, 10, 13, 23, 36), independent of where the window sits (the three-gap theorem, Chapter 6). It contains $n - 2$ golden triads. (Theorem; the step sizes in the table were computed from exact φ.)

Proposition (35 ↔ 53). 35 = $[-3, 6]$ and 53 = $[-6, 3]$ on C differ by a shift of three links: positions 4, 5, 6 (^A, vF♯, D) are replaced by $-6, -5, -4$ (B♭, ^F♯, vE♭), ten links lower each, and $25 \cdot (-10) \equiv 2$: each rises a golden limma. The intersection is $[-3, 3]$ = golden7, the union $[-6, 6]$ = golden13. (Theorem.)

Melodies that cross between golden and Western keys

golden7 and C major share two notes, B and C, exactly the two notes of the Western cadential step B → C. The first example uses that hinge. A Western call in C major ends D–B–C over G → C; a golden answer in golden7 ends C♯–B–C over vEg → Cg. Twice, and then the last word goes to C major, the global rest on any instrument with harmonic tones.

The second example bends a Western tune into gold. The tune is played first in C major over C, F, G and C. Then every chord is replaced by its golden projection, the golden triad reached by bending two of its notes a sixth-step each (Chapter 35), and every chord tone of the melody bends with its chord: under Cg, E and G become vE and ^G; under Fg, A and C become vA and ^C; under Gg, B and D become vB and ^D. The roots stay. At the end the tune goes home to C major.

The shared notes and how to harmonize them

The shared notes are few (previous section), so each one has to carry a lot. The two notes of golden7 ∩ C major can be harmonized in either world:

So the melodic cadence B → C has a Western harmonization (G → C, V → I), a golden7 one (vEg → Cg, one circle step) and a 53 one (Bg → Cg, every voice rising). vEg is the projection of Em and Cg the projection of C, so the golden cadence is the golden image of iii → I, not of V → I: the projection of G, Gg = G vB ^D, does not contain B at all. A melody that ends B → C can therefore close in either world, and a piece can choose at the last moment, or say both: a golden close followed by the Western one, as in the examples. The pair B–C lies in only two major keys (C and G) but in four golden7 keys (C, vE, ^G, B), so the hinge works in more golden keys than Western ones.

Golden inflection of a Western melody

The projection π sends a major triad to the golden triad on its root, keeping the root and bending the third down and the fifth up by a sixth-step each; a minor triad keeps its fifth and bends root down and third up (Chapter 35). Apply it chord by chord to a harmonized Western melody and bend each chord tone with its chord: that is projection inflection. The scale under each colour chord is C major with the chord's notes bent, and the computation gives a pleasant surprise: these scales are exactly the four golden inflections of C major that Chapter 38 lists on its frontier at (3, 1) and (2, 2), the frontier inflections that bend only two notes.

  • Bend chord tones on strong beats; leave passing tones Western, unless they collide with a bent tone (E against vE).
  • Keep roots: they carry the tune's identity (in the example C, F and G never move).
  • Bend a whole phrase rather than isolated notes, so that the ear hears a world, not a mistake.
  • End by unbending: the last golden chord moves to its Western preimage (Cg → C) and the melody's bent notes return (^G → G, vE → E). On harmonic instruments this is the strongest release the system has.

Call and response, and the way home

Chapter 30's call and response between the two worlds (Chapter 30) gains two refinements here. First, answer a Western call by the golden transplant (same degrees in golden7) or the golden inflection (same notes bent by π); the transplant changes every step, the inflection keeps the roots. Second, end on a shared note, B → C, so that the answer can hand the music back without a seam. And end the piece on C major: on physical instruments, which are harmonic, the Western triad is the global minimum of tension, and the golden triad, in the author's model, a delicate local rest one can dwell in before it (Chapter 26; measurement on harmonic tones does not show that local minimum).

Proposition (projection inflection). For a major triad $M_r$ of C major, $\pi(M_r) = G_r$ and the inflected scale is $(C\text{ major} \setminus M_r) \cup G_r$; for a minor triad $m_r$, $\pi(m_r) = G_{r-1}$ (Chapter 35). Since $\pi$ of the I–iii pair (C, Em) and of the IV–vi pair (F, Am) bend the same two scale notes each (E, G and C, A), the six diatonic consonant triads give only four inflected scales. Among all $3^7 = 2187$ inflections the frontier point (2, 2) is reached by exactly two scales and (3, 1) by six; the four projection scales are the two at (2, 2) and the two at (3, 1) that bend only two notes (the other four bend six), i.e. the frontier examples of Chapter 38. (Computed.)

Proposition (keys holding B and C). {B, C} is chain $\{-3, 0\}$, contained in $n - 3$ windows of size $n$: 4 golden7 keys (C, vE, ^G, B), 7 decatonic and 10 golden13 keys; in Western terms it is a semitone, contained only in C and G major among the 36 major keys. (Theorem; computed.)

Recipes and worked melodies

Four complete melodies close the chapter, one per world of the chapter. The first is a golden-pentatonic air; the second a golden7 period with a half close on C♯ and a full close B → C; the third a decatonic song that sequences along the chain and turns to the 53 reading for its cadence; the fourth travels from C major into golden7, grows into the decatonic and comes home through the golden Ionian.

Recipes

These are compositional proposals, distilled from the computations above.

  1. Choose the gait. Leaps and fanfares: golden3, golden4. Open, anhemitonic: the golden pentatonic. Lean and stride: golden7. Smooth steps with golden colour at the semitones: the decatonic. Near-chromatic lines with three seams: golden13.
  2. Choose the chords the line will outline. golden7: degrees 1-3-5. Decatonic: degrees 1-4-7. Pentatonic: golden fifths only, the third supplied by the harmony from the other pentatonic.
  3. Harmonize strong beats with a golden triad containing the melody note. A note at chain position $j$ lies in the triads rooted at $j$, $j + 1$ and $j + 2$ (Chapter 36); prefer the one closest on the chain to the previous chord (one chain step: two common notes; a golden fifth: one).
  4. Lean into the cadence: B → C over vEg → Cg, C♯ → C over C♯g → Cg, or (53) B → C over Bg → Cg. Use a stop on C♯, D or ^A for half closes.
  5. To modulate, pick a pivot cell (a golden triad, a common subset), sound the new key's new note early and on a strong beat, and keep the rest of the line on common notes. Chain steps change one note; golden13 limma steps keep a golden triad.
  6. To grow, add chain notes in chain order and rest at the well-formed stages (3, 4, 7, 10, 13). To shrink, remove them from the edges.
  7. To cross into a Western key, use B–C (golden7, 53) or B–C–D (35, golden13) as hinges, or bend a Western tune by the projection; to come home, unbend (Cg → C) and end on C major.

A harmonization algorithm. Given a melody in a golden chain scale $[\ell, h]$ and the strong-beat notes $x_1, \dots, x_m$ at chain positions $j_1, \dots, j_m$, choose roots $r_i \in \{j_i, j_i + 1, j_i + 2\} \cap [\ell + 2, h]$ minimizing $\sum_i d(r_{i-1}, r_i)$, where $d(r, r')$ is the voice-leading distance between golden triads $|r - r'|$ chain steps apart: $3|r - r'|$ for $|r - r'| \le 6$ (Chapter 36), then 15, 12, 9, 6 for 7 to 10 steps, the widest gap inside golden13 (computed). This is a shortest path in a layered graph with at most three nodes per layer, solved by dynamic programming in $O(m)$ steps, the same method Chapter 39 used for golden reharmonization (Chapter 39). For the pentatonic, where only golden fifths are available, restrict $r_i$ to $j_i$ (the note as root, its fifth at chain $j_i - 2$) or $j_i + 2$ (the note as fifth, the root at chain $j_i + 2$). Exercise 40.1. How many whole tones, minor thirds and semitones does the 53 decatonic contain? Use the $n - k$ rule, then name the minor third.

Answer

53 is a window of 10 chain notes ($[-6, 3]$). Whole tones are chain distance 6: $10 - 6 = 4$; minor thirds chain 9: $10 - 9 = 1$; semitones chain 3: $10 - 3 = 7$. The minor third joins chain $-6$ and $3$: B♭ and C♯, i.e. B♭ up to C♯ (300 c in 36-ET, 297.81 c exact).

Exercise 40.2. A melody in pentatonic A holds vF on a downbeat. Which golden triads contain vF and use a golden fifth of the pentatonic? Which of them is one golden fifth from Cg?

Answer

vF is chain 2. It is the root of vFg (fifth C, chain 0, in the pentatonic) and the fifth of ^Ag (root ^A, chain 4, in the pentatonic); it is also the third of C♯g, but then neither root nor fifth is in pentatonic A. vFg is one golden fifth (two chain steps) from Cg and shares C with it.

Exercise 40.3. Invert the 35 motif C C♯ D vE vF about C. In which scale does the result lie?

Answer

x → −x: C, B, B♭, ^A♭, ^G (0 → 0, 3 → 33, 6 → 30, 11 → 25, 14 → 22). B♭ is not in 35; the result lies in 53 (C C♯ vE♭ vE vF ^F♯ ^G ^A♭ B♭ B), as the inversion theorem predicts.

Exercise 40.4. golden7 on C moves two steps down the chain (to golden7 a golden fifth higher, on ^G). Which notes leave, which enter, and how many are common?

Answer

One step down (to vE): C♯ → vE♭; a second (to ^G): vF → ^F♯. Common: $7 - 2 = 5$, namely C vE ^G ^A♭ B. Each change is 5 sixth-steps up (168.37 c).

Exercise 40.5. Bend the C major phrase G A G E C (over C, with A a neighbour note) by the projection of its chord. Which notes change, and into which scale does the result fall?

Answer

π(C) = Cg bends the chord tones E → vE and G → ^G and keeps C. The neighbour A stays (it is not a chord tone): ^G A ^G vE C. All five notes lie in the golden Ionian C D vE F ^G A B, the inflected scale under Cg. Note that ^G–A is now 166.67 c (5 sixth-steps), narrower than the old whole tone.

Exercise 40.6 (listening). In the alternation example, the 35 and 53 phrases share their chords. On which notes do you hear the difference, and which version sounds brighter to you? Compare your answer with the brightness of the two modes (35 = 159, 53 = 165, Chapter 36).

Answer

On five notes of each phrase: the third note of bar 1 (D/vE♭), the second and last notes of bar 2 (vF♯/^F♯ and ^A/B♭), and the second and last notes of bar 3 (^A/B♭ and D/vE♭). By the brightness measure (the sum of the degrees) 53 is the brighter mode, every changed note a golden limma higher; whether it also sounds brighter is for the listener, and it is a hypothesis that brightness in this arithmetic sense tracks perceived brightness, as it does roughly for the diatonic modes.
