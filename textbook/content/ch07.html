Pythagorean tuning gives perfect fifths and a chain that never closes. That was enough for music built on fifths and fourths. Between the 14th and 16th centuries thirds moved to the center of Western music, and the Pythagorean third, 81/64, is not the simple 5/4 of the harmonic series. Temperament is the art of adjusting ("tempering") intervals slightly away from pure so that more of them can be good at once. This chapter follows the three great solutions, meantone, well temperament and equal temperament, and corrects a famous myth along the way: Bach did not invent equal temperament.

The syntonic comma and meantone

First, the problem. Listen to two major thirds over C. The first is the Pythagorean third, made from four pure fifths. The second is the pure third of the harmonic series. The Pythagorean third is bright and restless; it beats quickly. The pure third is calm. Then hear both inside a full major chord.

The solution widely used on keyboards in the 16th and 17th centuries was meantone: make every fifth a little narrow, just narrow enough that four of them land exactly on the pure third. The fifths now beat slightly, but the thirds are perfect. The price is paid elsewhere: one "fifth" in twelve becomes a wolf, and some thirds become unusable.

Four fifths up from C, less two octaves, give E at $81/64$ = 407.82 c. The pure major third is $5/4$ = 386.31 c. The gap between them,

$$\frac{81/64}{5/4} = \frac{81}{80} \approx 21.51\ \text{c},$$

is the syntonic comma, which Chapter 4 met in the ratio lattice. It is close to the Pythagorean comma (23.46 c) but a different interval: the Pythagorean comma compares fifths with octaves, the syntonic comma compares fifths with thirds. Their difference, 1.95 c, is called the schisma.

Quarter-comma meantone. Narrow each fifth by a quarter of the syntonic comma, $21.51/4 = 5.38$ c. Then four fifths are smaller by a whole syntonic comma, and the third C–E comes out pure. The fifth itself is $\sqrt[4]{5} \approx$ 696.58 c, because four of them must equal $5 = 4 \times 5/4$. The whole tone C–D is then exactly half of the pure third, 193.16 c: the "mean tone" that gives the system its name.

This is the usual twelve-note layout, the chain from E♭ to G♯. In it:

  • eight major thirds are pure (386.31 c), on C, D, E♭, E, F, G, A and B♭;
  • four "major thirds" are really diminished fourths of 427.37 c, on C♯ (C♯–F), F♯ (F♯–B♭), G♯ (G♯–C) and B (B–E♭), 41.06 c too wide;
  • eleven fifths are 696.58 c, and the twelfth, G♯–E♭, is a wolf of 737.64 c, 35.68 c too wide.

The semitones are reversed compared with Pythagorean tuning. The chromatic semitone C–C♯ is 76.05 c and the diatonic semitone C♯–D is 117.11 c, so in meantone D♭ is higher than C♯, by 41.06 c. A keyboard with one key for both must choose; that is why the meantone wolf exists, and why some historical instruments had split black keys, with separate D♯ and E♭ or G♯ and A♭.

The figure shows why the thirds cannot all be pure. Three major thirds stack to the octave in 12-ET (4 + 4 + 4 = 12 semitones). But three pure thirds make $125/64$, which is 1158.94 c, short of the octave by $128/125$ = 41.06 c, the diesis. In meantone, one third out of every such cycle of three absorbs that whole gap.

The meantone family. A meantone tuning is a chain of fifths of size $f = 701.955 - c \times 21.506$ cents, narrowed by a fraction $c$ of the syntonic comma. The major third is four fifths up less two octaves, $4f - 2400$; the minor third is three fifths down plus two octaves, $2400 - 3f$:

Quarter-comma makes major thirds pure, 1/3-comma makes minor thirds pure, and 2/7-comma makes both equally impure (3.07 c each).

Quarter-comma exactly. $f = 1200\log_2 5^{1/4} = 300\log_2 5 = 696.58$ c, so $4f - 2400 = 1200\log_2(5/4)$: the third is exactly pure. The tone is $2f - 1200 = 600\log_2(5/4)$, exactly half the third.

Where the comma goes. Eleven fifths narrowed by 5.38 c each lose $11 \times 5.377 = 59.14$ c. But twelve fifths must total seven octaves, which requires a total shortfall of only one Pythagorean comma, 23.46 c (Chapter 5). So the twelfth interval must be wider than pure by $59.14 - 23.46 = 35.68$ c: the wolf, $701.96 + 35.68 = 737.64$ c.

The diesis. In quarter-comma meantone the diatonic semitone minus the chromatic semitone is $117.11 - 76.05 = 41.06$ c, exactly the diesis $128/125 = 2/(5/4)^3$, since three pure thirds and the diesis make an octave.

Meantone in history. Meantone tunings were described by Renaissance theorists, among them Gioseffo Zarlino in the 16th century, and quarter-comma meantone became a widely used keyboard tuning in the 16th and 17th centuries. Pietro Aron's description of 1523 is usually read as quarter-comma meantone.

Well temperament

Meantone is beautiful in the keys near C and unusable far from it. A composer who wants to write in F♯ major needs A♯, D♯ and E♯, and a meantone keyboard tuned from E♭ to G♯ simply does not have them: it offers B♭, E♭ and F, which are the wrong notes. The solution of the late 17th and 18th centuries was the well temperament: share the comma unevenly, so that every key is usable, but the near keys are sweeter and the far keys more sharply colored.

Listen to the same cadence in C major and in F♯ major, first in quarter-comma meantone and then in one famous well temperament, Werckmeister III.

Andreas Werckmeister published several temperaments in Musicalische Temperatur (1691). The one usually called Werckmeister III narrows four fifths, C–G, G–D, D–A and B–F♯, by a quarter of the Pythagorean comma each ($23.46/4 = 5.87$ c, giving 696.09 c), and leaves the other eight fifths pure. Four quarters make one whole comma, so the circle closes exactly, with no wolf.

The keys with at most two sharps or flats have the best thirds, better than 12-ET's 400 c; the keys far around the circle have Pythagorean thirds. Nothing is as bad as meantone's diminished fourths or wolf. Every key is playable, and every key has its own color.

Werckmeister's was one of many such schemes; later well temperaments by Johann Philipp Kirnberger and Francesco Antonio Vallotti, among others, distributed the comma in other ways. Eighteenth-century writers often attributed distinct characters to particular keys. How much of that came from the tuning, and how much from instruments, conventions and associations, is debated.

The closure condition. Any 12-note tuning with no wolf is a circle of twelve fifths $f_1, \dots, f_{12}$ with $\sum f_i = 8400$ c, that is, total tempering $\sum (701.955 - f_i) = 23.46$ c, one Pythagorean comma. A well temperament chooses the $f_i$ unevenly but keeps every $f_i$ acceptable.

Thirds from fifths. A major third spans four consecutive fifths of the circle (C–G–D–A–E), so in any closed 12-note tuning

$$\text{M3 on } X = 407.82 - (\text{tempering of the four fifths from } X).$$

In Werckmeister III each fifth is tempered by 0 or $\kappa/4 = 5.865$ c, so the third on $X$ is $407.82 - 5.865\,t$, where $t \in \{0, 1, 2, 3\}$ is the number of tempered fifths among the four. The four fifths from C are C–G, G–D, D–A (tempered) and A–E (pure): $t = 3$, giving 390.22 c. From F♯: F♯–C♯, C♯–G♯, G♯–E♭, E♭–B♭, all pure: $t = 0$, the Pythagorean 407.82 c. This is why the thirds come in exactly four sizes, a quarter comma apart.

The golden system has its own well-tempered cycle. The Well-Tempered Golden Clavier passes through every key of 36-ET: 36 tonics, each heard as major, minor, golden13 and silver13, at a much faster harmonic rhythm than Bach's (Chapter 24). Unlike Bach's tuning, 36-ET is an equal temperament, so its keys do not differ in color; the name honours the complete tour of keys.

Equal temperament

The simplest way to share the comma is to share it equally: make all twelve fifths exactly alike and all twelve semitones exactly alike. That is equal temperament, the tuning of almost every piano today. Every key sounds the same, and modulation anywhere is free. The cost is that no interval except the octave is pure. The fifths are almost pure, but the thirds are noticeably wide: listen for the shimmer in the equal-tempered chord, between two pure ones.

In twelve-tone equal temperament (12-ET) the octave is divided into twelve equal semitones, each a frequency ratio of $2^{1/12} \approx 1.059463$, exactly 100 c. Each fifth is 700 c, narrowed from pure by a twelfth of the Pythagorean comma, 1.96 c. The limma and the apotome both become 100 c, so C♯ and D♭ are the same key and the same pitch: 12-ET makes enharmonic notes equal, and the chain of fifths closes into the circle of fifths (Chapter 8).

Who computed equal temperament. The idea of equal semitones is old, and fretted instruments used nearly equal semitones in practice; Vincenzo Galilei, in the 16th century, recommended placing lute frets by the ratio 18:17 (98.95 c, close to an equal semitone). The mathematics of exactly equal semitones, each the twelfth root of 2, was worked out independently by the Chinese prince Zhu Zaiyu (1584) and the Flemish mathematician Simon Stevin (c. 1605). Marin Mersenne popularized it in Europe in Harmonie universelle (1636). Equal temperament became the normal keyboard tuning only gradually, over the 18th and 19th centuries. Bach did not invent it.

Definition. $n$-tone equal temperament ($n$-ET) has the notes $2^{k/n}$, $k \in \mathbb{Z}$; each step is $1200/n$ cents. 12-ET is $n = 12$.

Errors. 12-ET approximates a just ratio $r$ by the nearest multiple of 100 c; the error is $100\,\mathrm{round}(12\log_2 r) - 1200\log_2 r$. For the fifth, $700 - 701.955 = -1.955$ c, which is $-\kappa/12$.

Why the third is wide. The 12-ET third is four 12-ET fifths minus two octaves: $4 \times 700 - 2400 = 400$. The Pythagorean third is $4 \times 701.955 - 2400 = 407.82$. So the 12-ET third is the Pythagorean third narrowed by $4 \times 1.955 = 7.82$ c, still 13.69 c above pure. Every tuning built from a chain of equal fifths faces this trade: to bring the thirds down, the fifths must be narrowed more, as in meantone, and then the chain no longer closes after twelve fifths.

Irrationality. $2^{k/12}$ is irrational unless $12 \mid k$, so no 12-ET interval other than octaves is a whole-number ratio. (If $2^{k/12} = p/q$ in lowest terms, then $p^{12} = 2^k q^{12}$, forcing $q = 1$ and $p$ a power of 2, so $12 \mid k$.)

Bach's Well-Tempered Clavier

In 1722 Johann Sebastian Bach completed a collection of 24 preludes and fugues, one in every major and every minor key. Twenty years later, in 1742, he assembled a second set of 24. The title, Das wohltemperirte Clavier, means "the well-tempered keyboard". It showed that a well-tempered keyboard could play in all 24 keys.

The example below is not by Bach. It is a short arpeggiated texture, in the manner of a keyboard prelude, that travels to keys far apart on the circle of fifths: C, E, A♭ and back to C. On a meantone keyboard the E and A♭ sections would be badly out of tune. In a well temperament each would sound in tune with its own color; in the equal temperament used here they sound alike.

The facts, stated carefully:

  • Book I is dated 1722 and Book II 1742. Each contains 24 pairs of a prelude and a fugue, one pair for each major and minor key, arranged in rising chromatic order: C major, C minor, C♯ major, C♯ minor, D major, and so on up to B minor.
  • "Clavier" meant keyboard instruments in general: harpsichord, clavichord and organ.
  • "Well-tempered" is not "equal-tempered". The title refers to a good tempering that makes all keys usable. The word does not by itself rule out equal temperament, but the good temperaments of Bach's day were mostly unequal well temperaments, and exactly which tuning Bach used is not documented. Several modern reconstructions have been proposed, some based on readings of a decorative drawing on the title page of Book I; they remain debated.
  • Bach did not invent equal temperament. Zhu Zaiyu and Simon Stevin had computed it more than a century earlier, and Mersenne had published it in 1636.

Bach was not the first composer to write a cycle of pieces through many keys, but the Well-Tempered Clavier became the model for such cycles. Its musical point is the one the tuning history makes possible: once every key is usable, a composer can modulate anywhere, and the circle of keys becomes a space to travel in (Chapter 8).

This project's Well-Tempered Golden Clavier takes the WTC as its model in 36-ET: 72 major and minor keys plus 36 golden13 and 36 silver13 scales. Where Bach needed a tuning in which 24 keys were usable, 36-ET has 36 tonics, and each of them carries the golden scales as well as the Western ones (Chapter 24, Chapter 31).

Counting keys. With 12 tonics and two modes there are $12 \times 2 = 24$ keys. In a closed 12-note well temperament, transposing a key changes its tuning, so in principle up to 12 different "colors" of major key exist. In Werckmeister III the twelve major thirds take only four sizes (last section), and the three keys on F♯, C♯ and G♯ have the Pythagorean third. In 12-ET all twelve major keys are exact transpositions of each other: one color.

Other equal divisions: 19, 31, 53

Twelve is not the only way to divide the octave equally. Other divisions keep the convenience of equal steps but choose a different compromise. Listen to a C major triad in four equal temperaments and then in pure tuning. In 12-ET the third is wide. In 19-ET it is a little narrow. In 31-ET it is almost pure. In 53-ET both the third and the fifth are very close to pure.

  • 31-ET is almost exactly quarter-comma meantone (fifths 696.77 against 696.58 c), with nearly pure major thirds and a close 7/4. Christiaan Huygens wrote about the 31-fold division in the 17th century, and in the 20th century Adriaan Fokker built an organ with 31 keys per octave.
  • 53-ET is almost exactly Pythagorean (fifth 0.07 c narrow), the cycle of 53 fifths from Chapter 5, and its major third is also good. The Chinese theorist Jing Fang (1st century BCE) is credited with noticing that 53 fifths nearly make 31 octaves, and Nicholas Mercator studied the 53-fold division in the 17th century. Turkish classical music theory describes intervals in units of a 53rd of an octave.

The price is the number of notes. Nineteen, thirty-one or fifty-three keys per octave are hard to build and to play, which is one reason 12 prevailed.

The golden system makes the same kind of choice. 13-ET closes the golden chain at the golden comma, as 12-ET closes the chain of fifths, but its golden sixth (830.77 c) is 2.32 c flat and it does not contain 12-ET. 36-ET closes the golden chain at the smaller 36-comma: its golden sixth is 25 steps, within 0.24 c. And because $36 = 3 \times 12$, it contains 12-ET exactly. No other small tuning does both. That is why the golden system lives in 36-ET (Chapter 24, 13-ET and 36-ET).

Which divisions approximate which tuning. An $n$-ET has a fifth of $m$ steps, a fraction $m/n$ of the octave. It approximates a chain tuning well when $m/n$ is close to the chain's generator. By the theorem of Chapter 6, the sizes $n$ on the generator's Stern–Brocot path are exactly the well-formed sizes of that chain. Computed directly:

  • Pythagorean fifth ($0.584963$ octave): well-formed sizes 2, 3, 5, 7, 12, 17, 29, 41, 53, …
  • Quarter-comma meantone fifth ($0.580482$ octave): 2, 3, 5, 7, 12, 19, 31, 50, …
  • 1/3-comma meantone fifth ($0.578989$ octave): 2, 3, 5, 7, 12, 19, 31, 50, 69, …

The two families share 2, 3, 5, 7, 12 and then part ways: 7/12 has the children 11/19 and 10/17. Meantone fifths (smaller than 7/12 of an octave) go left to 11/19, then on to 18/31; the Pythagorean fifth (larger) goes right to 10/17 and on to 53. So 19 and 31 are to meantone what 17, 41 and 53 are to Pythagorean tuning.

Nearness. $|1200 \times 11/19 - 694.79| = 0.05$ c, and $|1200 \times 18/31 - 696.58| = 0.20$ c.

1. Show that in quarter-comma meantone the minor third is 5.38 c narrower than 6/5.

Answer

The minor third is three fifths down and two octaves up: $2400 - 3 \times 696.58 = 310.26$ c. Pure 6/5 is 315.64 c, so the meantone minor third is 5.38 c narrow, the same amount as each fifth. (Exactly: the pure minor third is a pure fifth minus a pure major third, and meantone keeps the third pure while narrowing the fifth by a quarter comma.)

2. In Werckmeister III, how large is the major third on G, and why?

Answer

It spans the fifths G–D, D–A, A–E and E–B. Two of them (G–D and D–A) are tempered by 5.865 c, so the third is $407.82 - 2 \times 5.865 = 396.09$ c.

3. (Listening.) In the equal-temperament example, the third beats at about 10.4 Hz. Which partials are beating, and why does the pure third not beat?

Answer

The fifth partial of C4 (5 × 261.63 = 1308.13 Hz) and the fourth partial of E4 (4 × 329.63 = 1318.51 Hz) are 10.38 Hz apart. In the pure third E = 5/4 × C, so 4E = 5C exactly and these partials coincide.

4. True or false: Bach's Well-Tempered Clavier was written to show off equal temperament, which Bach invented.

Answer

False on both counts. Equal temperament was computed by Zhu Zaiyu (1584) and Simon Stevin (c. 1605) and popularized by Mersenne (1636). "Well-tempered" means a good temperament in which every key is usable, which in Bach's time usually meant an unequal well temperament; Bach's exact tuning is not documented.

5. In 31-ET, how many steps is the chromatic semitone C–C♯, and how many the diatonic semitone C♯–D?

Answer

The fifth is 18 steps, so C♯ (seven fifths up, four octaves down) is $7 \times 18 - 4 \times 31 = 2$ steps, and D (two fifths up, one octave down) is $36 - 31 = 5$ steps. So C–C♯ is 2 steps (77.42 c) and C♯–D is 3 steps (116.13 c): a meantone pattern, with D♭ (3 steps) above C♯.
