Chapters 34 and 35 changed keys, by golden intervals (Chapter 34) and through golden pivot chords (Chapter 35), and Chapter 36 built the golden scales and their modes. This chapter collects five textures that blend golden and Western harmony inside a phrase: planing, pedals and drones, approach chords, sequences and passing keys. Each has a Western ancestor, from Debussy's parallel chords and the bagpipe drone to the Neapolitan sixth and Coltrane's major-third cycle, and each is tested here by the same standard as the rest of the book. Some results are theorems (golden planing is exactly three-key polytonality; there is no golden augmented sixth), some are measurements in the roughness model, and several are negative: the golden escalator does not work as a pair of passing keys, no golden7 scale lies between C major and ^C major, and canon at the golden third rarely sounds golden. The framing is the book's throughout (Chapter 26, Chapter 31): every example is played on harmonic tones, C major is the deepest rest there is, and arriving on it from golden harmony is release. The golden triad is, in the author's model and listening experience, a delicate local minimum; the roughness models on harmonic tones do not show that minimum, and this chapter says so wherever it measures. Embellishing chords (golden appoggiaturas, suspensions, neighbour and escape chords) are the subject of Chapter 39.

Golden planing and three-key polytonality

Debussy's planing doubles a melody with a chord that moves in parallel with it, like a line drawn with a broad brush (Chapter 22). Do the same with a golden triad: put a golden triad on every note of a C major scale, each the same exact shape. Then listen to the result in a second way. Take a short tune in C major, play it alone, add a copy of it a golden third higher, and then a second copy a golden fifth higher. Each copy, heard alone, is an ordinary major-key melody. Together they sound a golden triad on every note.

The texture is simple and the explanation is clean, which is why the technique ranks well among the blending methods of this Part: golden planing is polytonality, music in several keys at once, and it is polytonality of an extreme kind, because the three keys cannot share a single note.

Golden planing harmonizes every note $x$ of a line with the golden triad $\{x, x+11, x+22\}$ (in sixth-steps), the melody in the root. On the C major scale:

Western planing and Western polytonality

Western chromatic planing works the same way, with a difference that is the whole point. Parallel major triads on a C major line put the third in E major and the fifth in G major. But those keys overlap heavily: C and E major share 3 notes, C and G major 6, E and G major 4, and the three lines together use 11 of the 12 pitch classes (computed). The ear hears one blurred key, not three. In 36-ET the keys are on three different layers and cannot overlap at all, so golden planing is three-key polytonality in the strictest sense.

Polytonality has a Western history of its own. The bitonal Petrushka chord, C major against F♯ major (Chapter 22), is its best-known emblem, and Darius Milhaud (1892–1974) made simultaneous keys a hallmark of his style from around 1920. Western bitonality often chooses keys far apart on the circle of fifths, such as Petrushka's tritone, so that they share few notes. Golden planing gets three keys that share none, and the price of their combination is predictable: every vertical is the same chord shape.

Melody on top

With the melody in the top voice (as the fifth of each chord), the roots are $x - 22$: vFg, vGg, vAg, vB♭g, vCg, vDg, vEg under C, D, E, F, G, A, B. The two lower lines are then in vF major and ^A♭ major, the keys a golden fifth and a golden third below C (computed). Any 12-ET scale can be planed this way: the rule is a theorem about layers, not about the major scale.

Roughness (measurement, close position on each degree from C4, harmonic partials 1–8). All golden triads are the same shape, so in the model their roughness changes only with register: Cg 1.498, Dg 1.402, Eg 1.321, Fg 1.284, Gg 1.220, Ag 1.166, Bg 1.120, falling as the chords rise. The diatonic triads on the same roots measure C 1.319, Dm 1.238, Em 1.149, F 1.090, G 1.020, Am 0.977 and Bdim 1.201: the golden version is rougher on every degree except the seventh. A golden-planed passage is a band of constant colour, as Debussy's chromatic planing is, and like it, it ends best on a Western chord: in the example the final Cg opens onto C major, the release.

Canon at the golden third: a negative result. Polytonality in time instead of at once does not work. Let a follower repeat a stepwise C major leader 11 sixth-steps higher, one note behind. The vertical interval is then 11 minus the leader's last step: 17, 14, 11, 8 or 5 sixth-steps for steps of −6, −3, 0, +3 and +6. Only a repeated note gives a golden third (11); a falling semitone gives 14, the octave complement of the golden fifth; the whole steps give 5 and 17, which are no golden intervals. Canon (Chapter 15) needs the follower's notes to fit against the leader's later notes, and the golden third is not an interval a stepwise line can echo. Status: theorem; ranked last of the techniques in GOLDEN_BLENDING.md.

Exercise 37.1. Plane a C major line with golden triads, but with the melody in the third of each chord. In which keys are the root line and the fifth line?

Answer

With $x$ the third, the root is $x - 11 \equiv x + 25$ and the fifth is $x + 11$. So the root line is in ^A♭ major (25 sixth-steps above C) and the fifth line in vE major. The three keys ^A♭, C and vE major again lie on three layers (1, 0, 2) and share no note.

Theorem (golden planing is three-key polytonality). Let $K \subset \mathbb{Z}_{36}$ be any scale on the 12-ET layer (all its pitch classes $\equiv 0 \pmod 3$). Planing a line in $K$ in golden triads with the melody in the root puts the three voices in $K$, $K + 11$ and $K + 22$. These sets are pairwise disjoint and $|K \cup (K+11) \cup (K+22)| = 3|K|$; for C major, 21. Proof. The voice $x + 11$ is in $K + 11$ and $x + 22$ in $K + 22$ by definition. Since $11 \equiv 2$ and $22 \equiv 1 \pmod 3$, the three sets lie in the three different cosets of $3\mathbb{Z}_{36}$ (the layers), so they cannot meet. ∎ This is the layer theorem (Chapter 25) read horizontally: a golden triad has one note on each layer, so a stream of golden triads is three streams, one per layer.

The Western comparison. For a 12-ET scale $K$, $|K \cap (K + t)|$ is the number of times the interval $t$ occurs in $K$, read from its interval vector (Chapter 20). The diatonic vector is $\langle 2, 5, 4, 3, 6, 1\rangle$, so C major meets E major ($t = 4$ semitones) in 3 notes, G major ($t = 7$, interval class 5) in 6, and E meets G major ($t = 3$) in 4. In $\mathbb{Z}_{36}$ the transpositions by 11 and 22 are not multiples of 3, and every such intersection is empty.

Exact tuning. In exact golden tuning the three lines lie a golden third $2/\varphi$ (366.91 c) and a golden fifth $4/\varphi^2$ (733.82 c) above the melody; 36-ET renders them as 366.67 c and 733.33 c. The keys vE and ^G major are then not 12-ET major scales shifted by sixth-steps but the C major scale multiplied by $2/\varphi$ and $4/\varphi^2$, still disjoint from C major and from each other.

Golden pedals, Western drones

A pedal point is a note held, usually in the bass, while the chords above it change (Chapter 12); a drone is a pedal that never stops, like the bagpipe's. Hold a low C and ask which golden chords can stand on it. There are exactly three, and they are neighbours on the golden circle, so the harmony above the pedal can walk back and forth along the circle while the bass stays put.

The opposite experiment holds a golden chord as a drone and plays Western chords above it. Listen to the same simple progression twice, first over a C major drone, then over a Cg drone, and to the return of the C major drone at the end.

Every note lies in three golden triads

C is the root of Cg = C vE ^G, the third of ^A♭g = ^A♭ C vE and the fifth of vFg = vF ^A♭ C. No other golden triad contains it. On the golden circle (Chapter 31) these three chords sit at positions 0, −1 and −2: consecutive. So a C pedal supports exactly one golden progression, the walk Cg ↔ ^A♭g ↔ vFg, with one voice moving a semitone at each step (vE ↔ vF and ^G ↔ ^A♭). All three chords belong to C golden7, and together they use the five notes vF ^A♭ C vE ^G, the middle five of the golden chain (chain positions −2 to +2).

In Western harmony a held C lies in six consonant triads, C, F and A♭ major and C, F and A minor, so a Western pedal is far more permissive. A Western open-fifth drone C–G, the bagpipe's, supports no golden triad at all: C and G are both on the 12-ET layer, and a golden triad has only one note there. A golden dyad drone is more selective in the other direction: C–vE lies in two golden triads (Cg and ^A♭g), C–^G only in Cg (computed).

Exercise 37.2. Which three golden triads contain vE, and where are they on the golden circle relative to Cg?

Answer

$r \in \{11, 11 - 11, 11 - 22\} = \{11, 0, 25\}$: vEg (vE is its root), Cg (third) and ^A♭g = ^A♭ C vE (fifth). Their circle positions are +1, 0 and −1: again three consecutive chords, now centred on Cg.

Theorem (three golden chords per pedal). Every pitch class $x$ lies in exactly three golden triads, $G_x$, $G_{x-11}$ and $G_{x-22}$, which are consecutive on the golden circle. Proof. $x \in G_r = \{r, r+11, r+22\}$ iff $r \in \{x, x - 11, x - 22\}$, three distinct roots. The circle position of $G_r$ is $p(r) \equiv 23r$ (since $11 \cdot 23 = 253 \equiv 1 \pmod{36}$), and $p(r - 11) = p(r) - 253 \equiv p(r) - 1$. So the three positions are $p$, $p-1$, $p-2$. ∎

Corollary (dyads). Two notes lie together in a golden triad only if they differ by $\pm 11$ or $\pm 22$. A difference of $\pm 11$ is either root–third or third–fifth, so the dyad lies in two golden triads; a difference of $\pm 22$ is only root–fifth, so it lies in one. Two notes on the same layer differ by a multiple of 3 and lie in none: a Western drone of any interval excludes every golden triad.

The model. Sethares roughness with harmonic partials $k = 1, \dots, 8$ at amplitudes $0.88^{k-1}$, summed over all pairs of partials of all sounding notes (reference/golden/blending.py §16, the model of explore.py). The golden-spectrum comparison uses partials $2^m\varphi^n < 9$ ($m, n < 4$) with the same amplitude law. The numbers depend on register and voicing.

Approach chords: the golden Neapolitan

Western harmony approaches its goals by semitones. The Neapolitan sixth puts D♭ above the bass and lets it fall to the leading tone; augmented sixth chords push two voices a semitone outward onto an octave (Chapter 18). A golden triad can be approached by semitones too, but only from other golden triads. Listen first to the Western slide D♭ → C, every voice falling a semitone, and then to its golden counterpart, C♯g → Cg. Then hear the same chord from below, Bg → Cg, every voice rising, and vEg → Cg, where only one voice moves.

No Western chord leads into a golden triad by step

A semitone is three sixth-steps and a whole tone six, so a voice that moves by semitones or whole tones never changes layer. A Western chord lies on one layer, and a golden triad needs one note on each. So no Western chord resolves into a golden triad by semitone or whole-tone motion: at least two of its voices must bend by sixth-steps, and the cheapest such move costs 2, the golden projection of Chapter 35 (C → Cg: E falls and G rises one sixth-step).

Among all chords that move into Cg with every voice falling a semitone, rising a semitone or holding (26 motion patterns), exactly six are golden triads and none is Western; the other 20 are not triads of any of the seven named types (computed):

The golden Neapolitan

C♯g deserves the name golden Neapolitan. Like the Western Neapolitan, a major triad on D♭, the lowered second degree, it is built on the note a semitone above the tonic, and it is diatonic in its key: C♯ is the second degree of C golden7 (C C♯ vE vF ^G ^A♭ B), and C♯g is the triad on it. Its resolution is the purest semitone descent there is: C♯ → C, vF → vE, ^A♭ → ^G. In exact golden tuning each of the three falls is a golden apotome, $\varphi^3/4$ = 99.27 c, because each voice moves three places down the golden chain. The analogy has a limit worth stating. In classical practice the Neapolitan is a predominant: it usually stands in first inversion and moves to the dominant (D♭/F → G7 → C). The direct slide D♭ → C with every voice falling is rarer there and more typical of Phrygian and other modal contexts, and it is that slide which the golden Neapolitan imitates. Inside golden7 the triad a semitone above the tonic is also three circle steps from it, the far end of the scale's falling side (Chapter 31), so C♯g → Cg is the longest move into the tonic within the key (9 sixth-steps), yet no voice moves more than a semitone.

Golden leading-tone chords

Two chords rise into Cg. vEg contains B, the scale's one leading tone, and resolves by that single rising semitone with two common tones: the leading-tone exchange in reverse, and the candidate for a dominant-like role in golden7 that Chapter 31 proposes as a hypothesis. Bg = B vE♭ ^F♯ is the mirror of the golden Neapolitan, every voice rising a semitone; it is not in C golden7 (vE♭ and ^F♯ are foreign), so it acts as a chromatic, applied leading-tone triad. The phrase of the audio example walks the falling side of golden7 (Cg – ^A♭g – vFg – C♯g), resolves the golden Neapolitan, touches vEg, and only then opens onto C major, the deepest arrival. Which of these approaches the ear hears as most cadential is a question for listening tests, not a theorem.

There is no golden augmented sixth

An augmented sixth chord works because it contains an interval just short of an octave, A♭ up to F♯ (10 semitones), whose two notes move a semitone outward each, in contrary motion, onto the octave G–G. Its golden counterpart would need an interval that two voices can open to an octave, or close to a unison, by equal contrary steps. In 36-ET those intervals are 34, 32 and 30 sixth-steps (opening by a sixth-step, a third-step or a semitone in each voice) and 2, 4 and 6 (closing). The intervals of a golden triad or a golden seventh are 3, 11, 14, 22, 25 and 33 sixth-steps. None is on the list, so no golden triad or seventh contains a golden augmented sixth (theorem). Golden approach chords resolve by parallel motion or by one or two voices, never by the outward wedge that makes the augmented sixth so directional.

Golden sequences: giant steps and the golden-third cycle

John Coltrane's "Giant Steps" (recorded 1959) moves through three keys a major third apart, B, G and E♭, which divide the octave into three equal parts. Its root motion is a cycle of major thirds, and major triads a major third apart are joined by very smooth voice leading: one common tone, and two voices moving a semitone (Chapter 18). Play it on C, C – A♭ – E – C, and put one strict passing chord into each move. The passing chords come out golden, all three, and they too divide something into three equal parts: the golden circle.

A second cycle moves the key by golden thirds: C major, vE major, ^G major, B major. Each move goes through two golden chords, and every voice moves in one direction only. After three moves the music is back on the 12-ET layer, a semitone below where it began.

Golden giant steps

C → A♭ and A♭ → E and E → C are pairs of same-quality triads a major third apart. By the quality theorem (Chapter 27) each has exactly one golden chord on its strict path, the golden triad on the upper root: C – Cg – ^E♭T13/C – A♭, A♭ – A♭g – ^BT13/A♭ – E, E – Eg – ^GT13/E – C, with the bronze partner as second passing chord (Chapter 27). With one passing chord per move (a single ~ in SongMap), the sequence is C – Cg – A♭ – A♭g – E – Eg – C. In each move one voice holds, one falls a sixth-step and one rises a sixth-step into the golden chord, then each moving voice continues by two sixth-steps:

On the golden circle the three golden chords sit at positions 0, 12 and −12: twelve circle steps apart, so they divide the 36-step circle into three equal arcs, as Coltrane's three keys divide the octave. Twelve circle steps transpose a golden triad by 24 sixth-steps, which is exactly the major third down from C to A♭: the golden circle, read in steps of twelve, is the major-third cycle.

The golden-third key cycle

Move the tonic up a golden third (11 sixth-steps) again and again: C → vE → ^G → B → vE♭ → ^F♯ → B♭ → vD → ^F → A → vC♯ → ^E → A♭ → … Three steps make 33 sixth-steps, a semitone down, back on the 12-ET layer (C → B → B♭ → A …); 36 steps visit every major key of 36-ET once, because 11 generates $\mathbb{Z}_{36}$. Neighbouring keys share no note (they are on different layers), and the scales are 11 sixth-steps of voice leading apart, so as a key relation the golden third is remote. As a chord relation it is close: the tonic triads C and vE major are 7 sixth-steps apart, and that distance can be covered entirely through golden chords.

Bridge scales and passing keys

Western music often passes through a key on the way to another: a few bars that tonicize a chord, then move on (Chapter 11). Can a golden scale serve as such a passing key between two Western keys? The answer of this section is mostly negative, and the negative results are as useful as the positive one. The positive one is this: C major and ^C minor, keys on different layers with no note in common, can be bridged by two bars in vE golden7.

The golden escalator is not a pair of keys

The golden escalator (Chapter 28) carries C major to D major through two golden chords, C – vC♯g – ^C♯g – D. It is tempting to hear those chords as two brief keys, vC♯ golden7 and ^C♯ golden7. Every metric says no (exhaustive):

  • vC♯ golden7 (vC♯ vD ^E ^F A♭ A vC) shares one note with C major (A) and one with D major (A); ^C♯ golden7 shares one with each (F with C major, F♯ with D major). The two golden7 keys share nothing and are 14 sixth-steps of voice leading apart, while C and D major share five notes at distance 6.
  • No golden7, golden10 or silver13 key contains both escalator chords. Exactly one golden13 key does, vB golden13, because vC♯g and ^C♯g lie 10 circle steps apart (circle positions 10 and −16), the full span of the 11 golden triads of a golden13 key. vB golden13 shares only two notes with each Western key. For F → G the key is vE golden13.
  • The degree-by-degree averages of the two scales, (2·C + D)/3 = ^C D E ^F G A B and (C + 2·D)/3 = vC♯ D E vF♯ G A B, contain only two triads of any of the seven types: G and Em.

The escalator is a voice-leading phenomenon, two lines rising through the layers, not a key phenomenon. Use it as passing harmony, as Chapter 28 does, not as passing keys.

No golden scale between C and ^C major

The nearest key on another layer is a sixth-step lift away: C major to ^C major costs 7, every note rising one sixth-step (Chapter 34). No golden7 scale lies between them. The best golden7 detour, through ^C golden7 or E golden7, costs 15 (7 + 8 or 8 + 7), more than twice the direct route. The reason is a theorem: every scale on a smoothest voice leading from a key to its sixth-step lift uses only the notes of the two keys, two layers, while golden7 has notes on all three (3, 2 and 2 per layer).

The one bridge that stands out is vE golden7 = C vE♭ vE vF ^G ^A♭ B between C major and ^C minor. It shares C and B with C major and ^G and ^A♭ with ^C minor, and it sits symmetrically between them, 7 sixth-steps from each, against 10 for the direct move. Its five golden triads are ^Gg, vEg, Cg, ^A♭g and vFg, and two of them are projections of chords in the target key: Cg = π(^Cm) and ^A♭g = π(^A♭), the tonic and submediant of ^C minor. So the bridge key contains the pivot C → Cg → ^Cm of the silver-third keys (Chapter 35), and the example uses it: C → Cg enters, Cg → vEg and ^Gg → vEg establish the passing tonic, vEg → Cg → ^Cm leaves.

A Western-only alternative exists between C and ^C major: raise the notes of C major one at a time. Of the 5040 raising orders, the one that keeps the most consonant triads at every stage is D, F, A, C, E, G, B, never fewer than three (exhaustive); the intermediate scales contain no golden triad, since they use only two layers. It is a Western "comma pump" in seven stages (proposal), and it needs no golden chord at all.

How long must a passing key last?

No measurement exists yet. GOLDEN_BLENDING.md §7.4 proposes a testable criterion (proposal):

  1. Content. The passing key must sound the notes that distinguish it from both neighbours. For a golden13 key that can be a single note (neighbouring golden13 keys differ by one comma-shifted note, Chapter 34); a golden7 key needs a note five sixth-steps from its predecessor. vE golden7 brings five notes new to C major (vE♭ vE vF ^G ^A♭) and five new to ^C minor (C vE♭ vE vF B).
  2. Anchor. Its tonic golden triad on a strong beat, approached by a circle step (whether vEg → Cg, or here ^Gg → vEg, sounds dominant-like is itself a hypothesis, Chapter 31).
  3. Duration. A working figure of at least two chord changes or about 2–4 s, by analogy with Western tonicization, to be replaced by data. The example's passing key lasts two bars, four chord changes, about 5.5 s at 88 beats per minute.
  4. Test. Probe-tone ratings, the method of Krumhansl and Kessler (1982), after sequences in which the passing key lasts 1, 2, 4 and 8 beats, with probes from the old key, the passing key and the new key.

Exercise 37.5. Check that vE golden7 is the transposition of C golden7 by a golden third, and that it contains Cg. Which golden triad of C golden7 does it not contain?

Answer

C golden7 = {0, 3, 11, 14, 22, 25, 33}; adding 11 gives {11, 14, 22, 25, 33, 0, 8} = C vE♭ vE vF ^G ^A♭ B. It contains Cg = {0, 11, 22}. C golden7's triads are vEg, Cg, ^A♭g, vFg and C♯g; vE golden7 has all of them except C♯g (C♯ is missing) and gains ^Gg instead. The two keys share six notes, one golden-chain step apart.

Exercise 37.6 (listening). In the passing-key example, at which chord do you first hear that C major has been left, and at which that ^C minor has arrived? Compare your answers with the anchor and content conditions of the proposal.

Answer

There is no single right answer; this is the kind of judgement the proposed probe-tone test would measure. By the content condition, C major is contradicted from the first Cg (vE and ^G replace E and G), and ^C minor is first sounded by ^Cm (its ^C and ^E♭ are foreign to vE golden7) and confirmed by ^Fm – ^G.

Theorem (layer betweenness, Chapter 34). If $X$ lies on one layer and $Y = X + 1$, every geodesic hinge between $X$ and $Y$ is a subset of $X \cup Y$. Consequence. A golden7 scale has notes on three layers, so it is never a hinge between C major and ^C major; the exhaustive minimum of the detour is 15. The same argument shows that no golden, silver or bronze triad lies on a smoothest voice leading between a triad and its sixth-step lift.

The escalator keys. The circle positions of vC♯g and ^C♯g are $23 \cdot 2 = 46 \equiv 10$ and $23 \cdot 4 = 92 \equiv 20 \equiv -16$; the shorter arc between them has $36 - 26 = 10$ steps. A golden13 key contains 11 golden triads, consecutive on the circle, so it contains both chords only if the arc of 10 steps fits exactly: one key, vB golden13 (blending.py §10).

The bridge. Write $K_1$ for C major, $S$ for vE golden7 and $K_2$ for ^C minor. Then $d(K_1, S) = 7$, $d(S, K_2) = 7$ and $d(K_1, K_2) = 10$ (minimum over cyclic matchings of the sorted scales; computed). Among all 36 golden7 keys none does better than 14 for this pair (blending.py §9). Passing keys, as a technique, rank low in GOLDEN_BLENDING.md §12: the escalator result is negative, and the duration criterion awaits data.
