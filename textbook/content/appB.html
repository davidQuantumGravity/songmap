This appendix is the complete reference for the notation of the book: note names in 12-ET and 36-ET, interval and chord symbols, Roman numerals, scales, the symbols of the Derive layers, and the SongMap syntax in which every audio example is written. The tables of notes, chord qualities, scales, instruments and styles were generated from the same code that renders the examples (the naming rule of tools/textbook/build.py and the parsers in songmap/), so they agree with it by construction. Chapter 24, Chapter 25 and Chapter 36 explain the ideas; this page only lists.

Note names

12-ET

Notes are named with the letters A–G, sharps and flats, and an octave number that begins at each C: C4 is middle C (MIDI note 60) and A4 = 440 Hz (MIDI 69). A note with MIDI number $m$ has frequency $440\cdot 2^{(m-69)/12}$ Hz and pitch class $m \bmod 12$. Prose uses ♯ and ♭; code and SongMap files use # and b. In the Western chapters pitch classes are numbered in semitones from C = 0; in the golden chapters in sixth-steps, where a 12-ET pitch class $p$ becomes $3p$.

36-ET: sixth-steps, ups and downs

36-ET divides the octave into 36 sixth-steps of $1200/36 = 33.33$ c. A prefix ^ (up) raises a note by one sixth-step and v (down) lowers it by one; they combine with sharps and flats and with each other (^^C is two sixth-steps above C). The marks follow Kite Giedraitis' "ups and downs" notation for equal divisions of the octave. The standard spelling uses at most one arrow: write a pitch class as $p = 3q + r$ with $r \in \{0, 1, 2\}$; its name is the 12-ET name of $q$ if $r = 0$, ^ plus that name if $r = 1$, and v plus the name of $q + 1$ if $r = 2$. So step 2 is vC♯, not ^^C, and ^E (13) and vF (14) are different notes. Every step's layer is $r$: 0 for the twelve 12-ET notes, 1 for the ^ notes, 2 for the v notes.

With octaves. ^C4, vE♭4 and so on. The absolute position in sixth-steps is three times the MIDI number plus the ups minus the downs: middle C is 180, A4 is 207, vE4 is $3 \cdot 64 - 1 = 191$, and a note at step $s$ sounds at $440\cdot 2^{(s - 207)/36}$ Hz. In the piano-roll figures each row is one sixth-step and rows are shaded by layer.

Spelling chords. Chord tones are named by their own standard spelling, not by "the root's third with an arrow": ^Cg = ^C E vA♭ and ^Gg = ^G B vE♭. A staff for performers may prefer letter-faithful spellings that keep the letters of stacked thirds (^^D for the fifth of ^Gg); both name the same pitch (§38.1). In SongMap the same prefixes are typed in ASCII: ^c4, ve8, ^^g, vEb.

Intervals

Western intervals are named by size and quality: P (perfect), M (major), m (minor), A (augmented), d (diminished), with the number of letter steps: m3 is a minor third, P5 a perfect fifth, A4/d5 the tritone. In 36-ET an interval of $3q + r$ sixth-steps is named like a note: the 12-ET interval of $q$ semitones, with ^ if $r = 1$, or v on the next interval up if $r = 2$. So the golden third is vM3, the golden fifth ^P5 and the golden sixth ^m6 (§24.3).

Intervals are also given as ratios (3/2, 5/4, φ) and in cents ($1200\log_2$ of the ratio, two decimals), and chord shapes as step notation, the list of notes above the root: (0, 4, 7) in semitones for a major triad, (0, 12, 21) for the same triad in sixth-steps, (0, 11, 22) for the golden triad.

The golden chain

Chain note $j$ is $j$ golden sixths above C, folded into the octave: exactly $1200\{j\log_2\varphi\}$ cents ($\{\cdot\}$ the fractional part), and in 36-ET step $25j \bmod 36$. The chain distance of two notes is the difference of their $j$. Chain note $j = -1$ is the golden third vE, $j = -2$ the golden fifth ^G, $j = 3$ the golden apotome C♯, $j = 13$ the golden comma ^C (§23.3). The inverse rule is $j \equiv 13t \pmod{36}$ for pitch class $t$ (Appendix A).

Chord symbols

Anatomy of a symbol

A chord symbol has up to four parts, written without spaces: a root (a note name, possibly with ^ or v), a quality, an optional alteration list in parentheses, and an optional slash bass: $$\underbrace{\text{v}C}_{\text{root}}\ \underbrace{\text{g}}_{\text{quality}}\ \underbrace{\text{(…)}}_{\text{alterations}}\ \underbrace{/\text{G}}_{\text{bass}}$$ The arrows on the root move the whole chord; the rest of the chord is built on the moved root. Every Western symbol is valid in 36-ET, on any layer: ^C is C major a sixth-step higher (^C ^E ^G), vE♭m is vE♭ vG♭ vB♭ (standard spelling vE♭ vF♯ vB♭).

Slash chords

The note after / is the lowest note: C/E is C major in first inversion, vCg/G is the golden triad vC ^E♭ G with G in the bass (G is its fifth: the golden six-four). The bass may carry arrows too (vCg/vA♭, G/^F♯), and it need not belong to the chord: vFg/C is a golden triad "over a pedal" when C is not one of its notes.

Names produced by the passing engine

When SongMap fills a placeholder (§B.4) it names the chord it found, in the same notation: golden (vCg/G), equal-third (vE♭T13/G, Cs), Western on the 12-ET layer, planing (a Western chord on another layer, such as ^Em), or micro-altered, the nearest named chord with alterations (vF♯dim(v3), vA♭g7(v7)/^F♯). A set that is none of these is printed as its notes in braces, as in {C F F♯}. The engine also recognizes mmaj7 (minor-major seventh, steps 0, 9, 21, 33), so dictionary entries such as Fmmaj7(v3,^7) appear in §29.2; that quality is a label only, and the parser does not accept it as input.

Roman numerals

Chords in a key are numbered by the scale degree of their root: upper case for major, lower case for minor, ° for diminished, + for augmented, 7 for a seventh: I ii iii IV V vi vii° in major, i ii° ♭III iv v (or V) ♭VI ♭VII in minor. Chromatic chords take ♭ or ♯ on the numeral (♭II, ♭III, ♭VI, ♭VII); secondary dominants are written V/x ("five of x"); inversions take figured-bass superscripts (I⁶ = C/E, I⁶₄ = C/G; seventh chords 7, 6/5, 4/3, 4/2) (§9.2, §11.3).

Paths

A passing progression is written X → P₁ → P₂ → Y or with dashes, G – vE♭T13/G – vCg/G – C. "At 1/3" and "at 2/3" (P₁ and P₂) name the two positions of a three-step path (§27.2).

Scale degrees

In scale-degree notation degree 0 is the tonic and degrees count scale steps, wrapping into the next octave after the last note: in golden7, degree 0 is C, 1 C♯, 2 vE, 3 vF, 4 ^G, 5 ^A♭, 6 B and 7 the octave; in golden13 degree 13 is the octave. Negative degrees go down. Alterations ♯/♭ (#4, b7) and arrows (^3, v5) bend a degree by a semitone or a sixth-step. In the Western chapters degrees are also written 1 to 7 with carets in prose (3 → 2 → 1).

Every audio example is a SongMap song: a YAML text file (§36.1). This section lists the parts of the format the book uses, checked against the parser in songmap/parse/ and the engine in songmap/theory/ and songmap/arrange/. Tuning

tuning: 36edo allows sixth-step pitches anywhere: ^/v prefixes on notes, degrees, chord-relative tones, chord roots, bass notes and keys, the qualities g, g7, s and T13, alteration lists, golden keys and passing placeholders. In a 12edo song any pitch off the 12-ET grid is an error whose message names the fix, so a Western example cannot drift off the grid by a typo. The accent mark ^ is a suffix after the duration and never clashes with the up prefix: ^c4^ is C up, a quarter note, accented.

Harmony strings

A harmony string is a sequence of Chord:bars tokens separated by spaces; | may separate bars for readability. The length is in bars of the meter, and fractions are fine (G:0.5 is half a bar). A bare symbol lasts chord_bars bars (default 1). N.C. is silence. A section's harmony: is either such a string or the name of a progression defined under harmony: progressions:; it is laid along the section's bars and loops if it is shorter.

Passing placeholders. A token beginning with ~ asks the engine for the passing chord that the voice leading from the previous chord to the next picks out (§36.3):

Motifs and the note language

A motif is a string (letter notes, base octave 4) or a mapping with exactly one of notes:, deg: or rel:, plus optional octave: and key:: tune: {deg: "0/4 2 4 6 | 7/2 6/4 4 | 3/4 2 1 2 | 0/1", key: "C golden7", octave: 5}. Sections and parts

Each section is a mapping with name (a word) and bars, and optionally harmony, parts, like (copy an earlier section's parts and harmony), add, drop, dyn (a dynamic arc such as pp<mp), tempo and key. A part's value is a style name (organ: hold), a motif name or list (bell: [m1]), or a mapping with one source, style, motif/motifs, or notes/deg/rel, plus options: oct (octave shift), transpose, dyn, art, gain_db, gate, mute, role (which family of styles to use), and for voiced styles voices, range (MIDI numbers), rhythm, voice and vel.

The voiced styles serve every part. They voice the song's chords once, as four voice-led parts in the song's tuning (range D3–D5, MIDI 50–74, each chord placed to move least from the previous one), and play some of those voices: hold (all four, struck at each change and held), comp (all four on the rhythm x..x.x..x..x.x.. plus every change), pulse (every quarter note), top (the soprano, an octave up) and bassline (the lowest voice, an octave down) (§36.4). Passing chords that come from placeholders are the exception: the voiced styles play the passing engine's own four lines for them, moved by whole octaves toward the middle of the part's range, so a held or melodic part sounds exactly the computed sub-chromatic voice leading. Chord-relative styles belong to a role: arpeggios {style: gentle8, role: arp} and {style: roll16, role: arp}, bass figures {style: roots, role: bass} (half-note roots) and {style: root5, role: bass} (root and fifth; a golden chord gives its golden fifth). The role is needed when the part has another name, as in low: {style: roots, role: bass}.

Mix and master

mix: {part: {gain_db: -6, pan: 0.1, send: {hall: 0.3}}} sets a part's level in decibels, its stereo position and its send to a reverb return; the return hall is defined by default. The book's examples use master: clean: the mix is scaled so its highest peak is at −1 dBFS, with no equalization, compression, limiting or saturation (§37.4).
