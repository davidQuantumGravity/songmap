Instruments as spectra

A flute and a clarinet can play the same note at the same loudness and still sound nothing alike. The difference is timbre, the colour of a sound. Chapter 2 showed that a steady musical tone is a sum of sine waves, its partials, and that timbre depends largely on their strengths. Listen to one note, A3, played five times. Only the recipe of partials changes.

The pure sine is colourless. Strong harmonics make the tone bright and buzzy. Taking away the even harmonics makes it hollow, rather like a clarinet in its low register. Weakening the upper harmonics makes it soft and round. The last tone has partials at golden ratios instead of whole-number multiples. It still has a pitch, but its colour is metallic, and it will matter later in the chapter.

Many instruments do not have harmonic partials at all. Struck bars, drum heads and bells vibrate in modes whose frequencies are not whole-number multiples of the lowest one.

The harmonic tone has a clear pitch. The bar's pitch is clear but its colour is glassy. The drum head's pitch is vague, and the bell sounds as if it contained its own minor chord.

Sustained tones are harmonic. A bowed string, a wind instrument or a singing voice produces a sound that repeats itself (it is periodic) as long as it is held steady. A periodic sound, by Fourier's theorem (Chapter 2), contains only partials at whole-number multiples of its fundamental. So these instruments differ not in which partials they have but in how strong each one is, and in how the sound starts, changes and stops. Some typical features:

InstrumentSpectral character
Flute (high register)close to a sine: few, weak upper partials
Clarinet (low register)odd harmonics much stronger than even ones, because its cylindrical bore acts like a pipe closed at one end
Oboe, trumpet, bowed stringsmany strong harmonics; brass grows brighter as it gets louder
Voiceharmonic partials shaped by formants, resonances of the vocal tract that stay roughly fixed in frequency while the pitch changes: they are what distinguish the vowels
Pianonearly harmonic, but the stiff strings stretch the upper partials slightly sharp

Struck and plucked objects can be inharmonic. A bar, a plate or a membrane is not a string: its modes of vibration have frequency ratios set by its shape and stiffness. An ideal uniform bar free at both ends has modes at 1 : 2.756 : 5.404 : 8.933. An ideal circular membrane has modes at 1 : 1.593 : 2.136 : 2.295 : 2.653 : 2.917. Instrument makers fight these ratios: xylophone and marimba bars are carved thinner in the middle to pull the second mode toward a harmonic of the first, and the timpani's kettle and enclosed air shift several of the head's modes into nearly harmonic ratios, giving it a definite pitch. Church bells are shaped so that their main partials fall near the ratios 0.5 (the hum), 1 (the prime), 1.2 (the tierce, a minor third above the prime), 1.5 (the quint) and 2 (the nominal). The minor-third tierce is why bells sound faintly minor.

Timbre is more than a spectrum. The attack matters a great deal: the scrape of a bow, the chiff of an organ pipe, the thump of a hammer. Classic experiments found that cutting off the beginning of instrument tones makes many of them much harder to identify. John Grey's study of perceived similarity among instrument tones ("Multidimensional perceptual scaling of musical timbres", 1977) found a few underlying dimensions, the clearest being related to how energy is distributed across the spectrum, which listeners hear as brightness, and others related to the attack and to how the spectrum changes over time.

Definition. A steady tone's spectrum is a list of partials $(f_k, a_k)$: frequency and amplitude. Its spectral centroid, the amplitude-weighted mean frequency,

$$C = \frac{\sum_k a_k f_k}{\sum_k a_k},$$

is the standard simple correlate of brightness. (Some authors weight by power, $a_k^2$, instead.) For the five tones of the first example, in multiples of 220 Hz and with the amplitudes the synthesizer actually uses:

Modes of a free bar. For an ideal uniform beam with free ends (Euler–Bernoulli theory), the mode frequencies are proportional to $x_n^2$, where $x_n$ are the positive roots of $\cos x \cosh x = 1$: $x_1 = 4.7300$, $x_2 = 7.8532$, $x_3 = 10.9956$, $x_4 = 14.1372$. The ratios $(x_n/x_1)^2$ are 1, 2.7565, 5.4039, 8.9330.

Modes of a membrane. For an ideal circular membrane fixed at its rim, the mode frequencies are proportional to the zeros $j_{m,k}$ of the Bessel functions $J_m$. Sorted, the first six are $j_{0,1}, j_{1,1}, j_{2,1}, j_{0,2}, j_{3,1}, j_{1,2}$, with ratios to $j_{0,1} = 2.4048$ of 1, 1.5933, 2.1355, 2.2954, 2.6531, 2.9173.

Stiff strings. A string with bending stiffness has partials $f_n = n f_1 \sqrt{1 + B n^2}$, with a small inharmonicity coefficient $B$ that depends on the string's dimensions and material. The upper partials of piano strings are therefore progressively sharp, one reason pianos are tuned with slightly stretched octaves.

Blend and contrast

Orchestration is the art of deciding what the ear will hear as one thing and what it will hear as separate things. The pipe organ makes the point vividly. Listen to a short melody on a soft organ tone; then the same melody with two more ranks of pipes added, sounding an octave and a twelfth above every note; then the same three lines with the upper two starting a fraction of a second late.

In the middle passage you do not hear three melodies in parallel fifths and octaves, the very thing voice-leading rules forbid (Chapter 14). You hear one melody with a new colour. In the last passage the same notes come apart, simply because they no longer start together.

Blend can also hide things you want heard. Listen to a melody and its chords played on the same tone in the same register, and then with the melody moved to a different, brighter instrument an octave higher.

The ear groups sounds into "objects" using several cues at once, studied by Albert Bregman and others under the name auditory scene analysis. The most important for orchestration:

  • Harmonicity. Partials that fit one harmonic series are heard as one tone. Two tones whose partials coincide, as in octaves and twelfths, tend to fuse.
  • Common onset. Partials that start together belong together. A small delay is often enough to separate them, as the third passage of the organ example shows.
  • Common change. Partials that move together in pitch or loudness, with the same vibrato, are grouped. A soloist's vibrato helps them stand out from an accompaniment.
  • Similarity and register. Similar timbres in the same register blend; different timbres or registers separate.

Organ registration is additive synthesis with pipes. Stops are named by the length of their lowest pipe in feet. An 8-foot stop sounds at written pitch; 4-foot sounds an octave higher; 2⅔-foot a twelfth higher (an octave and a fifth); 2-foot two octaves higher; 1⅗-foot two octaves and a major third higher. Dividing 8 by each length gives the harmonic that the stop reinforces: 1, 2, 3, 4 and 5. Mixture stops combine several such high ranks on one knob. Because the ranks reinforce harmonics of the written note, they are heard as colour, not as extra voices.

Orchestrators use the same principle. Maurice Ravel's Boléro (1928) famously gives one statement of its melody to a horn and a celesta with two piccolos playing it in parallel in G major and E major, at intervals that match the third and fifth harmonics of the C-major melody, so that the doublings merge into a single, organ-like sound. Treatises on orchestration, from Hector Berlioz's (1844) to Nikolai Rimsky-Korsakov's Principles of Orchestration (published 1913), are largely catalogues of which combinations blend and which stand apart.

A rule of thumb follows from the harmonic series itself: space chords widely at the bottom and closely at the top. The harmonic series does exactly this (C2 C3 G3 C4 E4 G4 …), and chords voiced like it sound clear and resonant, while close thirds in the bass sound muddy.

Why the ranks fuse (theorem). Let the 8-foot rank play a harmonic tone with fundamental $f$, partials $kf$. A rank at exact ratio $m$ (a whole number) has partials $kmf$, all of which are already partials of the 8-foot tone. The union of the ranks' spectra is still a subset of $\{f, 2f, 3f, \dots\}$, a single harmonic series with fundamental $f$: the combined sound is, acoustically, one tone with a different spectral envelope.

Why mixture ranks are tuned pure. If the 2⅔-foot rank were tuned to the equal-tempered twelfth, $2^{19/12} = 2.99661$ instead of 3, its fundamental would sit just below the third harmonic of the 8-foot tone and beat against it. At C4 = 261.63 Hz: $3 \times 261.63 = 784.89$ Hz against $261.63 \times 2^{19/12} = 784.00$ Hz, a beat of 0.89 Hz. The 12-ET major seventeenth ($2^{28/12}$) against the fifth harmonic is worse: 1318.53 Hz against 1308.15 Hz, a beat of 10.38 Hz. Organ builders tune these ranks to the exact harmonics so that they fuse.

Onset asynchrony. In the third passage of the organ example, each upper note starts 0.12 s after the 8-foot note. The spectra are unchanged when all three sound; only the onsets differ. The separation you hear is therefore a measurement of the onset cue alone: the ear needs common onset as well as harmonicity to fuse.

Register and spacing (measurement). The critical band, within which partials interact roughly, is wider in cents at low frequencies than at high ones (Chapter 3). So the same chord is rougher low than high. With the roughness model of the next section (harmonic partials 1–8, amplitudes $0.88^i$), a close C major triad measures

Timbre shapes harmony

Here is the most important idea of this chapter, and one of the foundations of the golden tonal system: whether a chord sounds smooth or rough depends on the instrument playing it. Listen to two chords, C major and the golden triad (C vE ^G in exact golden ratios). First they are played on a harmonic tone, like an organ. Then the same two chords are played on a tone with a golden spectrum, which no physical instrument has: it is computed.

On the harmonic tone, C major is calm and the golden triad shimmers with tension. On the golden tone the roles reverse: C major sounds clangorous, and the golden triad settles. The chords did not change; only the timbre did.

Chapter 3 explained the reason. Roughness comes from pairs of partials that are close but not equal in frequency: they beat quickly. When two tones form a chord, every partial of one interacts with every partial of the other. Simple ratios like 3/2 and 5/4 make many partials of harmonic tones coincide exactly, so they produce little roughness. But "simple ratio" is only special because the partials are whole-number multiples. Change the partials and the special intervals change with them.

William Sethares developed this idea in Tuning, Timbre, Spectrum, Scale (1998), extending Reinier Plomp and Willem Levelt's roughness measurements (1965) to arbitrary spectra. For any spectrum one can draw a dissonance curve: computed roughness against interval size. Its dips mark the intervals where that spectrum's partials line up. Sethares designed spectra to make unusual tunings consonant, and proposed that the tunings of some musical traditions, such as the Indonesian gamelan with its metallophones, are related to the spectra of their instruments. That last proposal is a hypothesis about history and perception, not an established fact.

With harmonic partials the golden triad is rougher than major, minor and augmented, but smoother than diminished. With golden partials it is the smoothest chord in the table by a wide margin. These are measurements: they depend on the model, the number and strength of partials, and the register. The pattern is robust, though. Using the actual spectra of the audio example above (10 partials each, amplitudes falling as $k^{-1.28}$), the same model gives C major 0.495 and golden 0.535 on the harmonic tone, and C major 0.862 and golden 0.643 on the golden tone: the same reversal.

Timbre shapes harmony in more familiar ways too. Heavily distorted electric guitars favour bare fifths ("power chords"), and a common explanation is that distortion adds many new combination frequencies, which stay tidy for a fifth but turn a major third into a cluster. The spectral composers Gérard Grisey and Tristan Murail built harmony directly from analysed spectra (Chapter 22). And the golden tonal system of this book turns the principle into a laboratory. On harmonic timbres, the timbres of every acoustic instrument, C major is the deepest rest and golden chords are tense colors or, in the author's model, a delicate local rest. On digital golden timbres golden chords measure as the smoothest, a setting in which to study golden harmony on its own terms before carrying the lessons back to harmonic instruments.

Golden-spectrum timbres, and the full picture of which golden chords are smooth on which instruments, are the subject of Chapter 26; the reversal heard above is the timbre flip, a laboratory for hearing golden harmony as home rather than passage; the golden homes themselves are in Chapter 31.

The roughness model. For two partials with frequencies $f_1 \le f_2$ and amplitudes $a_1, a_2$, the model (Sethares' parametrization of the Plomp–Levelt curve) assigns

$$d = \min(a_1, a_2)\left(e^{-3.5\, s\, (f_2 - f_1)} - e^{-5.75\, s\, (f_2 - f_1)}\right),\qquad s = \frac{0.24}{0.0207 f_1 + 18.96},$$

and a chord's roughness is the sum of $d$ over all pairs of partials of all its notes. The term vanishes when $f_1 = f_2$ and is largest when the partials are a fraction of a critical band apart. For the table above: root 261.63 Hz; harmonic spectrum = partials 1 to 8 with amplitudes $0.88^{i}$ ($i = 0, \dots, 7$); golden spectrum = the 11 values $2^m \varphi^n$ ($0 \le m, n \le 3$) below 9, with amplitudes $0.88^{i}$ in increasing order.

Coincidence counts (theorem). Let $S_K = \{2^m \varphi^n : 0 \le m, n < K\}$, a "full" golden spectrum. Its $K^2$ elements are distinct, because $2^a = \varphi^b$ only for $a = b = 0$. For an interval of ratio $r = 2^p \varphi^q$, the partial $r \cdot 2^m \varphi^n = 2^{m+p}\varphi^{n+q}$ lies in $S_K$ exactly when $0 \le m + p < K$ and $0 \le n + q < K$. So

  • the golden sixth $\varphi$ ($p = 0, q = 1$) makes $K(K-1)$ of the $K^2$ partials coincide;
  • the golden third $2/\varphi$ ($p = 1, q = -1$) makes $(K-1)^2$ coincide;
  • the golden fifth $4/\varphi^2$ ($p = 2, q = -2$) makes $(K-2)^2$ coincide;
  • a rational interval such as 3/2 or 5/4 makes none coincide, because $2^m\varphi^n \cdot 3/2$ is never of the form $2^{m'}\varphi^{n'}$: that would make $\varphi^{n - n'}$ rational, forcing $n = n'$ (every nonzero power of $\varphi$ is irrational), and then $3/2$ would have to be a power of 2.

For harmonic spectra the situation is mirrored: an irrational ratio such as $\varphi$ can never map a whole number $k$ to a whole number $j$, so no partials coincide exactly, while $p/q$ makes a fraction $1/q$ coincide (Chapter 14). For the truncated golden spectrum of the table (11 partials), direct counting gives 7 coincidences of 11 for the golden sixth, 6 for the golden third, 3 for the golden fifth, and 0 for the major third and the fifth; the harmonic spectrum 1–8 gives 2 for the fifth (partials 3 and 6), 1 for the major third (partial 5), and 0 for every golden interval.

Coincidence is not the whole story (near misses within a critical band are what create roughness), but it explains the direction of the flip: each spectrum makes "its own" intervals smooth.

Exercise 17.1 (listening). Rank the five tones of the first example from darkest to brightest. Compare your ranking with the spectral centroids.

Answer

By centroid: sine (1.00), soft (1.94), golden (2.69), hollow (3.10), bright (3.77). Many listeners hear the hollow tone as darker than its centroid suggests, because the missing even harmonics change the character, not just the brightness. Brightness is one dimension of timbre, not all of it.

Exercise 17.2. Which harmonic does a 1⅗-foot organ stop reinforce, and what interval above the written note does it sound? What about a 1⅓-foot stop?

Answer

$8 \div 1.6 = 5$: the fifth harmonic, two octaves and a major third up. $8 \div (4/3) = 6$: the sixth harmonic, two octaves and a fifth up (a nineteenth).

Exercise 17.3. At A3 = 220 Hz, how fast does an equal-tempered twelfth beat against the third harmonic of the lower note?

Answer

$3 \times 220 = 660$ Hz against $220 \times 2^{19/12} = 659.26$ Hz: 0.74 Hz, a slow wave about every 1.3 seconds.

Exercise 17.4 (proof). Show that with ideal harmonic spectra no partials of two tones a golden third apart coincide.

Answer

A coincidence would need $k \cdot (2/\varphi) = j$ for whole numbers $j, k \ge 1$, that is $\varphi = 2k/j$, a rational number. But $\varphi = (1 + \sqrt 5)/2$ is irrational.

Exercise 17.5. Using the register table, by roughly what factor does the roughness of a close C major triad grow for each octave it is moved down, between C5 and C2? Why might an orchestrator still double a melody in the bass?

Answer

The ratios are $1.319/0.889 \approx 1.48$, $2.344/1.319 \approx 1.78$ and $4.468/2.344 \approx 1.91$: the growth itself accelerates as the chord gets lower. A single line doubled in octaves adds no thirds, and octave partials coincide exactly, so the doubling adds weight without much roughness.

Exercise 17.6. In the full golden spectrum $S_K$ with $K = 4$ (16 partials), how many partials coincide for the golden sixth, the golden third and the golden fifth?

Answer

$K(K-1) = 12$, $(K-1)^2 = 9$ and $(K-2)^2 = 4$.
