Changing layers

Modulation means moving the sense of home from one key to another. Western music does it through common ground: keys near each other on the circle of fifths share most of their notes and several chords, and a shared chord can be reinterpreted as belonging to the new key (Chapter 11, Chapter 8). 36-ET adds a kind of key change that has no common ground at all. Listen to a cadence in C major, then the same cadence in ^C major, every note one sixth-step (a third of a semitone) higher.

The effect resembles the abrupt step-up key change of many pop songs, but smaller than any semitone, and stranger: nothing carries over. Every note of the second key is new.

The key system of 36-ET

36-ET contains 12-ET exactly, plus two copies shifted up and down by a sixth-step: the three layers (Chapter 24). A Western scale lives on one layer, so 36-ET has 36 major and 36 minor keys, twelve of each on each layer (Chapter 24). Their relationships come in two kinds:

Within a layer, everything from Chapter 8 holds unchanged. Between layers there are no common tones, so there are no common chords and no pivot chords of the Western kind. ^C major is the closest possible key to C major in pitch, every note just 33 c away, and the farthest possible in common tones. In 12-ET the nearest key in pitch, C♯, still shares two notes with C (F = E♯ and C = B♯).

Four ways across

  1. Direct shift. Simply continue in the new key, as in the example. The ear accepts it as a change of plane, because the new key is internally the same as the old.
  2. Planing. Slide chords by sixth-steps, as the half-step theorem forces when chords a semitone apart move in parallel (Chapter 28): C → ^C → vC♯ → C♯ passes through two layers on its way to the next semitone. Stopping at ^C instead of C♯ is a planing modulation.
  3. A golden pivot. Use a golden chord, which touches all three layers, as the hinge (the next section).
  4. A passing bridge. Let the passing-chord engine connect the last chord of one key to the first of the next. The Well-Tempered Golden Clavier uses passing slots to bridge every tonic to the next one, a sixth-step higher (Chapter 31).

Western techniques, translated

Chapter 11 described the standard ways to modulate. Here is what becomes of each when the two keys lie on different layers. Status: the common-tone facts are theorems; the descriptions of effect are informal.

The table shows the character of layer modulation. Because the keys share nothing, modulation between layers is carried by motion, voices sliding by sixth-steps, rather than by reinterpretation of something held in common. The one exception is the golden chord, which reaches into every layer. That is why it is the natural pivot of 36-ET.

Exercise 32.1. Of the 72 major and minor keys of 36-ET, how many share at least one note with C major (counting C major itself)?

Answer

Exactly the 24 keys on the 12-ET layer. Any two major scales share at least two notes (the minimum, 2, occurs at five or six fifths apart), and a natural minor scale has the same notes as its relative major. The 48 keys on the other two layers share none.

Theorem (layer disjointness). Let $K\subseteq\ell+3\mathbb{Z}_{36}$ and $K'\subseteq\ell'+3\mathbb{Z}_{36}$ be Western scales on layers $\ell\ne\ell'$. Then $K\cap K'=\varnothing$. Proof: distinct cosets of a subgroup are disjoint. ∎

Same layer. On one layer, $3\mathbb{Z}_{36}\cong\mathbb{Z}_{12}$ by $3x\mapsto x$, so all of 12-ET key theory applies verbatim. For major scales $k$ fifths apart the common-tone count is $7,6,5,4,3,2,2$ for $k=0,\dots,6$ (computed).

Golden pivot chords

A golden triad has one note on each layer (Chapter 25). So it can hold hands with keys on every layer at once. Listen to C major turning into ^C major through Cg.

Cg also sits two sixth-steps from a Western triad on each layer: C major, ^C minor and vE augmented. Here it acts as a hub, going out to each layer and returning.

A one-note hinge on every layer

Cg = C vE ^G. Its C is the root of the C major triad, its ^G is the fifth of the ^C major triad, and its vE is the third of the vC major triad. In voice-leading distance (sixth-steps summed over voices) Cg is 2 from C and 3 from each of ^C and vC. So the pivot C → Cg → ^C costs 2 + 3 = 5 sixth-steps of total motion:

  • C → Cg → ^C: C holds, then rises a sixth-step to ^C; E falls a sixth-step to vE, then rises two to ^E; G rises a sixth-step to ^G, then holds. One common tone at each joint.
  • C → Cg → ^Cm: C holds, then rises to ^C; E falls one sixth-step at a time, vE then ^E♭; G rises to ^G, then holds. Total motion 2 + 2 = 4, and every voice moves in one direction only. It is the most efficient golden route from C major to any key on another layer, and it changes mode at the same time.

In four parts, with the root doubled, the first pivot can be written so that every voice moves by at most two sixth-steps at each joint:

The golden pivot is closer to Western common-tone modulation than to pivot-chord modulation: the golden chord does not belong to both keys as a chord, but it shares one note with each and lies very near both tonic triads.

Pivots between modes

The golden chord also pivots between major and minor on the same layer. By the quality theorem, Cg is the strict passing chord of C ↔ A♭ (major world) and of Cm ↔ Fm (minor world) (Chapter 27). That makes the golden modal mixture C – Cg – Cm (Chapter 29) a modulation tool: Cg is the centroid of C, Cm and Caug, two sixth-steps from the first and three from the other two.

Exercise 32.2. Find the golden triad that plays Cg's hub role for the three G major triads G, ^G and vG, and say which note it shares with each.

Answer

Transpose everything by 21 sixth-steps (C → G): Gg = G vB ^D (21, 32, 7). It contains G, the root of G major; ^D, the fifth of ^G major (^G ^B ^D); and vB, the third of vG major (vG vB vD). By transposition its distances to the three are again 2, 3 and 3.

Proposition (one note per key). If $W$ lies on one layer and $G_r$ is a golden triad, then $|W\cap G_r|\le 1$. Proof: $G_r$ has exactly one element in each coset of $3\mathbb{Z}_{36}$. ∎ Of the 36 major-scale sets, exactly 21 contain a note of Cg, seven on each layer, each containing exactly one (computed).

The three C's. C major $=\{0,12,21\}$, ^C $=\{1,13,22\}$, vC $=\{35,11,20\}$. $G_0=\{0,11,22\}$ meets them in 0, 22 and 11 respectively, and the minimal voice-leading distances are 2, 3, 3 (with moves $(0,+1,-1)$, $(+1,+2,0)$, $(-1,0,-2)$ for C, vE, ^G).

Why ^Cm is as close as C. The inversion $I_{22}: x\mapsto 22-x$ fixes $G_0$ and maps $\{0,12,21\}$ to $\{22,10,1\}$ = ^Cm; it preserves voice-leading distance. The table of best pivots was computed by minimizing $d(X,G)+d(G,Y)$ over all 36 golden triads $G$.

Between golden scales

Golden scales have keys too. A golden7 scale can start on any of the 36 tonics, and neighbouring golden keys share almost everything. First, hear how little changes between neighbours. Each scale is played from C, so only the altered note is different.

Now a real modulation from C golden7 to vE golden7 through a chord both keys share.

The circle of golden keys

A golden7 scale is a window of seven consecutive golden-chain notes. Moving its tonic by one chain step, up a golden sixth (C → ^A♭) or up a golden third (C → vE), slides the window by one: one note leaves and one enters. This is the golden counterpart of moving one step on the circle of fifths, where G major replaces F by F♯ (Chapter 8).

The size of the change is the scale's chroma, the difference between its two step sizes. For the diatonic it is the chromatic semitone (113.69 c in Pythagorean tuning, 100 c in 12-ET). For golden7 it is 267.64 − 99.27 = 168.37 c, which 36-ET renders as 5 sixth-steps: a key change alters one note by about a sixth-step less than a whole tone. For golden13 it is 99.27 − 69.10 = 30.17 c, the golden comma, one sixth-step: neighbouring golden13 keys differ by the smallest interval of 36-ET.

Common chords and a worked modulation

C golden7 has five golden triads (C♯g, vFg, ^A♭g, Cg, vEg; chain roots +3 to −1). vE golden7 has ^Gg, vEg, Cg, ^A♭g, vFg (chain roots +2 to −2). Four are common: Cg, vEg, vFg, ^A♭g. That is the same number of shared triads as between C and G major, which share C, Em, G and Am, but out of five triads per key instead of seven.

The worked example: Cg – C♯g – Cg confirms C golden7. ^A♭g belongs to both keys and serves as the pivot. ^Gg is the first chord that uses the new note vE♭; the move ^A♭g → ^Gg lowers every voice a semitone. ^Gg – vEg – vFg – vEg settles the new key; the last move, vFg → vEg, again lowers every voice a semitone, and the bell's final vE♭ rises to vE. In vE golden7, ^Gg and vFg play the roles that vEg and C♯g play in C golden7, so if the functional hypothesis of Chapter 31 holds, the modulation ends with the new key's "falling" cadence. Status: the chord facts are theorems; any functional reading is hypothesis.

Golden keys are not on a layer

A Western key lives on one layer. A golden key never does. Every golden7 scale has three notes on its tonic's layer (B, C, C♯ for C golden7) and two on each of the other layers; every golden13 scale has five, four and four. So the two kinds of modulation in this chapter are independent: changing layers moves Western keys between disjoint worlds, while golden keys always straddle all three.

This also reverses some intuitions about distance. Read the common-tone table above by pitch instead of by chain:

  • golden7 on ^C, one sixth-step above C, is chain distance 13 away and shares no notes with C golden7;
  • golden7 on C♯, a semitone above, is chain distance 3 away and shares four (C, C♯, vF, ^A♭);
  • golden7 on D, a whole tone above, is chain distance 6 away and shares one (C♯).

In 12-ET the keys a semitone apart are among the most distant (two common tones). In golden7 the semitone neighbour is fairly close, because three golden sixths make two octaves and almost exactly a semitone: transposing by a semitone is nearly a three-step move along the chain.

Between kinds of scale

  • golden7 inside golden13. C golden13 contains seven golden7 scales, on C, C♯, vE, vF, ^G, ^A♭ and B, just as a chromatic aggregate contains every diatonic set. Moving from golden7 to golden13 on the same tonic adds six notes and loses none.
  • golden13 and silver13. silver13 is golden13 with every ^ and v swapped. On the same tonic they share seven notes, C, C♯, D, vF♯, ^F♯, B♭ and B: the five 12-ET notes, which the swap fixes, and the pair vF♯, ^F♯, which it exchanges. That is as many as golden13 keys six chain steps apart share.
  • Golden and Western. C golden7 shares only C and B with C major (Chapter 30). The smoothest bridge uses exactly those notes: Cmaj7 = C E G B bends its inner voices by a sixth-step each into Cg7 = C vE ^G B, which is the tonic seventh of golden7.

On real instruments, which are harmonic, the golden world is a delicate local rest and a return to C major is always the deeper release. In the digital laboratory of Chapter 26 the crossing can be paired with a change to a golden-spectrum timbre, on which the arriving golden chords also measure as smooth; the example does this to let you dwell in the golden key.

Answer

Exercise 32.4. Explain why neighbouring golden13 keys differ by only one sixth-step, while neighbouring golden7 keys differ by five.

Answer

The entering and leaving notes are $n$ chain steps apart (the window length), so the change is the chain-$n$ interval: chain 13 is the golden comma, 30.17 c (one sixth-step); chain 7 is 168.37 c after octave reduction (five sixth-steps). Equivalently, the change is the scale's chroma, its large step minus its small step.

Proposition (window overlap). Let $W_n(t)$ be golden$n$ ($n = 2m+1$) on tonic $t$, a chain window $\{25j: k-m\le j\le k+m\}$ where $k\equiv 13t \pmod{36}$ is the chain position of $t$ (13 is the inverse of 25 mod 36). Take $k\in[-18,18]$. Then $|W_n(0)\cap W_n(t)| = \max(0, n-|k|)$. Proof: $j\mapsto 25j$ is injective on any 36 consecutive integers, and the union of the two windows spans $n+|k|\le 7+18 = 25$ (golden7) or $13 + 18 = 31$ (golden13) consecutive positions, so the overlap of the sets equals the overlap of the integer intervals. ∎

Proposition (the altered note). Shifting the window by $\mp 1$ removes chain note $\pm m$ and adds $\mp(m+1)$. The two differ by chain distance $2m+1 = n$; for golden7, $1200(7\log_2\varphi \bmod 1) = 1031.63$, i.e. 168.37 c octave-reduced; for golden13, chain 13 is $\varphi^{13}/2^9 = 30.17$ c.

Common triads. The golden triads in $W_n(t)$ are $G$ with chain roots $k-m+2,\dots,k+m$ ($n-2$ of them; 5 for golden7, 11 for golden13), so two golden7 keys at chain distance $|k|$ share $\max(0, 5-|k|)$ triads: 4, 3, 2, 1, 0.

golden13 ∩ silver13 on C is $\{0, 3, 6, 17, 19, 30, 33\}$ = C, C♯, D, vF♯, ^F♯, B♭, B (computed).
