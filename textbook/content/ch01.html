Vibration and waves

Every sound begins with something moving back and forth. A guitar string blurs when you pluck it; the cone of a loudspeaker trembles under your fingertips; your own throat buzzes when you hum. That motion pushes on the air next to it, the air pushes on the air beyond, and a ripple of pressure spreads outward until it reaches your ear, where it moves your eardrum back and forth in the same pattern. Hearing is the ear's report on that motion.

The simplest possible back-and-forth motion is a smooth, even swing, like a weight bouncing on a spring or a pendulum that never runs down. As a sound, it is a pure tone, or sine tone: plain, a little hollow, with no color of its own. Listen to one, then to a tone with the same pitch whose vibration has a more complicated shape.

Both tones repeat their pattern 440 times every second, and that is why they have the same pitch. They differ in the shape of each repetition, and that is why they sound different. This chapter is about the rate. The shape is the subject of Chapter 2.

Picture a single point on a vibrating string, or a single molecule of air near a loudspeaker. Its position changes in time. Plot that position against time and, for the simplest motion, you get the curve in the figure below: a sine wave.

Three numbers describe it.

  • The period $T$ is the time one full cycle takes, measured in seconds.
  • The frequency $f$ is the number of cycles per second, measured in hertz (Hz). It is the reciprocal of the period: $f = 1/T$. A tone at 440 Hz has a period of $1/440$ s, about 2.27 milliseconds.
  • The amplitude is how far the motion swings from the middle. Bigger swings push harder on the air.

A vibrating object does not send its own molecules to your ear. It sends a wave: each layer of air pushes the next and then falls back, so a pattern of slightly squeezed and slightly stretched air (compressions and rarefactions) travels outward while each molecule only jiggles in place. In air at 20 °C the pattern moves at about 343 metres per second. In one period the wave travels one wavelength $\lambda$, so

$$\lambda = \frac{v}{f},$$

where $v$ is the speed of sound. The table shows how wide the audible range is in space as well as in time.

The pressure changes involved are tiny compared with the steady pressure of the atmosphere, yet the ear detects them over an enormous range of strengths. What reaches the eardrum is a single wiggly line, pressure against time, however many instruments are playing. Everything we call pitch, loudness, color and harmony has to be read out of that one line.

Sound in air is a longitudinal wave: the air moves along the direction the wave travels. A wave on a string is transverse: the string moves sideways while the wave runs along it. The two obey the same wave equation (Chapter 2), so the same mathematics describes both.

Why sines. Let a mass $m$ sit on a spring. If it is displaced by $x$ from rest, the spring pulls it back with force $F = -kx$, where $k$ is the spring's stiffness (Hooke's law). Newton's second law, force equals mass times acceleration, gives

$$m\,\frac{d^2x}{dt^2} = -kx.$$

Claim. Every solution is $x(t) = A\sin(2\pi f t + \theta)$ with

$$f = \frac{1}{2\pi}\sqrt{\frac{k}{m}},$$

where the amplitude $A$ and the starting phase $\theta$ depend only on how the motion was started.

Check. Differentiate twice: $x'' = -(2\pi f)^2 A\sin(2\pi f t + \theta) = -(2\pi f)^2 x$. This equals $-(k/m)\,x$ exactly when $(2\pi f)^2 = k/m$. That these are all the solutions follows from the uniqueness theorem for linear differential equations: a solution is fixed by its starting position and velocity, and the two free numbers $A$ and $\theta$ can match any such pair. $\square$

The frequency depends on stiffness and mass, not on amplitude: a spring swings at the same rate whether it swings widely or barely. That is why a struck bell or a plucked string keeps very nearly the same pitch as it fades. Almost any system near a stable resting point behaves like a spring for small motions, since the restoring force is then approximately proportional to the displacement. Sines are therefore the natural vibrations of the physical world. (For example, $k = 100$ N/m and $m = 0.01$ kg give $f = \frac{1}{2\pi}\sqrt{10^4} \approx 15.92$ Hz.)

A travelling wave. A sine pressure wave moving in the $+x$ direction at speed $v$ is

$$p(x, t) = P\sin\!\Big(2\pi\big(f t - x/\lambda\big)\Big).$$

A crest sits where $f t - x/\lambda$ is constant, so it moves with $dx/dt = f\lambda$. Hence $v = f\lambda$, the formula above.

Frequency, pitch and loudness

Speed up a vibration and the tone rises; slow it down and it falls. The next example glides a pure tone from 55 to 1760 vibrations per second. Listen to how steady the climb sounds, and notice that the low end seems quieter even though the sweep's strength never changes.

Now keep the rate fixed and change only the size of the swing. The pitch stays put while the tone grows quieter.

It is worth keeping physical quantities and heard qualities apart, because they do not match one for one.

The audible range. Young listeners hear roughly from 20 Hz to 20,000 Hz (20 kHz); the upper limit falls with age. Musical pitch is clearest in a narrower band: a piano runs from 27.5 Hz to about 4186 Hz, and most melodies live between about 100 and 1000 Hz.

Pitch of complex tones. A real instrument's tone is not a single sine. Its pitch is set by how often the whole pattern repeats, which is normally the frequency of its lowest component, the fundamental. We will see in Chapter 3 that the ear can hear this repetition rate even when no component sits at that frequency.

Loudness and decibels. The ear handles pressure amplitudes over a range of roughly a million to one, from the faintest audible sound to the painfully loud. To keep the numbers manageable, levels are measured on a logarithmic scale in decibels (dB). Every halving of the amplitude lowers the level by about 6 dB, and every factor of ten in amplitude is 20 dB, so a million to one is about 120 dB. Loudness as heard is not simply amplitude: at the same physical level, very low and very high tones sound softer than tones in the middle of the range. This was mapped in the equal-loudness contours first measured by Fletcher and Munson (1933). Those contours are measurements on listeners, and they vary from person to person.

Pitch, loudness and timbre are not completely independent. Loud tones can sound very slightly different in pitch from soft ones, and brightness can make a tone seem louder. For the steady tones of this book the three are close enough to separate.

Definitions. For a pressure wave with amplitude $A$, the sound level relative to a reference amplitude $A_0$ is

$$L = 20\log_{10}\frac{A}{A_0}\ \text{dB}.$$

Intensity (power per area) is proportional to the square of the amplitude, $I \propto A^2$, so equivalently $L = 10\log_{10}(I/I_0)$. The standard reference intensity for sound in air is $I_0 = 10^{-12}$ W/m², near the threshold of hearing at 1 kHz.

Consequences.

  • Doubling the amplitude adds $20\log_{10}2 \approx 6.02$ dB; doubling the intensity adds $10\log_{10}2 \approx 3.01$ dB.
  • The steps in the loudness example are exactly $-6$ dB in amplitude, $20\log_{10}(10^{-6/20}) = -6$, about half the amplitude each time ($10^{-0.3} \approx 0.501$).
  • An amplitude ratio of $10^6$ is $20 \cdot 6 = 120$ dB.

The decibel is a first example of the idea of the next section: when a quantity matters by ratios, measure it with a logarithm.

Hearing in ratios: the logarithm

Here is a simple experiment with a surprising result. First you will hear tones that climb in equal steps of 110 vibrations per second: 110, 220, 330, and so on up to 880. Then you will hear tones that climb by doubling: 110, 220, 440, 880. Both runs end in the same place.

The first step of the equal-hertz run is a big leap; the last step is a modest one, though each adds the same 110 Hz. The doubling run sounds like a staircase with equal treads. The ear does not hear differences in frequency. It hears ratios.

The same thing shows up in glides. The next example first raises the frequency by the same number of hertz every second, then raises it by the same ratio every second.

A ratio compares two frequencies by division. From 110 Hz to 220 Hz is a ratio of $220/110 = 2$; so is 220 to 440, and 440 to 880. Equal ratios sound like equal distances in pitch. Musicians call a pitch distance an interval, and the conclusion of the experiment is:

an interval is a ratio of frequencies.

The equal-hertz steps above have ratios $2/1, 3/2, 4/3, 5/4, 6/5, 7/6, 8/7$, which get smaller and smaller. That is exactly what you heard. (The first of these ratios, 2/1, 3/2, 4/3, 5/4 and 6/5, will turn out to be among the most important intervals in music; see Chapter 2.)

To turn ratios into distances we can add, we use the logarithm. The base-2 logarithm of a number $x$, written $\log_2 x$, answers the question "2 to what power gives $x$?"

Its key property is that it turns multiplication into addition: $\log_2(xy) = \log_2 x + \log_2 y$. Stacking two intervals multiplies their ratios, so it adds their logarithms. The figure shows the same six notes, each twice the frequency of the one before, on an ordinary (linear) axis and on a logarithmic axis. On the linear axis the low notes crowd together; on the logarithmic axis they are evenly spaced, as the ear hears them. A piano keyboard is a logarithmic axis made of wood: equal distances along it are equal ratios.

The idea that sensation grows with the logarithm of the stimulus is associated with Ernst Weber and Gustav Fechner in the nineteenth century. For pitch it holds well in the musical range; for other senses it is only a rough rule. The logarithm itself was introduced by John Napier in 1614 as a way to turn long multiplications into additions, which is exactly the job it does here.

Definition. For $x > 0$, $\log_2 x$ is the unique real $y$ with $2^y = x$. Since $y \mapsto 2^y$ is continuous and strictly increasing from $(-\infty, \infty)$ onto $(0, \infty)$, this is well defined.

Laws. For $x, y > 0$ and real $n$:

$$\log_2(xy) = \log_2 x + \log_2 y,\qquad \log_2(x^n) = n\log_2 x,$$ $$\log_2 x = \frac{\ln x}{\ln 2} = \frac{\log_{10} x}{\log_{10} 2}.$$

The first follows from $2^{a}2^{b} = 2^{a+b}$, the second from $(2^a)^n = 2^{an}$, the third from writing $x = 2^y$ and taking any other logarithm of both sides.

Theorem (equal-hertz steps shrink). The interval from $nf$ to $(n+1)f$ has size $\log_2\frac{n+1}{n}$ octaves, which decreases strictly as $n$ grows, and tends to 0.

Proof. $\frac{n+1}{n} = 1 + \frac1n$ decreases strictly toward 1, and $\log_2$ is strictly increasing with $\log_2 1 = 0$. $\square$

Why a logarithm and nothing else (sketch). Suppose we want a measure $d(r)$ of interval size that is continuous in the ratio $r$, adds when intervals stack, $d(rs) = d(r) + d(s)$, and gives the octave size 1. Then $d = \log_2$. Setting $g(u) = d(2^u)$ gives a continuous $g$ with $g(u + v) = g(u) + g(v)$ and $g(1) = 1$; the only continuous solutions of this equation (Cauchy's functional equation) are $g(u) = cu$, so $g(u) = u$ and $d(r) = \log_2 r$. The logarithm is forced on us by two requirements: intervals add, and size varies continuously. See Appendix A for a primer on logarithms.

Sweeps. A glide whose frequency is $f(t) = f_0 + (f_1 - f_0)t/T$ is linear in hertz. A glide with $f(t) = f_0 (f_1/f_0)^{t/T}$ is linear in $\log_2 f$: it rises $\log_2(f_1/f_0)/T$ octaves per second. The sweep in the previous section rose 5 octaves in 15 s, one octave every 3 s.

The octave

Play a note, then the note with exactly twice its frequency. The second is clearly higher, and yet it sounds like the same note, lifted. When men and women sing a tune together, they usually sing it this far apart without noticing that they are not in unison. This interval, the ratio 2:1, is the octave.

When the four C's sound together they fuse so well that they can be hard to count. The octave is one of the smoothest intervals there is, and many musical traditions treat notes an octave apart as being in some sense the same, though how much of this is learned rather than built into hearing is still debated.

Because of this octave equivalence, Western music names notes with seven letters, A to G, plus sharps and flats, and repeats the names in every octave. To say which C we mean, we add an octave number: middle C is C4, and each octave number begins at a C. The tuning standard is A4 = 440 Hz, the A above middle C. Every A is a power of 2 times 440 Hz:

The MIDI number counts semitones, with middle C = 60 and A4 = 69; it is how computers name pitches. The piano's 88 keys span $108 - 21 = 87$ semitones, seven octaves and a minor third.

A pitch class is a note name with the octave forgotten: "C", meaning every C. Twelve-tone music has twelve pitch classes, which Chapter 8 arranges on a circle and studies as a group.

Why the octave sounds so smooth is a question about the inside of a tone, not its rate. The answer, in Chapter 2 and Chapter 3, is that for the harmonic tones of most instruments every component of the upper note coincides with a component of the lower one.

Equal-tempered frequencies. The note with MIDI number $n$ has frequency

$$f(n) = 440\cdot 2^{(n-69)/12}\ \text{Hz},\qquad n(f) = 69 + 12\log_2\frac{f}{440}.$$

For example $f(60) = 440 \cdot 2^{-9/12} \approx 261.63$ Hz. Non-integer $n$ is meaningful: $n = 60.5$ is a quarter-tone above middle C. 36-ET, the golden system's tuning, uses steps of $1/3$ of a MIDI unit (Chapter 24).

Octave equivalence as a relation. Call two frequencies equivalent, $f \sim g$, when $\log_2(f/g)$ is an integer. This is an equivalence relation:

  • reflexive: $\log_2(f/f) = 0$;
  • symmetric: $\log_2(g/f) = -\log_2(f/g)$;
  • transitive: $\log_2(f/h) = \log_2(f/g) + \log_2(g/h)$, a sum of integers.

An equivalence class is a pitch class. Fixing a reference $f_0$, the class of $f$ is labelled by the fractional part of $\log_2(f/f_0)$, a number in $[0, 1)$. Pitch classes therefore form a circle: walking up from the reference, you return to it after one octave. In 12-ET the twelve classes sit at the points $k/12$, $k = 0, 1, \dots, 11$, the group $\mathbb{Z}_{12}$.

Measuring intervals in cents

A semitone is the smallest step on a piano, but the ear can tell much smaller differences, and tuning is all about those. To talk about them we split each equal-tempered semitone into 100 equal parts called cents, so the octave is 1200 cents. Listen to pairs of tones that differ by 100, 50, 25, 10 and finally 5 cents.

A semitone is obvious and a quarter-tone (50 cents) sounds clearly "out of tune". At 10 cents many listeners can still hear a difference when the two tones follow each other closely; at 5 cents fewer can. These are rough figures: the smallest difference a listener can detect depends on the person, the training, the register and the kind of tone, and in the middle range careful listeners can manage a few cents.

The size of an interval with ratio $r$, in cents, is

$$\text{cents}(r) = 1200\log_2 r.$$

An octave ($r = 2$) is 1200 c, an equal-tempered semitone ($r = 2^{1/12}$) is exactly 100 c, and any 12-ET interval of $k$ semitones is $100k$ cents. Intervals that are not in 12-ET get fractional values. The table lists some that will matter throughout the book; the ratios come from the harmonic series of Chapter 2.

Computing cents. With a calculator that only has base-10 logarithms, use $\text{cents}(r) = 1200\,\log_{10} r / \log_{10} 2 \approx 3986.31\,\log_{10} r$. To go back from cents to a ratio, use $r = 2^{c/1200}$.

Worked example. How far apart are 440 Hz and 450 Hz? $1200\log_2(450/440) \approx 38.91$ c, a bit more than a third of a semitone. And one cent at A4 is $440\,(2^{1/1200} - 1) \approx 0.25$ Hz; one cent is a frequency change of about one part in 1731.

Cents add. Stack a fifth and a fourth and you get an octave: $3/2 \cdot 4/3 = 2$, and $701.96 + 498.04 = 1200$. That additivity is the whole point of cents.

The golden system uses intervals that are not ratios of whole numbers at all. Its basic interval is the golden ratio $\varphi = (1+\sqrt5)/2 \approx 1.618$, which in cents is 833.09 c, a slightly wide minor sixth. Its inversion, the golden third $2/\varphi$, is 366.91 c, between the minor third (300) and the major third (400). The golden system's grid, 36-ET, divides the octave into 36 equal sixth-steps of 33.33 c each (Chapter 23, Chapter 24).

Definition. $c(r) = 1200\log_2 r$ for a ratio $r > 0$. It is the unique continuous measure of interval size that adds under stacking and assigns 1200 to the octave (by the argument in the logarithm section).

Properties.

  • $c(rs) = c(r) + c(s)$: stacking adds.
  • $c(1/r) = -c(r)$: a downward interval is negative.
  • $c(r^n) = n\,c(r)$: $n$ equal intervals stacked.
  • $c(2/r) = 1200 - c(r)$: the inversion of an interval (the step from its top note up to the octave) fills the octave. For example $c(4/3) = 1200 - c(3/2)$.

Theorem. The perfect fifth, $c(3/2) = 701.955\ldots$, is irrational, and more generally $c(p/q)$ is irrational for every ratio of whole numbers $p/q$ that is not a power of 2.

Proof. Write $p/q$ in lowest terms and suppose $1200\log_2(p/q) = a/b$ with integers $a, b$, $b > 0$. Then $(p/q)^{1200b} = 2^{a}$. If $a \ge 0$ this says $p^{1200b} = 2^{a}q^{1200b}$; if $a < 0$ it says $2^{-a}p^{1200b} = q^{1200b}$. Either way, an odd prime dividing $p$ would divide $q$, and an odd prime dividing $q$ would divide $p$, which lowest terms forbid. By unique factorization into primes, $p$ and $q$ are then powers of 2, and so is $p/q$. $\square$

So no pure interval other than octaves ever lands exactly on the 12-ET grid, or on the grid of any equal division of the octave. This small fact is the root of the whole history of tuning (Chapter 4, Chapter 5).

Exercise 1.1. What are the period and the wavelength in air (343 m/s) of middle C, 261.63 Hz?

Answer

$T = 1/261.63 \approx 3.82$ ms; $\lambda = 343/261.63 \approx 1.31$ m.

Exercise 1.2. Find the frequency seven semitones above A4 in 12-ET, and compare it with $\tfrac32 \cdot 440$ Hz. How many cents apart are they?

Answer

$440 \cdot 2^{7/12} \approx 659.26$ Hz (E5), against 660 Hz. The gap is $1200\log_2(660/659.26) \approx 1.96$ c: the 12-ET fifth is 1.96 c narrower than the pure fifth, $701.96 - 700$.

Exercise 1.3. A tone is raised by 1 percent ($r = 1.01$). How many cents is that? What ratio is 1 cent?

Answer

$1200\log_2 1.01 \approx 17.23$ c. One cent is $2^{1/1200} \approx 1.000578$, about 0.058 percent.

Exercise 1.4. Show that the inversion of an inversion is the original interval, and that an interval and its inversion always add to 1200 c. Which interval is its own inversion?

Answer

Inversion sends $r \mapsto 2/r$; applying it twice gives $2/(2/r) = r$. Then $c(r) + c(2/r) = c(2) = 1200$. An interval is its own inversion when $r = 2/r$, i.e. $r = \sqrt2$, which is 600 c, the equal-tempered tritone.

Exercise 1.5. (Listening.) Play the cents example and write down the smallest step you can reliably hear. Then repeat it at a lower volume. Does your threshold change?

Answer

There is no single right answer: it is a measurement on you. Many listeners hear 10 c in this setting and fewer hear 5 c; very soft levels usually make small differences harder to hear.
