Beats

Play two pure tones that are almost, but not quite, the same. You do not hear two tones. You hear one, which swells and fades in a steady pulse called beating. The closer the two tones, the slower the pulse.

Beats are the tuner's best friend: a guitarist or piano tuner adjusts a string until its beats against a reference slow down and stop (for unisons and octaves), or settle at a chosen rate (for tempered intervals). They also appear between the partials of two complex tones, which is where they start to matter for harmony. Listen to a fifth that is perfectly tuned, then mistuned by one vibration per second, first with rich tones and then with pure ones.

The mistuned fifth beats clearly only when the tones have harmonics. The beat comes from the third harmonic of the lower note (660 Hz) rubbing against the second harmonic of the upper one (662 Hz). Pure sines a fifth apart have no partials close enough to beat; at most a much fainter waver remains.

When two sines of nearly equal frequency $f_1$ and $f_2$ add, they drift in and out of step. When their crests line up, they reinforce; half a beat later, the crest of one meets the trough of the other and they cancel. The result is a tone at the average frequency whose loudness rises and falls $|f_1 - f_2|$ times per second, the beat rate.

Between complex tones, beats arise wherever a partial of one tone lies close to a partial of the other. If harmonic $q$ of the lower tone, at $q f_1$, lies near harmonic $p$ of the upper tone, at $p f_2$, they beat at

$$|p f_2 - q f_1|\ \text{per second}.$$

The beat disappears exactly when $f_2/f_1 = q/p$: a pure interval. That is why tuners can set pure fifths and octaves by ear, and why 12-ET intervals, which are not pure, beat:

12-ET fifths beat slowly, about once a second in the middle register, while 12-ET major thirds beat about ten times a second. You will hear that difference directly in Chapter 4. Beat rates scale with register: the same interval an octave lower beats half as fast.

Golden chords have their own beat patterns. A close-voiced golden seventh on harmonic tones, with root 110 Hz, beats at 6.13, 7.58, 9.37 and 12.26 per second. The first three are in the proportions $1 : 2/\varphi : 4/\varphi^2$, so the chord's beats form a golden triad in rhythm (Chapter 26).

The beat identity. For equal amplitudes,

$$\sin(2\pi f_1 t) + \sin(2\pi f_2 t)$$ $$= 2\cos\!\big(\pi(f_1 - f_2)t\big)\,\sin\!\big(\pi(f_1 + f_2)t\big).$$

This is the sum-to-product formula $\sin a + \sin b = 2\cos\frac{a-b}{2}\sin\frac{a+b}{2}$. The second factor is a tone at the mean frequency $(f_1 + f_2)/2$. The first is a slow envelope of frequency $|f_1 - f_2|/2$; the loudness follows its absolute value, which peaks twice per envelope cycle, so the beat rate is $|f_1 - f_2|$.

Unequal amplitudes $a \ge b$: the envelope swings between $a + b$ and $a - b$ instead of reaching zero, so the beat is shallower but has the same rate. (Write the sum as the imaginary part of $e^{2\pi i f_1 t}(a + b\,e^{2\pi i (f_2 - f_1)t})$: the bracket's modulus oscillates between $a - b$ and $a + b$ with period $1/|f_2 - f_1|$.)

Beats near a ratio. Let $f_2 = \frac{q}{p} f_1 (1 + \varepsilon)$ with $q/p$ in lowest terms and $\varepsilon$ small. Then harmonic $p$ of the upper tone and harmonic $q$ of the lower beat at

$$|p f_2 - q f_1| = q f_1|\varepsilon|,$$

and so do harmonics $kp$ and $kq$, at $k$ times that rate. For the 12-ET major third, $2^{4/12} = \frac54(1 + \varepsilon)$ with $\varepsilon \approx 0.00794$, and on C4 the lowest beat is $5 \cdot 261.63 \cdot 0.00794 \approx 10.38$ Hz. Notice the factor $q$: the more complex the ratio, the faster a given mistuning beats, but the higher (and usually weaker) the partials involved.

Roughness and the critical band

What happens when the beating gets faster? The next example holds one pure tone and slides a second one slowly upward from unison to a fifth. Listen to the stages.

At first you hear slow beats. As they speed up past about fifteen or twenty per second they stop being a pulse and become a buzzing, sandpapery roughness. Further apart, the roughness thins out and you hear two separate, smooth tones. For pure tones, roughness lives in a narrow zone just above unison.

How wide that zone is depends on register. Here is the same interval, a minor third, played low and then high.

Low down, the minor third is muddy and grainy; high up, it is clean. This is why bass parts favour wide intervals and why close chords sound murky at the bottom of the piano.

Helmholtz's theory. In Die Lehre von den Tonempfindungen (1863; in English On the Sensations of Tone), Hermann von Helmholtz proposed that dissonance is, at bottom, roughness: the fast beating of partials that lie close together. Consonant intervals are those whose partials either coincide exactly or lie far apart.

The critical band. Inside the inner ear, the basilar membrane works like a bank of overlapping filters, each responding to a band of nearby frequencies. Two pure tones within one band interact and produce beats or roughness; two tones in well-separated bands are heard as separate and smooth. The width of a band is called the critical bandwidth. One standard measure, the equivalent rectangular bandwidth of Glasberg and Moore (1990), is about 35 Hz at 100 Hz, 53 Hz at middle C, 72 Hz at 440 Hz, 133 Hz at 1000 Hz and 456 Hz at 4000 Hz. In hertz it grows with frequency, but more slowly than frequency itself, so in musical terms it is much wider at the bottom: from a note up to the note plus one ERB is about 685 cents at C2, but only 319 cents at C4.

Plomp and Levelt. Reinier Plomp and Willem Levelt (1965) asked listeners to rate the consonance of pairs of pure tones. Roughness was greatest when the tones were about a quarter of a critical bandwidth apart, and pairs more than about a critical bandwidth apart were judged consonant, whatever their ratio. For pure tones, in other words, there are no special intervals: only "too close" and "far enough". William Sethares later fitted their curve with a simple formula, used in the figure below and in this book's measurements.

Complex tones. Real tones have many partials, and every pair of partials (within one tone or across the two) contributes its own bit of roughness. Add up the contributions for two harmonic tones at every interval across an octave, and a striking curve appears.

The dips sit at the simple ratios of the harmonic series, to within a hundredth of a cent: 3/2 (the deepest), 4/3, 5/3, 5/4, 6/5, 8/5, and the septimal intervals (ratios involving the prime 7) 7/4, 7/5, 7/6 and 8/7. At each dip, some partials of the upper tone coincide exactly with partials of the lower one, and coinciding partials do not beat. Move slightly away from the ratio and those partials start to beat, and the roughness rises sharply. The octave, at the far right, is smoother than any interval inside it. (This is a measurement in a model: 8 partials with amplitudes falling by 0.88 per partial, lower tone C4. The pattern of minima is robust, but their depths change with the tones and register.)

Sethares' formula. For two sines with frequencies $f_1 \le f_2$ and amplitudes $a_1, a_2$, the model roughness is

$$d = \min(a_1, a_2)\,\big(e^{-b_1 s\Delta} - e^{-b_2 s\Delta}\big),$$

where

$$\Delta = f_2 - f_1,\qquad s = \frac{0.24}{0.0207 f_1 + 18.96},$$

with $b_1 = 3.5$, $b_2 = 5.75$. The factor $s$ rescales frequency differences by an approximation of the critical bandwidth at $f_1$. The roughness of a set of partials is the sum of $d$ over all pairs. These constants are fitted to data; the results are measurements in a model, not laws.

Where the peak is. Setting $\partial d/\partial\Delta = 0$ gives $b_1 e^{-b_1 s\Delta} = b_2 e^{-b_2 s\Delta}$, so

$$\Delta^* = \frac{\ln(b_2/b_1)}{(b_2 - b_1)\,s}.$$

At $f_1 = 261.63$ Hz, $s \approx 0.009846$ and $\Delta^* \approx 22.41$ Hz (142 c above C4). At C3 it is 19.92 Hz (245 c), at 440 Hz 25.80 Hz (99 c), at 1000 Hz 36.46 Hz (62 c). The peak value is the same everywhere, $(b_1/b_2)^{b_1/(b_2-b_1)} - (b_1/b_2)^{b_2/(b_2-b_1)} \approx 0.1808$ for unit amplitudes, because $s$ only rescales the axis.

The ERB formula. Glasberg and Moore's equivalent rectangular bandwidth is $\mathrm{ERB}(f) = 24.7\,(4.37 f/1000 + 1)$ Hz. Sethares' peak lies at 0.42 ERB at middle C. (Plomp and Levelt's "quarter of a critical band" used an older measure of the band, which is wider: about 100 Hz at middle C in Zwicker's tables, a quarter of which is about 25 Hz, against Sethares' peak at 22.4 Hz. The two statements agree roughly.)

Theorem (coinciding partials). Let two tones have harmonics $1, \dots, N$ on fundamentals $f$ and $\frac{m}{n}f$, with $m/n$ in lowest terms. Harmonic $j$ of the upper tone coincides with harmonic $k$ of the lower tone exactly when $(j, k) = (tn, tm)$ for a whole number $t$, so there are $\lfloor N/\max(m, n)\rfloor$ coincidences.

Proof. $j\cdot\frac{m}{n}f = kf$ means $jm = kn$; since $\gcd(m, n) = 1$, $n \mid j$; put $j = tn$, then $k = tm$. Both must be at most $N$. $\square$

Simple ratios (small $m$ and $n$) give many coincidences and push the other partials apart; complex ratios give few. With eight partials there are $\lfloor 8/2\rfloor = 4$ coincidences for the octave, $\lfloor 8/3\rfloor = 2$ for 3/2, $\lfloor 8/4\rfloor = 2$ for 4/3, one each for 5/4, 5/3 and 6/5, and none for 9/8. This is the structural reason the dips fall at simple ratios; their depths are measurements.

Harmonicity and virtual pitch

Roughness explains a great deal, but not everything. Here is a puzzle. The next example plays a tone on C3, then removes its lowest partial, then the next, then the next. Listen to the pitch.

Even when nothing at all is sounding at 130.81 Hz, you still hear C3. The ear and brain recognize the pattern of harmonics 2, 3, 4, 5, … and supply the fundamental that would explain them. This missing fundamental or virtual pitch is why a small loudspeaker that cannot reproduce low frequencies still lets you follow a bass line.

The same pattern-matching seems to operate on chords. A major triad tuned 4:5:6 is a slice of one harmonic series, and it tends to sound like one rich, fused tone; a chord whose notes fit no simple series sounds less unified.

Virtual pitch. In the 1840s August Seebeck's experiments with sirens showed that a tone could be heard at a pitch where there was almost no energy, which started a long debate with Ohm about what the ear measures. One influential modern account, Ernst Terhardt's theory of virtual pitch (1970s), holds that the auditory system resolves the lower partials and then estimates the fundamental of the harmonic series that best fits them; other models work instead with the periodicity of the waveform in time. On either account the pitch is a conclusion the auditory system draws, not a partial it detects.

Harmonicity of intervals and chords. Extend the idea: an interval or chord is harmonic to the degree that its notes fit a single harmonic series with a fundamental that is not too low. Take C4 as the lower note:

The major triad's implied fundamental is its own root, two octaves down; the minor triad's is a remote A♭. This is one classical account of why major sounds more settled than minor, and of why the root of a minor triad is a less compelling bass. It is a hypothesis about perception, with some support, not a settled fact.

Simplicity measures. Several numbers try to capture how "simple" a ratio is.

  • Leonhard Euler's gradus suavitatis ("degree of sweetness", 1739) is computed from the prime factors of the ratio's least common multiple (see Derive). Lower is sweeter.
  • Tenney height, named after James Tenney, is $\log_2(n \cdot d)$ for a ratio $n/d$ in lowest terms.

Both rank the intervals roughly as the dissonance curve does, and both say nothing about mistuned intervals: 3/2 is simple, but a fifth 1 cent away is, as a fraction, horribly complex. The next section repairs that.

Virtual pitch as an approximate common divisor. If partials are heard at $f_1, \dots, f_k$, a candidate fundamental $f_0$ explains them when every $f_i/f_0$ is close to a whole number. For exact harmonics the largest such $f_0$ is $\gcd(f_1, \dots, f_k)$: harmonics 2–10 of 130.81 Hz have greatest common divisor 130.81 Hz, so the period, and the virtual pitch, are unchanged by removing harmonic 1. (Smaller divisors such as $f_0/2$ also fit, which is one source of octave errors in pitch perception.)

Implied fundamentals. A chord tuned $a_1 : a_2 : \dots : a_k$ (whole numbers with no common factor) on lowest note $f$ is harmonics $a_1, \dots, a_k$ of $f/a_1$. For 4:5:6 on C4, $f/4 = 65.41$ Hz = C2. For 10:12:15 on C4, $f/10 = 26.16$ Hz, which is $12\log_2(26.16/440) + 69 \approx 20.14$ in MIDI terms: 13.69 c above A♭0.

Euler's gradus. For $n = \prod_p p^{e_p}$ define $\Gamma(n) = 1 + \sum_p e_p(p - 1)$. The gradus of a ratio $n/d$ in lowest terms is $\Gamma(nd)$, and of a chord $a_1 : \dots : a_k$ it is $\Gamma(\operatorname{lcm}(a_1, \dots, a_k))$. Examples: $\Gamma(6) = 1 + 1 + 2 = 4$ for 3/2; $\Gamma(20) = 1 + 2 + 4 = 7$ for 5/4. For the major triad, $\operatorname{lcm}(4, 5, 6) = 60$ and $\Gamma(60) = 1 + 2 + 2 + 4 = 9$. For the minor triad, $\operatorname{lcm}(10, 12, 15) = 60$ as well, so Euler's measure rates the two triads equal: a reminder that each of these measures captures only part of what we hear.

Tenney height $\log_2(nd)$ is the length of the ratio in a lattice where each prime step is weighted by $\log_2 p$ (Chapter 4).

Harmonic entropy

Listen to three thirds above middle C: a pure minor third, a pure major third, and between them a third that is exactly neither.

The outer two each sound like something: one sad, one bright, each settled. The middle one is not rough, exactly, but ambiguous. It sounds like a minor third that wants to be major, or the other way round. The ear seems to try to read every interval as some simple ratio, and when no single reading wins, the interval sounds uncertain.

The idea. Paul Erlich, in the 1990s, turned this into a measure called harmonic entropy. Hearing is not infinitely precise, so the ear perceives an interval as a slightly blurred position. Several simple ratios lie inside the blur, and each is a possible "interpretation" of the interval. If one ratio dominates, the interpretation is clear and the entropy is low; if several compete about equally, the interval is ambiguous and the entropy is high. Entropy is the standard measure of uncertainty from information theory; computed with natural logarithms, it is measured in nats.

The version computed in this book (the code is in reference/golden/explore.py) uses all fractions $n/d$ between 1 and 4 with $n \cdot d \le 12000$, weights simpler fractions more, and blurs each interval with a spread of 17 cents. Erlich and others have explored several variants of these choices, which differ in detail but agree on the main features.

The curve has deep valleys at the octave and the fifth, clear ones at 4/3, 5/3 and 5/4, and shallower ones at 6/5, 8/5, 9/5, 10/9 and the septimal intervals 7/4, 7/5, 7/6 and 9/7. Unlike Euler's gradus, it is continuous: an interval 1 cent from 3/2 still sits in the 3/2 valley. Unlike the roughness curve, it does not depend on the timbre at all; it is a model of how intervals are interpreted, not of how partials beat.

The golden intervals fall in revealing places. The golden third (366.91 c) sits at the 27th percentile, between the major and minor thirds it averages. The golden fifth (733.82 c) sits at the 69th percentile, high on the ridge just above the fifth's valley, and the golden sixth $\varphi$ (833.09 c) at the 65th. With harmonic timbres, the golden triad's instability is concentrated in its fifth (Chapter 26).

Definition. Let $\{r_j\}$ be a finite set of candidate ratios with sizes $c_j$ in cents and weights $w_j > 0$. For an interval of $x$ cents and a blur width $s$, set

$$p_j(x) = \frac{w_j\,e^{-(x - c_j)^2/2s^2}}{\sum_i w_i\,e^{-(x - c_i)^2/2s^2}},$$ $$H(x) = -\sum_j p_j(x)\ln p_j(x).$$

The $p_j(x)$ are the probabilities of each interpretation, and $H(x)$ is their Shannon entropy, in nats. In this book: $r_j = n/d$ with $\gcd(n, d) = 1$, $d \le n \le 4d$, $nd \le 12000$ (5054 ratios, 2526 of them within the octave); $w_j = 1/\sqrt{nd}$; and $s = 17$ c.

Properties.

  • $0 \le H(x) \le \ln N$ for $N$ candidates. $H$ is small when one $p_j$ is near 1, and largest when all are equal.
  • $H$ is a smooth function of $x$, so a slightly mistuned interval has nearly the same entropy as the pure one: the defect of the gradus is gone.
  • The local minima, computed on a 1-cent grid between 0 and 1200 c (excluding the endpoints), are at 181, 264, 317, 386, 435, 498, 583, 702, 811, 885, 969 and 1020 c: near 10/9, 7/6, 6/5, 5/4, 9/7, 4/3, 7/5, 3/2, 8/5, 5/3, 7/4 and 9/5.

Every number in this section depends on the three choices (the set, the weights, $s$), and is a measurement in that model.

Consonance depends on timbre

So far "consonant" has meant "close to a simple ratio". That is true for the tones of most instruments, whose partials form a harmonic series. But it is a fact about those tones, not about intervals in themselves. Change the partials, and the smooth intervals move.

The next example uses two kinds of tone. The first is harmonic. The second has its partials stretched so that its "octave" partial lies at 2.1 times the fundamental instead of 2. On each, you hear a true octave (2:1) and then a stretched octave (2.1:1).

On harmonic tones the true octave is smooth, with no beats, and the stretched one is sour. On the stretched tones it is the other way round: the true octave beats, and the "wrong" octave is pure. Now the same experiment with chords: a C major triad and a golden triad, first on harmonic tones and then on tones whose partials follow the golden ratio.

Sethares' point. In Tuning, Timbre, Spectrum, Scale (1998), William Sethares built on Plomp and Levelt to argue that consonance is a relation between an interval and a timbre. Compute the roughness curve for any spectrum and its dips fall wherever many partials of the two tones coincide. For harmonic tones these are the simple ratios. For other spectra they are elsewhere, and a tuning designed to match a spectrum makes its own intervals the smooth ones. (For comparison, a just major triad 4:5:6 on harmonic tones measures 1.19, against 1.32 for the 12-ET major triad.)

What timbre does not decide. Roughness is one ingredient of consonance. Harmonicity, entropy, and above all musical context and familiarity also shape what we hear as stable. A listener raised on harmonic instruments and major and minor chords brings expectations that no spectrum changes overnight. How the ear's preferences have changed over history is the subject of Chapter 35.

This is the acoustic foundation of the golden system. With harmonic timbres, golden chords are tense and make natural passing chords between Western ones. With golden-spectrum timbres the roles reverse: the golden triad becomes the place of rest and Western chords become the tension. Chapter 26 measures this timbre flip in detail (Chapter 26), and Chapter 17 follows the same idea through orchestration.

Theorem (self-matching spectra). Let a tone have partials at ratios $S = \{s_1, s_2, \dots\}$ to its fundamental. If $rS \subseteq S$ for some $r > 1$, i.e. every partial times $r$ is again a partial, then in the dyad at ratio $r$ every partial of the upper tone coincides with a partial of the lower tone (for an infinite spectrum; for a truncated one, every partial whose image stays in range).

Proof. The upper tone's partials are $r s_i f$, and $r s_i \in S$ by assumption. $\square$

Coinciding partials contribute zero roughness in the model, so such an $r$ is a natural candidate for a dip of the roughness curve. Three cases:

  • Harmonic, $S = \{1, 2, 3, \dots\}$: $rS \subseteq S$ exactly for whole numbers $r$, and the octave $r = 2$ is the first. For other simple ratios $m/n$ a fraction $1/n$ of the upper partials coincide (the coincidence theorem above).
  • Stretched, $S = \{k^{a} : k \ge 1\}$ with $a = \log_2 2.1$: since $2^a = 2.1$, we get $2.1\,k^a = (2k)^a \in S$. The stretched octave 2.1 is self-matching, and 2 is not.
  • Golden, $S = \{2^m\varphi^n : m, n \ge 0\}$: both $2S \subseteq S$ and $\varphi S \subseteq S$. The golden sixth $\varphi$ and its octave relatives become "pure" intervals of this timbre.

The numbers in the tables are measurements in the Sethares model; the self-matching property is a theorem. Together they make precise the sense in which consonance depends on timbre.

1. Two strings sound 440 Hz and 444 Hz. What is the beat rate? If you tighten the second string and the beats speed up, which way should you turn the peg?

Answer

4 beats per second. Speeding up means the gap grew, so the second string was already sharp: loosen it.

2. The 12-ET major third C4–E4 beats at 10.38 Hz. How fast does C3–E3 beat? And C5–E5?

Answer

Beat rates scale with frequency: about 5.19 Hz an octave down and 20.76 Hz an octave up.

3. Derive the location of the peak of Sethares' curve, $\Delta^* = \ln(b_2/b_1)/((b_2 - b_1)s)$, and evaluate it at $f_1 = 440$ Hz.

Answer

Differentiate $e^{-b_1 s\Delta} - e^{-b_2 s\Delta}$ and set it to zero: $b_1 e^{-b_1 s \Delta} = b_2 e^{-b_2 s\Delta}$, so $e^{(b_2 - b_1)s\Delta} = b_2/b_1$. At 440 Hz, $s = 0.24/(0.0207\cdot440 + 18.96) \approx 0.008551$ and $\Delta^* \approx 0.4964/(2.25 \cdot 0.008551) \approx 25.80$ Hz.

4. With eight harmonics per tone, how many partials coincide in a just major sixth 5/3? In a just whole tone 9/8?

Answer

$\lfloor 8/5\rfloor = 1$ for 5/3 (harmonic 3 of the upper tone meets harmonic 5 of the lower), and $\lfloor 8/9\rfloor = 0$ for 9/8.

5. Compute Euler's gradus for 6/5 and for the chord 4:5:6:7.

Answer

$\Gamma(30) = 1 + 1 + 2 + 4 = 8$. For 4:5:6:7, $\operatorname{lcm} = 420 = 2^2\cdot3\cdot5\cdot7$, so $\Gamma = 1 + 2 + 2 + 4 + 6 = 15$.

6. (Listening.) In the timbre-flip example, which chord sounds more at rest in the first half, and which in the second? Does your answer agree with the measured roughness?

Answer

The measurements predict C major in the first half (1.32 against 1.50) and the golden triad in the second (2.08 against 3.17). Your own judgment is a measurement too, and it may also reflect familiarity with major chords.
