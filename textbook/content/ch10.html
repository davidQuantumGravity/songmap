Home: the tonic

Music in a key has a home. You can feel it without being told: a phrase that stops somewhere else sounds unfinished, and a phrase that returns sounds complete. Listen to the same three chords twice. The first time the music stops on the third chord and simply waits. The second time it goes one chord further, and that chord is home.

That home chord is the tonic. Everything else in a key is heard in relation to it: some chords lean away from it, some pull back toward it, and some can stand in for it. This web of relations is called harmonic function.

The tonic is the first degree of the key's scale (C in C major) and the triad built on it (I). The other degrees have traditional names too:

The names describe positions around the tonic. The dominant is a fifth above it; the subdominant is a fifth below it (not "just below the dominant"); the mediant and submediant lie halfway to them, a third above and a third below.

Three functions. A widely used simplification sorts the diatonic chords into three families:

FunctionRoleMain chordSubstitutes
tonic (T)rest, arrivalIvi, and sometimes iii
subdominant or predominant (S)departure, preparationIVii
dominant (D)tension that resolves to TV (V7)vii°

A substitute shares two of its three notes with the main chord: vi (A C E) shares C and E with I (C E G); ii (D F A) shares F and A with IV (F A C); vii° (B D F) shares B and D with V (G B D). The basic shape of a tonal phrase is then T – S – D – T: home, departure, tension, return.

Is the tonic natural or learned? Both ideas have support. The tonic triad is a major triad, which copies harmonics 4, 5 and 6, and its root is the bass that the dominant's root, a perfect fifth above, points to. But what counts as home also depends on familiarity with a style. In Carol Krumhansl and Edward Kessler's probe-tone experiments (1982), listeners rated how well each pitch fitted after a passage in a key; the tonic fitted best, followed by the fifth and the third. Those ratings measure an expectation shaped by exposure, and Chapter 35 returns to the question of how much of it is learned.

With harmonic timbres the golden triad is tense, so in the golden system it is usually a passing chord between Western homes. On golden-spectrum timbres it becomes the smoothest chord, and golden harmony can be home. A golden home moves differently from a Western one: by the golden circle, by planing and by breathing, with Western chords only visiting. See why golden homes move differently.

Proposition (common tones of diatonic triads). Two triads of the same diatonic scale whose roots are $k$ scale steps apart share 3, 0, 2, 1, 1, 2, 0 notes for $k = 0, 1, \dots, 6$.

Proof. Number the degrees by $\mathbb{Z}_7$. The triad on degree $j$ is $\{j, j+2, j+4\}$. Compare $\{0, 2, 4\}$ with $\{k, k+2, k+4\}$: for $k = 2$ the second set is $\{2, 4, 6\}$ (two shared), for $k = 4$ it is $\{4, 6, 1\}$ (one shared), for $k = 1$ it is $\{1, 3, 5\}$ (none). The cases $k = 5, 3, 6$ are the reverses of $k = 2, 4, 1$. $\square$

So chords a third apart share two notes, chords a fifth apart share one, and chords a step apart share none. This is the arithmetic behind the substitutes in the table: each lies a third from its main chord.

Exercise 10.1. Which two diatonic triads of C major share two notes with iii (E G B)? Which function families do they belong to, and why is iii often described as ambiguous?

Answer

The triads a third away: I (C E G, sharing E and G) and V (G B D, sharing G and B). iii therefore leans toward both the tonic family and the dominant family, and its function depends on context: iii going to vi often acts as part of a descending chain, while iii in place of I acts as a weak tonic.

Pull: the dominant and the leading tone

Sing up a major scale and stop one note short of the top. The note you stop on, the seventh, seems to lean on the octave above it, so strongly that many listeners finish the scale in their heads. That note is called the leading tone, because it leads to the tonic.

The dominant chord, G B D in C major, contains that leading tone. Add one more note, F, and you get the dominant seventh G7, which pulls even harder. The pull is concentrated in two notes, B and F, an interval called the tritone. Listen to them alone: they squeeze together into C and E, or spread apart into E and C.

The leading tone is the seventh degree, a semitone below the tonic. Its size depends on the tuning:

The Pythagorean leading tone is the smallest of the three (Chapter 5). Singers and string players, who can bend pitch, often play leading tones slightly high in expressive performance, which narrows the step to the tonic.

The dominant triad V contains the leading tone (B) and the second degree (D), which both move to the tonic triad by step: B up to C, D down to C or up to E. Its root G moves to C by a fifth (or a fourth, by octave equivalence), traditionally regarded as the strongest root motion in tonal harmony. Jean-Philippe Rameau treated root motion by fifth as the model of harmonic progression in his Traité de l'harmonie (1722).

The dominant seventh G7 (G B D F) adds the fourth degree, F, which falls a semitone to E. Now the chord contains B and F, a diminished fifth (600 c). In resolution the two notes move by semitone in opposite directions:

  • B–F (diminished fifth) closes inward to C–E (major third, 400 c);
  • F–B (augmented fourth, its inversion) opens outward to E–C (minor sixth, 800 c).

In the smallest four-voice V7 → I, the voices move G → G, B → C, D → C and F → E: four semitones of motion in all, with the leading tone rising and the seventh falling. The triad without its root, vii° (B D F), keeps both tendency tones, which is why it acts as a dominant too.

Why one dominant seventh per key. Among the diatonic seventh chords of C major only G7 has the dominant quality (previous chapter), and the scale contains only one tritone, B–F. A dominant seventh therefore names its key more clearly than any triad can. But the tritone itself is symmetric: B–F and F–C♭ are the same two pitch classes. The other dominant seventh containing it is D♭7 (D♭ F A♭ C♭), and jazz players use that fact as tritone substitution: D♭7 can replace G7 before C.

In 36-ET the leading tone can rise to the tonic through two sixth-steps: B → ^B → vC → C. That is the soprano of the golden six-four, G – vE♭T13/G – vCg/G – C, in which the second degree also rises D → vE♭ → ^E♭ → E and the golden triad vCg sits a sixth-step below the tonic, over the dominant bass. In the upper voices each chord of this path is exactly one semitone of total motion from the next; the bass waits on G and moves only at the arrival. See the golden six-four and the golden authentic cadence.

Proposition (two dominants per tritone). Each tritone $\{x, x+6\} \subset \mathbb{Z}_{12}$ lies in exactly two dominant seventh chords, whose roots are a tritone apart.

Proof. The dominant seventh on $r$ is $\{r, r+4, r+7, r+10\}$; its only tritone is $\{r+4, r+10\}$ ($r$ and $r+7$ have no partner a tritone away inside the chord). So $\{r+4, r+10\} = \{x, x+6\}$, giving $r = x - 4$ or $r = x - 10 = x + 2$, and these differ by 6. For B–F ($x = 11$): $r = 7$ (G) or $r = 1$ (D♭). $\square$

Leading-tone sizes. $1200\log_2(256/243) = 90.22$ c and $1200\log_2(16/15) = 111.73$ c. The just leading tone sits at $15/8 = 1088.27$ c above the tonic, 111.73 c below the octave.

The golden six-four as a uniform path. The bass waits on G until the arrival. The upper voices move from (G3, B3, D4) to (G3, C4, E4), displacements $d = (0, +1, +2)$ semitones, and the strict path splits each displacement into three equal parts: $P_k = X + k\,d$ in sixth-steps, $k = 0, 1, 2, 3$. Each step moves $0 + 1 + 2 = 3$ sixth-steps, one semitone of motion in total. In $\mathbb{Z}_{36}$ (C = 0), $P_1 = \{21, 34, 8\}$ = G ^B vE♭ is the bronze triad on vE♭ ($8 + 13 = 21$, $8 + 26 = 34$), and $P_2 = \{21, 35, 10\}$ = G vC ^E♭ is the golden triad on vC ($35 + 11 \equiv 10$, $35 + 22 \equiv 21$). The full classification of such paths is in Chapter 27.

Exercise 10.2. Which dominant seventh shares its tritone with C7? Spell both chords.

Answer

C7 = C E G B♭ has the tritone E–B♭. The roots are $x - 4$ and $x + 2$ with $x = 4$ (E): 0 (C) and 6 (F♯). So F♯7 = F♯ A♯ C♯ E, also spelled G♭7 = G♭ B♭ D♭ F♭. G♭7 is the tritone substitute for C7 on its way to F.

Departure: the subdominant

If the dominant pulls toward home, the subdominant gently leads away from it. Moving from C to F feels like opening a door. Moving back from F to C is the softest kind of arrival there is, the "amen" at the end of a hymn. Borrow F minor in place of F major and the same return turns bittersweet.

The subdominant IV lies a fifth below the tonic, so the tonic is IV's dominant. Moving I → IV is like moving V → I one step lower on the circle of fifths: the tonic gives up its status and becomes a dominant. That is the sense in which IV is a departure. Because IV contains the tonic note C, the return IV → I has a common tone and no leading tone; it is gentler than V → I.

Predominant. In practice IV and ii prepare the dominant. The progression ii6 – V (F A D → G B D) or IV – V is the standard approach to a cadence, and ii often carries a seventh (ii7, Dm7). The two chords share F and A, and both contain the fourth degree F, which becomes the seventh of V7 or moves to E.

Three triads make the scale. The tonic, subdominant and dominant triads of C major are C E G, F A C and G B D. Together they contain all seven notes of the scale, which is why I, IV and V can harmonize any melody that stays in the key. The words dominant and subdominant are older (Rameau used dominante and sous-dominante), but the idea of tonic, dominant and subdominant as three functions, categories that a chord can have, is due to Hugo Riemann (Vereinfachte Harmonielehre, 1893). The golden counterpart of the amen holds the subdominant in the bass while a golden triad passes: F – vFg/F – C, with vFg = vF ^A♭ C. On the way up, I → IV passes through vFg too, since vFg is the one strict golden passing chord between C and F. See golden plagal glow.

Dominant and subdominant as mirror images. On the circle of fifths ($k \mapsto 7k \bmod 12$ places C, G, D, … in order; Chapter 8), V and IV are the two neighbors of I. The reflection $p \mapsto -p$ (around C) exchanges G (7) and F (5), so it exchanges dominant and subdominant. It also turns C major (C E G) into F minor (C A♭ F) and G major into B♭ minor: the mirror of "a major tonic and its dominant" is "a minor tonic and its subdominant". This is Riemann's dualism in one line of arithmetic.

The just scale. The triads $\tfrac43(1, \tfrac54, \tfrac32) = (\tfrac43, \tfrac53, 2)$, $(1, \tfrac54, \tfrac32)$ and $\tfrac32(1, \tfrac54, \tfrac32) = (\tfrac32, \tfrac{15}{8}, \tfrac94)$, folded into one octave, give the seven ratios of the table ($\tfrac94 \to \tfrac98$). Cents are $1200\log_2$ of each ratio. The scale has two sizes of whole tone, $9/8 = 203.91$ c (C–D) and $10/9 = 182.40$ c (D–E); their ratio is the syntonic comma 81/80 (Chapter 7).

Exercise 10.3. Harmonize the melody E D C (degrees 3 2 1) with one chord per note using only I, IV and V. How many solutions are there if every chord must contain its melody note?

Answer

E lies only in I (C E G). D lies only in V (G B D). C lies in I and IV. So the solutions are I V I and I V IV: two. The first is an authentic cadence; the second ends on IV, a weak, open ending (V → IV is uncommon in classical style but frequent in blues and rock).

Cadences

A cadence is the musical equivalent of punctuation: the chords at the end of a phrase that tell you whether the sentence is finished. Listen for four kinds: the full stop, the comma, the soft "amen", and the surprise.

CadenceChordsIn CEffect

imperfect authentic (IAC)V – I with an inversion, or the melody on the third or fifthG – C/E, or G – C with E on topweaker stop
half (HC)any chord – Vii6 – G, IV – G, I – Gcomma, question

deceptive (DC)V – viG – Amsurprise, the phrase continues
Phrygian halfiv6 – V in minorFm/A♭ – G (in C minor)half cadence with a half-step descent in the bass

The deceptive cadence works because vi shares two notes with I (C and E): the leading tone still rises to C, but the bass, rising G → A instead of falling to C, denies the arrival.

The cadential six-four. Classical cadences often decorate V with a six-four chord over the dominant bass: C/G – G – C (I⁶₄ – V – I). The "C chord" here is heard as a decoration of G: over the bass G, the E and the C are a sixth and a fourth that resolve down to a fifth and a third, D and B. Many textbooks therefore write it as V⁶₄–⁵₃.

Each Western cadence has a golden counterpart in 36-ET, built from the passing-chord theorems of Part VII:
  • authentic: G – vE♭T13/G – vCg/G – C, the golden six-four (Chapter 29);
  • plagal: F – vFg/F – C (golden plagal glow);
  • deceptive: G – vA♭g – A♭g – Am, two golden chords on ♭VI, which foreshadow ♭VI, the other common deceptive goal (the golden deceptive cadence).

The deceptive cadence and the quality theorem. V (G) and vi (Am) have roots a step apart and opposite qualities. By the quality theorem (Chapter 27), no strict golden passing chord joins them; only the natural paths of System 2 do, through the two golden chords on A♭ (the golden escalator).

Exercise 10.4 (listening). In the cadence example, the second phrase (I – ii6 – V) and the fourth (I – IV – V – vi) both avoid ending on I. Which sounds more finished, and why?

Answer

Many listeners hear the deceptive cadence as more "arrived": vi is a tonic substitute that shares C and E with I, and the leading tone has resolved to C. The half cadence stops on V itself, with the leading tone unresolved, so it sounds like a question. But neither is a full stop: the deceptive cadence invites the phrase to continue toward a real I.

Neo-Riemannian moves and the Tonnetz

Some of the most magical chord changes, the kind often heard in film scores, do not follow the rules of function at all. They move from one major or minor chord to another by changing just one note, a little. C major to C minor: the E slips down. C major to E minor: the C slips down to B. C major to A minor: the G steps up to A.

Chain the first two moves, alternating, and you travel through six chords and come back home, each chord a semitone's nudge from the last. The sound is smooth and strange at once: none of these chords belongs to a single key.

The three moves are named after Hugo Riemann's terms (the theory built on them in the 1980s and 1990s, by David Lewin, Richard Cohn and others, is called neo-Riemannian). Each one exchanges a major triad and a minor triad that share two notes:

MoveNameFrom CNotes keptNote movedFrom Cm
PparallelCmC, G (the fifth)E → E♭, a semitoneC
Lleading-tone exchangeEmE, G (the minor third)C → B, a semitoneA♭
RrelativeAmC, E (the major third)G → A, a whole toneE♭

Each move undoes itself: doing P twice returns to the start, and the same for L and R. The moves are defined by the shape of the chord, not by any key, so they work the same way on every triad.

The Tonnetz. These moves have a beautiful picture. Arrange the twelve pitch classes on a triangular grid, with perfect fifths running horizontally and major thirds running diagonally. Then every small triangle is a triad, major triangles pointing one way and minor triangles the other, and two triangles that share an edge share two notes. P, L and R are the three ways to flip a triangle across one of its edges. The lattice, the Tonnetz ("tone network"), appears in Chapter 4 beside the ratio lattice of just intonation; here its points are 12-ET pitch classes.

Cycles. Alternating two moves walks along a strip of the Tonnetz:

AlternationChords visited before returningStarting from C
P, L6 (a hexatonic cycle)C Cm A♭ A♭m E Em
P, R8 (an octatonic cycle)C Cm E♭ E♭m F♯ F♯m A Am
L, R24 (all major and minor triads)C Em G Bm D F♯m A C♯m …

The hexatonic cycle uses only six pitch classes (C, E♭, E, G, A♭, B), the notes of the hexatonic scale. That scale is the union of two augmented triads, C E A♭ and E♭ G B, and the cycle weaves between them: its three major chords are semitone neighbors of C E A♭ and its three minor chords are semitone neighbors of E♭ G B (Chapter 9). The octatonic cycle uses the eight notes of an octatonic scale. The L–R cycle is a kind of circle of fifths that alternates major and minor. These cycles return in Chapter 18.

Golden passing chords and the Tonnetz fit together exactly: a strict golden passing chord exists between two triads precisely when they are two neo-Riemannian moves apart. From C, the six two-move destinations are A♭ (P then L), E♭ (P then R), E (L then P), G (L then R), A (R then P) and F (R then L), the same-quality partners a third or a fifth away, each joined by one golden chord (Cg, ^E♭g, Eg, vCg, ^Cg and vFg). Single moves change quality, so they never qualify. See the quality theorem and mediant gold.

Definitions. Write a major triad as $(r, +)$ and a minor triad as $(r, -)$, with root $r \in \mathbb{Z}_{12}$. Then $$P(r,\pm) = (r, \mp),\qquad L(r,+) = (r+4, -),\ L(r,-) = (r+8, +),\qquad R(r,+) = (r+9, -),\ R(r,-) = (r+3, +).$$ Each is an involution ($P^2 = L^2 = R^2 = 1$) and each commutes with every transposition $T_k$.

Theorem. The group generated by $P$, $L$ and $R$, acting on the 24 major and minor triads, has 24 elements and is a dihedral group.

Proof sketch. $L$ and $R$ already generate it, because $P = RLRLRLR$ (check it on C and on Cm, then use commutation with transposition; it was also checked on all 24 triads). A group generated by two involutions is dihedral, of order twice the order of their product. Applying $L$ then $R$ to C gives G: the composite keeps each triad's quality and moves major triads up a fifth and minor triads down a fifth (Cm → A♭ → Fm), so it has order 12, and the group has order 24. It acts simply transitively: for any two triads there is exactly one element taking one to the other. (Every step was also confirmed by computer.) $\square$

The cycle lengths in the table follow: the composite "P then L" has order 3, "P then R" order 4 and "L then R" order 12, and each alternation visits twice as many triads as the order.

Theorem (golden passing chords are two moves apart; checked exhaustively for this book). For distinct major or minor triads $X$ and $Y$, a strict golden passing chord exists between $X$ and $Y$ if and only if the shortest sequence of moves $P$, $L$, $R$ from $X$ to $Y$ has length exactly 2.

Proof. Both conditions commute with transposition and with inversion, so it suffices to compare them from C and from Cm. Breadth-first search gives the distances from C: Cm, Em, Am at 1; A, A♭, E, E♭, F, G at 2; the other minor triads except B♭m at 3; B, B♭, C♯, D, F♯ at 4; B♭m at 5. The six triads at distance 2 are exactly the major triads whose roots lie a minor third, major third or fifth from C, which by the quality theorem are the major triads joined to C by a strict golden passing chord; enumerating all stepwise uniform paths from C confirms one golden chord for each of these six and none for any other triad. The minor case is the mirror image (and was enumerated the same way). $\square$

Exercise 10.5. Compute P, L and R of A minor. Then find the chord reached from C by applying R, then L, then R, then L.

Answer

$P(\mathrm{Am}) = $ A major; $L(\mathrm{Am}) = (9 + 8, +) = $ F major (A C E → A C F: E rises to F); $R(\mathrm{Am}) = $ C major. From C: R → Am, L → F, R → Dm, L → B♭. Each "R then L" moves down a fifth (C → F → B♭), the mirror of "L then R".

Exercise 10.6. Show that the six chords of the PL cycle through C use only six pitch classes, and name them.

Answer

C (C E G), Cm (C E♭ G), A♭ (A♭ C E♭), A♭m (A♭ C♭ E♭, with C♭ = B), E (E G♯ B, with G♯ = A♭), Em (E G B). The union is {C, E♭, E, G, A♭, B}: six pitch classes, alternating steps of 3 and 1 semitones. This is the hexatonic scale, and it is the union of two augmented triads, C E A♭ and E♭ G B.
