Overshoot and settle: elastic resolution

Part VII joined Western chords through golden chords placed between them: the passing chords of Chapter 27 and Chapter 28. Tradition knows many other ways for a dissonance to meet a consonance. A note can lean on its target from above, wait over it, arrive too early, step away and come back, or circle around it (Chapter 12). This chapter turns each of those embellishments into a way of joining golden and Western chords, computes which ones exist exactly, and ends with a catalogue of every strategy in the book.

The first strategy is the author's own idea. When all the voices make a big jump, let them fly a little past the target chord, onto a golden chord just beyond it, and then settle back: an elastic response that overshoots and comes to rest at home. Listen to a dominant leaping down a fifth onto C major three times: straight, with a small overshoot, and with a full one.

The overshoot chord lasts one beat and the arrival three. Because the golden chord comes first and C major second, the arrival is a release twice over: a leap that lands, and a golden bend that straightens into the deepest rest there is (Chapter 31). Here is a whole phrase built this way, every chord change a leap that lands just beyond its target.

Definition. Let a voiced chord $X$ move to $Y$, voice $i$ by $D_i$ sixth-steps. An overshoot chord is $G = Y + E$ where every moving voice continues past its target in the direction it was going, by $|E_i| \in \{1, 2, 3\}$ sixth-steps (at most a semitone), and every held voice stays ($E_i = 0$ when $D_i = 0$). The settle $G \to Y$ moves each voice back by $E_i$. If some moving voices land exactly and the others overshoot, call the overshoot partial; if every moving voice overshoots, full.

There is a neat way to see what this means. A passing chord lies between $X$ and $Y$: on a smoothest path from one to the other. An overshoot chord is the other way round: the target $Y$ lies between $X$ and $G$, so $Y$ is a passing chord on the way from $X$ to the overshoot. The extra distance travelled is exactly twice the settle, $d(X,G) + d(G,Y) - d(X,Y) = 2\,d(G,Y)$: the elastic stretch, counted out and back.

Western mirror. The distance is the voice-leading distance of Chapter 14, total motion in sixth-steps with each voice keeping its identity, and "between" means lying on a shortest path in the chord space of Chapter 14. Passing chords (Chapter 12's passing chords, Part VII's golden ones) are the points between; overshoot, escape and neighbour chords are the three ways of leaving that path.

The parallel rule. The commonest big jump moves all voices the same way: root-position blocks leaping a fourth, a fifth, a third or an octave. Then the answer is the same whatever the size of the leap, and it has a clean form. Write π(Y) for the golden projection of Y, its nearest golden triad (π(C) = Cg, π(Am) = vAg; Chapter 35). Then:

  • the partial overshoot is π(Y) planed one sixth-step in the direction of motion: ^Cg when the voices rise into C, vCg when they fall into it (settle 3);
  • the full overshoot is π(Y) planed two sixth-steps: vC♯g rising, ^Bg falling (settle 6).

It is as if the golden image of the target were carried along by the momentum of the leap, one or two sixth-steps past home. Planing is one of the golden home's own moves (Chapter 31), so the elastic is made of golden motion.

Two of these chords already have a job elsewhere in the book. vCg, the partial overshoot of every parallel falling leap into C, is the shadow that passes into C in the smooth golden cadence (Chapter 29): the same chord is the passing chord of the smooth V → I and the overshoot of the leaping one. ^Bg, the full overshoot of every parallel falling leap, is the last golden chord of the natural path B♭ – vBg – ^Bg – C, the golden backdoor of §39.7. Its settle into C is a rise in all three voices.

The projection as an overshoot. Cg itself, the nearest golden chord of all, is reached only by converging voices: the root held, the third approached from above and the fifth from below. Among Western triads the sources are C itself (a voice exchange, E and G trading places), F (A falls to E while F rises to G, a contrary-motion plagal cadence), F minor, C minor, A♭ and A minor. A converging approach overshoots onto the projection: E falls a little too far to vE, G rises a little too far to ^G, and both straighten.

Stretching the passing path does not work. A natural guess is to stretch the uniform path of Chapter 27 one step past its end: if $P_k = X + k\,d$ with $Y = P_3$, try $P_4 = Y + d$, the target pushed a third of the way further. This is never golden when the source is a major, minor or augmented triad, whatever the target and however far the voices move (a theorem, below). Golden overshoot is always uneven: the voices must fly past by different amounts, which is the same lesson System 2 taught for passing (Chapter 28).

Damped oscillation

A real spring does not stop after one overshoot: it swings back past the rest point, less each time. Could the voices do the same, overshoot onto one golden chord, undershoot onto another and settle? Strictly, no. If $G = Y + E$ is golden, the mirror image $Y - E$ is never golden (theorem below), and even allowing every voice to swing back by a smaller amount, no undershoot of a golden overshoot is golden within a semitone (exhaustive: 45 candidates for C, 45 for C minor, none golden and none even tertian).

A looser elastic does exist. If only one voice swings back while the others creep in or stop, there are 11 two-stage settles into C (and 11 into C minor), and in every one of them exactly one voice reverses. The one that follows the full overshoot of a falling leap is ^Bg → Cg → C (8 sixth-steps of motion against 6 for settling at once). The fifth swings below G and back above it (vG, ^G, G) while the third creeps up (E♭, vE, E) and the root arrives at once. There are even four three-stage settles into C in which one voice swings twice; in three of them its amplitudes are 2, 1, 1 sixth-steps, three Fibonacci numbers in a row (the fourth goes 2, 2, 1; exhaustive within a semitone per voice, each stage no wider than the last). For example vA♭g → ^Bg → Cg → C, whose fifth goes vA♭, vG, ^G, G (G + 2, G − 1, G + 1, G).

The spring, as a metaphor. An underdamped spring released from a stretch swings with extrema that shrink by a constant factor $e^{-\pi\zeta/\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}}$ every half period, where $\zeta$ is the damping ratio; the same factor is its first overshoot as a fraction of the step. A decay of $1/\varphi$ per swing means $\pi\zeta/\sqrt{1-\zeta^2} = \ln\varphi = 0.4812$, so $\zeta = 0.1514$, and such a spring would overshoot a leap of a fourth (15 sixth-steps) by $15/\varphi = 9.27$ sixth-steps, more than a minor third. Musical overshoot is far smaller: about 2 sixth-steps on a fourth, a fraction 0.133, which a spring produces with $\zeta = 0.540$; its next swing would be under 2% of the leap. So the elastic is a metaphor, not a model: real voices overshoot little, and their swings, when they swing at all, go 2, 1, 1 rather than shrinking smoothly.
Precedents. The overshoot chord is an appoggiatura from beyond: Chapter 12 defined the appoggiatura as a note reached by leap and left by step, usually in the opposite direction, which is exactly what each overshooting voice does. The counterpoint tradition taught that a leap should be followed by stepwise motion the other way, and Leonard Meyer called the resulting shape gap-fill (Explaining Music, 1973; Chapter 13). Paul von Hippel and David Huron (2000) showed that much of this reversal is regression toward the middle of the range, which suits the elastic picture: the further a voice flies, the more room it has to come back. Steve Larson's Musical Forces (2012) described melodic expectation with the physical metaphors of gravity, magnetism and inertia; the overshoot is the inertia of a leap, and the settle its pull home.

Lemma (betweenness). For one voice with pitches $x \to g \to y$, all distinct, exactly one of the three lies between the other two: $g$ (passing), $y$ (overshoot) or $x$ (escape). With fixed voice assignment and the $L^1$ norm, $d(X,G) + d(G,Y) = d(X,Y) + \sum_i e_i$ where $e_i = 0$ for a passing voice, $2|g_i - y_i|$ for an overshooting voice and $2|g_i - x_i|$ for an escaping one. Proof. On a line, $|g-x| + |y-g| - |y-x|$ is zero when $g$ is between and twice the distance from $g$ to the nearer endpoint otherwise. Summing over voices gives the formula. ∎

Theorem (parallel rule). Let every voice approach a major or minor triad $Y$ in the same direction. The only full golden overshoot is $\pi(Y)$ planed two sixth-steps in that direction, and the only additional partial one is $\pi(Y)$ planed one sixth-step. Proof. Rising overshoots of $+1, +2, +3$ sixth-steps put a voice on layers 1, 2, 0 (falling ones: 2, 1, 0). A golden triad has one note per layer (the layer theorem), so a full overshoot uses each size once, in one of $3! = 6$ orders. For C major rising, the orders (root, third, fifth) give $(2,1,3)$: vC♯g; $(1,2,3)$: A♭T13 (bronze); $(3,1,2)$: C♯s (silver); the other three are not tertian. A partial overshoot has one landing voice (layer 0) and sizes 1 and 2 on the others, which for C rising gives only ^Cg. The layer argument holds for every target; which of the orders is golden depends on the triad, and that part is an exhaustive check: the script tests both statements for all 24 major and minor triads and both directions. ∎

Theorem (no proportional overshoot). If $X$ is a major, minor or augmented triad, $Y$ any chord on the 12-ET layer and $d = (Y - X)/3$ voice by voice, then $P_4 = Y + d$ is not golden. Proof. Suppose $P_4 = G$ is golden, with notes $g, g+11, g+22$ in some voice order. Then $d = G - Y$ and $X = Y - 3d = 4Y - 3G \pmod{36}$. Each $Y_i$ is a multiple of 3, so $4Y_i \equiv 12\,(Y_i/3) \in \{0, 12, 24\}$, and $-3G_i \in \{-3g, -3g+3, -3g+6\}$ because $33 \equiv -3$ and $66 \equiv -6 \pmod{36}$. In semitones, $X_i \equiv 4a_i - g + c_i \pmod{12}$ with $(c_i)$ a permutation of $(0, 1, 2)$. Reduced mod 4, the three notes of $X$ have three different residues. But a major triad $\{r, r+4, r+7\}$ has residues $r, r, r+3$, a minor triad $r, r+3, r+3$, an augmented triad one residue only. ∎ (Diminished and suspended triads do have three residues, and for them golden $P_4$ chords exist; the script finds them by brute force. By reversal, the uniform backswing $X - d$ before a major or minor $Y$ is never golden either. The census over all 2,616 voice leadings from C or Cm to a major or minor triad with each voice moving up to an octave finds no golden $P_4$.)

Theorem (no golden mirror). If $Y$ lies on one layer and $G = Y + E$ is golden, then $Y - E$ is not golden. Proof. Suppose $G' = Y - E$ is golden too, $G_i = g + 11\sigma_i$ and $G'_i = g' + 11\tau_i$ with $\sigma, \tau$ permutations of $(0,1,2)$. Then $G_i + G'_i = 2Y_i$, a constant mod 6 since $Y$ lies on one layer. As $11 \equiv -1 \pmod 6$, $\sigma_i + \tau_i$ is constant mod 6, hence constant (it lies in $0, \dots, 4$), and since $\tau = c - \sigma$ must be a permutation of $(0,1,2)$, $c = 2$. Then $G_i + G'_i = g + g' + 22$ for every $i$, so $2Y_i$ is constant mod 36 and $Y$ has at most two pitch classes (equal or a tritone apart): not a triad. ∎ The script confirms it over all 1,320 voice assignments with $|E_i| \le 6$.

Exhaustive results (reference/golden/elastic.py §2–§6): the overshoot tables (8 full and 9 partial patterns for C, the same counts for C minor; the settle is always the smoothest voice leading back); the 11 relaxed two-stage settles into C and into C minor, each with exactly one reversing voice; and the sources of every golden overshoot of C among the 24 major and minor triads. The four three-stage settles into C (four into C minor as well) with a voice swinging twice were enumerated separately for this chapter in the same way: three golden stages, each overshoot at most a semitone and no wider than the stage before, voice by voice.

Exercise 39.1. The voices of F4 A4 C5 leap down a fourth to C4 E4 G4. Name the partial and the full overshoot chords, with their notes, and the settle cost of each.

Answer

All voices fall, so by the parallel rule the partial overshoot is π(C) = Cg planed down one sixth-step, vCg = vC4 ^E♭4 G4 (root 1 and third 2 sixth-steps past, the fifth lands; settle 1 + 2 = 3), and the full one is Cg planed down two, ^Bg = ^B3 E♭4 vG4 (settle 2 + 3 + 1 = 6).

Exercise 39.2. For the parallel leap C4 E4 G4 → F4 A4 C5, compute the stretched chord $P_4 = Y + d$. What kind of chord is it, and why could you have predicted that it is not golden?

Answer

Every voice moves 5 semitones, so $d = (5, 5, 5)$ sixth-steps and $P_4$ = vG4 vB4 vD5: a G major triad on the v layer, a Western chord moved off the grid. A parallel motion keeps the shape of the chord, and stretching it only transposes it. The general reason is the theorem: the source C major has two notes with the same residue mod 4.

Golden appoggiatura chords

An appoggiatura arrives on the beat, unprepared, leaning on the note it will become. A whole chord can do the same: the tonic can arrive bent, its third a sixth-step low and its fifth a sixth-step high, and straighten a moment later. That bent tonic is Cg. Listen to it on the downbeat after G, then to the dominant arriving the same way, then to a golden cadential six-four that falls into the dominant.

Definition. A golden appoggiatura chord is a golden chord on a strong beat, approached by leap or without preparation, that resolves by sixth-steps into a Western chord on the following weaker beat. It is the chordal form of the appoggiatura of Chapter 12, and its Western ancestors are the appoggiatura chord and the cadential six-four, which is often described as a double appoggiatura over the dominant bass.

Beats and lengths. The appoggiatura chord belongs on the beat, where the passing chords of Part VII, kept on weak beats (Chapter 29), do not stand: beat 1, or beat 3 in 4/4. Eighteenth-century treatises, C. P. E. Bach's Versuch (1753) among them, teach that a long appoggiatura usually takes half the value of its main note (two thirds of a dotted one). Borrowing that rule, a golden appoggiatura chord on a half-note arrival lasts a quarter and its resolution a quarter. Status: a proposal; the right proportion for a bend of 33 cents is a matter for the ear.

Three cadences.

  1. The bent tonic. V – Cg – I: the final tonic arrives as its own golden image. The resolution is the smallest golden-to-Western motion there is, two sixth-steps in contrary motion, and the release is C major.
  2. The bent dominant. ii⁶ – Gg – V – I: the dominant arrives as Gg (G vB ^D) and sharpens its leading tone vB → B as it straightens. The leading tone, approached from a sixth-step below, already leans upward.
  3. The golden six-four, falling. IV – vCg/G – V – I. The upper voices G3 C4 E4 of the Western cadential six-four fall to G3 B3 D4 when it resolves. Split evenly in 36-ET, that resolution passes through vCg/G and then the bronze chord vE♭T13/G. So the golden six-four of Chapter 27, which rises into I over the dominant bass, is also the first step of the Western six-four falling into V: the same chord on the same bass, heard in two directions.

The roll above is a palindrome: I⁶₄ – V – I with every step split into sixth-steps. A cadence written this way puts vCg/G on both sides of the dominant. Status: the path is a theorem (it is the uniform path); its use is a proposal.

Western mirror. The Western appoggiatura and cadential six-four are in Chapter 12 and Chapter 10; the golden six-four as a passing chord is in Chapter 27.

Golden suspensions and retardations

In a suspension one voice refuses to leave while the harmony changes under it, then gives in. The golden version needs a golden note to hold, so it starts from a golden chord. Listen first to a double suspension: the bass and the lowest upper voice move from B to C, while the two golden notes above are held, and only then do they let go, one rising and one falling. Then the same two bends taken one at a time, and a chain in which three voices fall a semitone in turn.

Why Western suspensions cannot be golden. A suspension holds notes of one chord against another. If both are Western, every note in sight is on the 12-ET layer, so the vertical is Western too (theorem: a golden triad needs a note on each layer). A golden suspension therefore needs a golden preparation.

The golden double suspension. Hold two golden notes and move the third voice onto a Western note; if the vertical is golden, the two held notes then resolve by sixth-steps. A golden chord sharing two notes with another is its neighbour on the golden circle (Chapter 31), so the change is a circle step: one voice moves a semitone, two are held. Staggered resolution. The two bends of a projection need not straighten together. Cg → C(^5) → C lets the third resolve first and holds the fifth a sixth-step high, a small suspension; Cg → C(v3) → C holds the third low, a small retardation. Measured on harmonic tones (C3 C4 E4 ^G4 against C3 C4 vE4 G4), the held high fifth is the rougher of the two, 2.262 against 2.003, close to Cg's 2.292 and C's 1.956: most of the golden tension sits in the golden fifth (measurement).

Chains of suspensions. Baroque writers liked chains in which voices fall by step in turn, each dissonance prepared by the last. The golden circle is such a chain by construction: each step moves one voice a semitone and holds two. Walking it into Cg and then straightening gives a golden suspension chain: ^Ag → C♯g → vFg → ^A♭g → Cg → C (each moving voice falls a semitone) or, rising, vE♭g → Bg → ^Gg → vEg → Cg → C. Every falling semitone here is an exact golden apotome in golden tuning (99.27 c); the walk is the apotome pump of Chapter 34 read backwards. Status: the chords are a theorem (circle steps); hearing them as suspensions is a proposal.

Theorem (shape of a golden suspension). Let a vertical $S$ consist of held notes of a golden preparation and moved notes that already belong to a Western target $Y$. If $S$ is golden, exactly two notes are held and one is moved. Proof. Moved notes lie on the 12-ET layer, held golden notes on distinct layers. A golden $S$ has one note on each layer, so it has exactly one layer-0 note: either one moved note and two held off-layer notes, or no moved note at all (no change). ∎ Corollary. Preparation and suspension share two notes, so they are neighbours on the golden circle: $G_r \cap G_s$ has two elements only for $s = r \pm 11$: the differences between notes of $\{0, 11, 22\}$ are 11 and $25 \equiv -11$ (twice each) and 22 and $14 \equiv -22$ (once each), so a shift by $\pm 11$ keeps two common notes and a shift by $\pm 22$ only one.

Exhaustive list. Over the 36 preparations, each voice moving at most a semitone to a note of C or Cm, the script finds the six suspensions into C tabled above and six into C minor (elastic.py §8). The Western-only check (every Western triad held against every other, any held note) finds no golden vertical, as the layer argument predicts.

Exercise 39.3. Transpose the double suspension vEg → Cg → C to G major. Which chord prepares it, which voice moves, and how do the held notes resolve?

Answer

Add 21 sixth-steps (a fifth): vEg becomes vBg = vB ^D F♯, Cg becomes Gg = G vB ^D. F♯ rises to G (the leading tone of G) while vB and ^D are held; then vB rises to B (retardation) and ^D falls to D (suspension).

Anticipations and escape chords

Two more classical figures. An anticipation arrives early: one voice reaches its note of the next chord before the others. An escape tone steps away from the goal and then leaps to it, a small feint. Listen for both as golden chords: first an alto that reaches C a beat early while the other voices are still bending upward, then all three voices stepping down, away from A minor, before leaping up into it, then a gentler escape into C.

Three findings. For the smooth fifth progressions V → I, IV → I (and likewise I → IV, I → V), the only golden chord on a smoothest path is the strict passing chord, and no golden chord is a pure anticipation, overshoot or escape: every other golden chord nearby mixes types and costs at least two extra sixth-steps. For progressions in which every voice moves the same way (V → vi, IV → V), all the pure types exist. And a golden anticipation chord costs nothing: in V → vi the alto reaches C while the other two voices are one and five sixth-steps along their whole-tone climbs (G → ^G, D → vE), and the chord they make is Cg. Placed on the weak beat before the arrival, it is the golden form of the anticipation that so often precedes a deceptive or a final cadence. (In the audio the bass holds G under these chords, hence the symbols Cg/G and ^F♯g/G; the census concerns the three upper voices.)

The golden escape chord. ^F♯g between V and vi steps every voice down (G → ^F♯, B → B♭, D → vD) and then leaps up 6 to 8 sixth-steps (a whole tone or more) into A minor: an échappée in three voices at once, with an excess of 12 sixth-steps. A milder escape is Gg between V and I, where only the leading tone steps away (B → vB) before rising four sixth-steps to C. The échappée is a weak-beat figure, and so are these chords.

A golden cambiata. The nota cambiata of Renaissance counterpoint steps down from a consonance to a dissonance, leaps a third further down, past the note the dissonance would have resolved to, and steps back up to it. Read as motion toward a goal, this is passing, then overshoot, then settle. Chord by chord, and rising instead of falling, golden chords exist for each stage in V → vi: G3 B3 D4 → vA♭3 ^B3 E♭4 (passing, vA♭g) → ^A3 C♯4 vF4 (overshoot, ^Ag) → A3 C4 E4. The motion is 6 + 15 + 6 = 27 sixth-steps against 15 for the direct move. Status: a proposal; the chords and costs are computed.

The census. For a voiced progression $X \to Y$, enumerate every voicing $G$ with $\min(x_i, y_i) - 6 \le g_i \le \max(x_i, y_i) + 6$ whose pitch classes form a golden triad, classify each voice by the betweenness lemma of §39.1, and record the excess $d(X,G) + d(G,Y) - d(X,Y)$. A chord is uniform when its moving voices share one type. Counts for V → I: one passing chord, and 36 mixed chords in ten mixed classes, with excesses 2 to 12 (elastic.py §9–§10). The uniform escape and overshoot chords of V → vi each have excess 12, which by the lemma means every voice detours by 1, 2 and 3 sixth-steps: the parallel rule of §39.1 seen from the other side.

Golden neighbour chords

The simplest embellishment of all leaves home and comes straight back. Over a held C in the bass, listen to C major stepping onto three golden neighbours and back: one that bends two voices in opposite directions, one that sinks, and one that lifts. Then the Western neighbour six-four, C – F/C – C, beside its golden counterpart, and finally a double neighbour that dips below and then rises above before coming home.

Types. A golden neighbour chord of C is a golden chord G in C – G – C. It is an upper neighbour if every voice that moves goes up, a lower neighbour if every one goes down, and split otherwise. Visitation, seen from the other side. Chapter 31 described Cg – C – Cg, a golden home that opens briefly onto C major (rational visitation). C – Cg – C is the same pair of chords with the other one as home. On harmonic instruments C major is the deeper rest, so the visit and the neighbour feel different even though the voice leading is identical: the neighbour returns to the global minimum; the visit leaves it and returns to the delicate local rest of the golden triad (the author's model).

The golden pedal six-four. The Western neighbour six-four, C – F/C – C, lifts E to F and G to A over a held C (3 + 6 sixth-steps). Its golden counterpart is vFg/C = C vF ^A♭, which lifts the same voices by 2 and 4: exactly two thirds of the way to F/C. It is the strict passing chord of C → F/C used as a neighbour, the plagal glow of Chapter 29 turned back on itself. ^A♭g/C = C vE ^A♭ is a split alternative (cost 5). Measured with the pedal (C3 C4 vF4 ^A♭4 against C3 C4 F4 A4), the golden one is rougher, 2.360 against 2.022.

Double neighbours. A double neighbour goes above and then below its note, or below and then above, before returning (C – D – B – C). Requiring every voice to do this, there are 98 golden pairs; the cheapest is C – ^Bg – vC♯g – C, with every voice first below and then above, 24 sixth-steps in all. The pair is the two full overshoots of C, from above and from below: the neighbour form of an elastic that swings both ways.

Counts (elastic.py §11): golden voicings $G$ with $|g_i - c_i| \le 6$ for C4 E4 G4: 28, of which 10 upper, 10 lower and 8 split; double neighbours $C \to G_1 \to G_2 \to C$ with $m^{(1)}_i m^{(2)}_i < 0$ for every moving voice: 98, the cheapest of total motion 24.

The pedal six-four. Upper voices C4 E4 G4 → C4 F4 A4 move $(0, 3, 6)$; the uniform path has $d = (0, 1, 2)$ and $P_2 = (C4, E4 + 2, G4 + 4)$ = C4 vF4 ^A♭4, pitch classes $\{0, 14, 25\}$ = vFg. ∎

Exercise 39.4. Show that ^Cg is an upper neighbour of C major and vCg a lower one, write each as a planing of Cg, and find the total motion of C – ^Cg – C – vCg – C.

Answer

^Cg = ^C E vA♭: C → ^C (+1), E stays, G → vA♭ (+2), all up. vCg = vC ^E♭ G: C → vC (−1), E → ^E♭ (−2), G stays, all down. Every note of Cg = C vE ^G moved up one sixth-step gives ^C E vA♭, and down one gives vC ^E♭ G, so ^Cg = Cg + 1 and vCg = Cg − 1: the projection planed up and down, as in the parallel rule. The motion is 3 + 3 + 3 + 3 = 12 sixth-steps.

Reharmonizing a melody with golden chords

Any Western melody can be reharmonized, its familiar notes set against new chords. With golden chords the melody stays in C major while the harmony slides through golden colours, and Western chords return at the cadences. Listen to the theme of Beethoven's Ode to Joy (1824) harmonized this way: twenty of its thirty notes carry a golden chord, the rest a diatonic one, with a half cadence on G and a full close on C.

Theorem (three consecutive chords). A pitch class $x$ lies in $G_r = \{r, r+11, r+22\}$ if and only if $r \in \{x, x-11, x-22\}$. On the golden circle $G_r$ sits at position $p$ with $11p \equiv r \pmod{36}$, that is $p \equiv 23r$ ($23 = 11^{-1}$ in $\mathbb{Z}_{36}$), so the three chords sit at $23x$, $23x - 1$ and $23x - 2$: consecutive. ∎ (Checked for all 36 pitch classes.)

The interval costs. For a melodic interval of $s$ semitones, the minimum of $d(G, G')$ over $G \ni x$, $G' \ni x + 3s$ is 0, 3, 12, 5, 3, 3, 12 for $s = 0, \dots, 6$ (exhaustive). The semitone case is a circle step (vFg → C♯g: vF ^A♭ C → vF ^A♭ C♯); the third and fourth cases are planing by one sixth-step (Cg → ^Cg; vFg → Fg).

The programme. Candidates for note $i$ are its three golden triads, plus, if allowed, the major and minor triads of C major containing it, each with a penalty $\lambda$. The cost of a sequence is $\sum_i d(c_i, c_{i+1}) + \lambda\cdot\#\{\text{diatonic chords}\}$, minimized by dynamic programming over the 30 notes (elastic.py §12; $\lambda = 0$ and $\lambda = 4$ give the second and third rows).

Exercise 39.5. The melody steps E → F. Which pair of golden chords harmonizes it most smoothly, and what moves?

Answer

E lies in ^Cg, Eg and vAg; F in ^C♯g, Fg and vB♭g. The cheapest pair is vAg → Fg (cost 3): vA ^C E → vA ^C F, a circle step in which only the melody moves. The next best are ^Cg → Fg and vAg → ^C♯g (cost 6).

Three further strategies

The golden pedal six-four. C – vFg/C – C, the golden neighbour over a tonic pedal, two thirds of the way to the Western C – F/C – C (§39.5). It suits plagal endings and the "amen" after a cadence. Status: the chord fact is a theorem; the use is a proposal.

The golden chromatic-mediant cadence. The Romantic mediants close onto I in one move (♭VI – I, III – I). Each pair is a same-quality pair a major third apart, so by the quality theorem it has exactly one golden passing chord, and in both cases that chord is a golden image of one of the two triads: A♭ – Cg/A♭ – C passes through π(C) = Cg over the A♭ bass, and E – Eg – C bends E into π(E) = Eg before falling into C. The strict paths are A♭ – {C ^E♭ vA♭} – Cg/A♭ – C and E – Eg – ^GT13/E – C (elastic.py §13). Status: the paths are theorems; calling them cadences is a proposal.

The golden backdoor. The jazz backdoor iv – ♭VII – I (Fm – B♭ – C) approaches C from a whole step below. The natural passing engine fills B♭ → C with two golden chords on the chromatic root between, B♭ – vBg – ^Bg – C (the whole-step root rule of Chapter 28), and the last of them, ^Bg, rises into C in every voice: ^B → C, E♭ → E, vG → G. It is the same ^Bg that every falling leap into C overshoots onto (§39.1): the golden backdoor arrives from below exactly where the elastic would swing. Status: the path is the engine's; the name and use are a proposal.

What the catalogue shows. Every strategy in the table is a relation between a golden chord $G$ and one or two Western chords, and the betweenness lemma sorts them. Passing chords lie between $X$ and $Y$; overshoot, appoggiatura and the falling six-four put $Y$ between $X$ and $G$; escape chords put $X$ between; neighbour chords have $X = Y$; suspensions and anticipations hold or pre-empt single voices. Projection-based strategies (colour chords, pivots, appoggiatura to the tonic) use the minimum distance 2 between the two worlds, which no strategy can beat because a golden triad has a note on every layer and a Western triad on one. And the asymmetry theorem of Chapter 31 explains why the ornament relation runs one way: golden chords can pass between Western ones, but no uniform path goes golden → Western → golden. So in these strategies the golden chord is the ornament, and C major the place where it comes to rest.

Exercise 39.6 (listening). Play the elastic phrase of §39.1 and the golden reharmonization of §39.6 one after the other. In which do the golden chords sound like ornaments of Western chords, and in which like a harmony of their own? Relate your answer to how long each golden chord lasts and to whether it is followed by its own projection's Western preimage.

Answer

There is no single right answer. In the elastic phrase every golden chord lasts one beat and resolves at once into the Western chord it is an image of (^Fg into F, vGg into G …), so most listeners hear ornaments. In the reharmonization golden chords follow each other (vAg – Fg – vCg), often for two beats, and resolve only at the cadences, so they are more likely to be heard as a harmony in their own right.
