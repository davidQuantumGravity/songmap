Chapter 32 described the three kinds of key change in 36-ET: within a layer, across layers, and between golden scales. This chapter measures them with the golden chain's own small intervals. The golden comma, limma and apotome of Chapter 23 turn out to be exactly the key changes by one, two and three sixth-steps, and each has its own way of moving: the comma lift is a pure change of plane, the limma lift passes through a golden triad at its exact midpoint, and a walk of thirteen golden13 keys raises a key by a comma one note at a time. The chapter ends with a map of all 36-ET keys under three different measures of distance.

The comma, the limma and the apotome as key changes

Western music has a favourite blunt key change: the whole song steps up a semitone for its last chorus. 36-ET offers three smaller steps of the same kind: one sixth-step, two, and three (a semitone). Listen to C major moving each distance and coming back. The first two moves leave the ordinary keyboard altogether; the third lands back on it, a semitone higher.

These three distances are not arbitrary. They are the three small intervals of the golden chain: one sixth-step is how 36-ET renders the golden comma, two the golden limma, three the golden apotome. A key change by a sixth-step is a modulation by a golden comma.

Two meanings of one sixth-step

The table also shows 36-ET's own enharmonic identification. One sixth-step up is chain distance 13, the golden comma (+30.17 c); one sixth-step down is chain distance 23 (−38.92 c). In exact golden tuning these are different intervals, and "comma up" followed by "chain-23 down" does not return home: it leaves you 8.75 c flat, the 36-comma. 36-ET makes them exact inverses, just as 12-ET makes B♯ and C the same note (Chapter 24).

Lifting a key: sixth-steps and third-steps

The simplest way to change key by a golden interval is to stop and continue somewhere else. Listen to a cadence in C major followed by the same cadence a sixth-step higher. Nothing is shared and nothing is prepared; the whole music simply moves to a slightly brighter plane.

The Well-Tempered Golden Clavier uses this lift as its spine: its tonics rise by sixth-steps through all 36 notes (Chapter 31).

Lifts

A lift by $s$ sixth-steps transposes the whole key. Chapter 32 called it a direct shift (Chapter 32); in pop music it is the "truck-driver" key change, there a semitone or a whole tone. Lifts by one and two sixth-steps are the golden-sized ones: modulation by a comma and by a limma. How far does each move the music? Measure it by voice-leading distance, the total motion in sixth-steps when each note of one chord or scale moves to a note of the other, matched as smoothly as possible (Chapter 14).

No golden chord on the way

Can the comma lift be smoothed by a golden chord in the middle, as the passing engine smooths C → A♭ with Cg (Chapter 27)? No. Every voice of C major must move at least one sixth-step to reach ^C major, and the plain lift already achieves exactly that, so any chord on a smoothest path must have each of its notes either at the old note or at the new one. Such a chord uses only two layers, and a golden triad needs all three.

The best a golden chord can do is a detour: C → Cg → ^C costs 2 + 3 = 5 against the direct 3 (Chapter 32). For scales the detour is worse still: no golden7 scale lies between C major and ^C major, and the cheapest golden7 detour, through ^C golden7 or E golden7, costs 15 against the direct 7 (exhaustive, blending.py §9). Modulation by a golden comma is a pure change of plane.

The submediant door

The theorem is about the tonic triads. Other chords of the two keys can still meet in gold. The shortest example: the vi chord of C major and the I chord of ^C major, Am → ^C, have two golden chords exactly on their smoothest path, ^Cg and vAg (distance 7, exhaustive, blending.py §6). So a comma lift can be entered through a golden door, from the submediant (Chapter 35 studies such hinges systematically).

Theorem (layer betweenness). Let $X$ be a pitch-class set on one layer and $Y = X + 1$. Then $d(X, Y) = |X|$, and every chord $G$ on a smoothest voice leading between them (a geodesic hinge, $d(X,G) + d(G,Y) = d(X,Y)$) is a subset of $X \cup Y$. In particular no golden, silver or bronze triad lies on a smoothest voice leading from $X$ to $X + 1$. Status: theorem; also checked exhaustively over all 48 major, minor, augmented and diminished triads on layer 0 (0 golden hinges, blending.py §6).

Proof. $X$ and $Y$ lie on different layers, so every voice moves at least one sixth-step: $d \ge |X|$, and the uniform $+1$ attains it. Let $G$ be a hinge with optimal bijections $\sigma: G \to X$ and $\tau: G \to Y$. Then $\tau\sigma^{-1}$ is a bijection $X \to Y$ of cost at most $\sum_g \big(|g - \sigma g| + |g - \tau g|\big) = d(X,G) + d(G,Y) = |X|$. Each pair $(\sigma g, \tau g)$ costs at least 1, so each costs exactly 1 and each triangle inequality is tight: $g$ lies on the shortest arc between two points one sixth-step apart, so $g \in \{\sigma g, \tau g\}$. Hence $G \subseteq X \cup Y$, which touches only two layers, while a golden triad has one note on each (the layer theorem). ∎

Lifts of a triad by $s$ (exhaustive over the 24 major and minor triads on layer 0, blending.py §6): golden geodesic hinges exist for none at $s = \pm 1$, exactly one at $s = \pm 2$ (next section), four at $s = \pm 4$ and seven at $s = \pm 5$, for every one of the 24 triads.

The golden midpoint of the limma lift

Lift the key by two sixth-steps instead of one, and something new appears. There is now room for a chord in the middle, and the chord that fits is golden. Listen to C major moving to vC♯ major through ^Cg.

Slowed down and dry, the midpoint is easy to follow, and it works the same way from a minor triad. The last part of the example asks the passing engine for one chord between C and vC♯, without telling it what to choose. It chooses the same golden chord.

Proof of the golden midpoint theorem. The comma pump: thirteen steps to ^C

A lift moves every note at once. There is a gentler way to rise a sixth-step: move one note at a time. Take thirteen steps through golden13 keys, each key the neighbour of the last on the golden chain. At every step exactly one note of the scale rises a sixth-step, and after thirteen steps every note has risen once: the key has been lifted by a comma, by stealth. In the example the keys play, at each new key, the note that leaves and then its replacement.

The harmony of the pump: the golden circle backwards

The tonic golden triad of each key also belongs to the next key, and consecutive tonic triads are neighbours on the golden circle of Chapter 31, walked in the opposite direction (exhaustive, blending.py §2):

Each step keeps two notes and raises the third a semitone (an exact golden apotome in exact tuning). The forward circle step lowers a voice: Cg → vEg takes C down to B. The pump raises one: Cg → ^A♭g takes ^G up to ^A♭, which becomes the new root. Thirteen such steps lift the chord by one sixth-step, from Cg to ^Cg. In close position the three voices climb by 15, 12 and 12 sixth-steps over the walk, 39 in all, three per step (computed), so the register drifts upwards; the example's organ climbs with it, from ^G3 C4 vE4 to ^C4 E4 vA♭4, and only its doubled top voice drops back once.

Down by ten, up by a limma; up by three, up by an apotome

Walk the other way, down the chain, and each step lowers one note by a sixth-step: C golden13 → vE golden13 lowers D to vD, then vF♯ falls to ^F, ^A to A, and so on. Thirteen steps down reach vC golden13. Now stop after ten. The tenth key is vC♯ golden13, which is C golden13 raised by two sixth-steps. Ten notes have each fallen a sixth-step, three have stayed, and the scale as a whole has risen by a golden limma.

There is no paradox. A scale is a set: ten lowered notes plus three kept notes can be the same set as all thirteen notes raised by two. The harmony shows it too: the walk is ten steps forwards on the golden circle, Cg → vEg → ^Gg → Bg → vE♭g → ^F♯g → B♭g → vDg → ^Fg → Ag → vC♯g, each step lowering one voice a semitone, and the last chord is Cg raised by a limma. Three steps up the chain give the third golden unit: C → ^A♭ → vF → C♯ golden13 raises B♭, ^F♯ and vE♭ by a sixth-step each, and the key has risen by a golden apotome, back onto the 12-ET layer.

Western mirror: five flats up a semitone. The diatonic has the same paradox. Move C major five fifths down, C → F → B♭ → E♭ → A♭ → D♭: each step lowers one note by an apotome (B → B♭, E → E♭, A → A♭, D → D♭, G → G♭), yet D♭ major is C major raised by a Pythagorean limma, 90.22 c (five fifths down, chain −5). And seven fifths up, C → C♯ major, raises all seven notes by an apotome. The golden pairs are thirteen notes with (13, 10, 3) steps for (comma, limma, apotome); the Pythagorean chromatic window has twelve notes with (12, 5, 7) (computed).

Exact tuning: 3.16 c short

In exact golden tuning there is no ^C; there is only the chain. Thirteen exact steps up raise each of the thirteen notes by an exact golden comma, so the walk lands 30.17 c above C. 36-ET's ^C is 33.33 c above C. The exact pump therefore arrives 3.16 c below 36-ET's ^C. The other two walks miss by less: the exact limma pump arrives at 69.10 c (36-ET: 66.67, 2.43 c sharper than 36-ET), the apotome pump at 99.27 c (36-ET: 100, 0.73 c flatter). These are exact values (theorem). Whether the 3.16 c gap is audible against a sustained 36-ET reference is an open question; here is a first probe. Above C5 the two notes are 0.97 Hz apart, so on pure tones they beat about once a second.

Proposition (window shift). golden13 on tonic $t$ is $W(k) = \{25j : k-6 \le j \le k+6\}$ with $k = c(t)$. Then $W(k+1) = W(k) \setminus \{25(k-6)\} \cup \{25(k-6) + 1\}$, and $W(k-1) = W(k) \setminus \{25(k+6)\} \cup \{25(k+6) - 1\}$. Proof: the entering note is $25(k+7) = 25(k-6) + 25 \cdot 13 \equiv 25(k-6) + 1$; downward symmetrically. ∎ Hence $W(13)$, golden13 on $25 \cdot 13 \equiv 1$ = ^C, is $W(0) + 1$: every chain position $-6, \ldots, 6$ has been raised once, in that order.

The limma walk. $W(-10)$ is golden13 on $25 \cdot (-10) \equiv 2$ = vC♯, so $W(-10) = W(0) + 2$. By the proposition it is also $W(0)$ with the ten positions $6, 5, \ldots, -3$ each lowered by 1. Both descriptions agree because $-10 \equiv 26 = 2 \cdot 13 \pmod{36}$: lowering ten notes by one sixth-step and raising all thirteen by two give the same set (checked). The apotome walk. $W(3) = W(0) + 3$ with positions $-6, -5, -4$ raised (B♭, ^F♯, vE♭).

Harmony. The tonic triad of $W(k)$ is $G_{25k}$. With $G_t = \{t, t+11, t+22\}$, $G_{t+25} = \{t+25, t+36, t+47\} = \{t+25, t, t+11\}$, so the step keeps $t$ and $t+11$ and replaces $t + 22$ by $t + 25$, a rise of 3; since $25 \equiv -11$, it is the golden-circle step $G_t \to G_{t+11}$ reversed. Since $13 \cdot 25 \equiv 1$, thirteen steps give $G_1$ = ^Cg. Reaching $G_s$ from $G_0$ this way takes $|c(s)|$ steps, forwards or backwards.

Exact values. $13g - 10800 = 30.17$ c against $1200/36 = 33.33$ c, a gap of 3.16 c (equivalently $13 \times 0.243$ c, the 36-ET rendering error of chain distance 13, Chapter 23). Beat rate above $f = 523.25$ Hz: $f\,(2^{33.33/1200} - 2^{30.17/1200}) = 0.97$ Hz (0.49 Hz above C4).

The golden circle as a modulation engine

How far apart are two golden13 keys? Play each one from C and count what changes. The neighbour a golden third up changes one note by one sixth-step. Two steps away, two notes have moved by one sixth-step each. The key a sixth-step up, nearest in pitch, has every note moved.

A perfect circle

Theorem. The voice-leading distance between golden13 on C and golden13 on any tonic $s$ equals the chain distance: $d = |c(s)|$. Status: theorem for $|c(s)| \le 13$, exhaustive check for all 36 tonics (blending.py §2).

So the 36 golden13 keys, measured by total voice motion, form a 36-cycle in which every neighbour is exactly one sixth-step away and every key is as far as its chain distance. A few values: vE and ^A♭ at 1; vF and ^G at 2; C♯ and B at 3; D and B♭ at 6; ^C and vC at 13; F and G at 15; F♯, the far side, at 18.

Western mirror. The same is true of the diatonic in 12-ET: the voice-leading distance from C major to any major key, in semitones, equals the number of fifths between them (G and F 1, D and B♭ 2, … F♯ 6; computed for all twelve keys). Each step around the circle of fifths moves one note by one semitone, the chroma (Chapter 8). golden13 in 36-ET is the exact counterpart, with the golden sixth for the fifth and the comma for the chromatic semitone. That is one reason to call it the golden diatonic of 36-ET; it is also maximally even there (Chapter 36).

Compare the other families. The 36 major keys of 36-ET form three separate circles of fifths, one per layer, with distance 3 per fifth (one note moves a semitone, 3 sixth-steps); the circles are joined only by lifts, and the shortest bridges, to ^C and vC major, cost 7 (exhaustive, blending.py §3). golden7 keys change by five sixth-steps per chain step, so the circle is uneven: from C golden7 the nearest keys are vE and ^A♭ (5), then ^C and vC (7, sharing no note), then vF, ^G, ^E♭ and vA (10) (exhaustive, blending.py §2).

Driving the engine

Because the tonic triads of neighbouring golden13 keys are neighbours on the golden circle, the circle of chords is a modulation engine: to reach the key on tonic $s$, walk $|c(s)|$ circle steps, backwards if $c(s) > 0$, forwards if $c(s) < 0$. To ^Cg it is 13 steps back, to vC♯g 10 forwards, to C♯g 3 back. Each step keeps two notes of the chord and changes one note of the key by one sixth-step: the smoothest long modulation that 36-ET allows.

A complete comma-pump modulation from C major to ^C major then reads: C major → Cg (two voices bend, distance 2) → 13 circle steps (3 each) → ^Cg → ^C major (distance 2). Proposal: its total motion is 2 + 39 + 2 = 43 sixth-steps, for a change the plain lift makes in 3, so its value is not economy but continuity: every chord shares two notes with the next, and every key differs from the last by one comma. Does a thirteen-step walk register as a key change at all, or as a drift? What pacing would make the arrival on ^C feel like a cadence? Fibonacci phrase lengths are one candidate. These are open questions for listening.

Exercise 34.4. How many circle steps, and in which direction, lead from Cg to the tonic triad of A golden13? How many notes of the key change on the way?

Answer

$c(27) = 13 \times 27 = 351 = 9 \times 36 + 27 \equiv -9$: nine steps forwards, Cg → vEg → ^Gg → Bg → vE♭g → ^F♯g → B♭g → vDg → ^Fg → Ag. Nine notes are each lowered by one sixth-step, and the voice-leading distance between the two scales is 9.

Proof of the theorem for $0 < c \le 13$. Shifting the window by $c$ chain steps removes positions $-6, \ldots, -7 + c$ and adds $7, \ldots, 6 + c$. Pair $j$ with $j + 13$: each pair differs by $25 \cdot 13 \equiv 1$ sixth-step, so $d \le c$. Conversely the two windows share exactly $13 - c$ notes (Chapter 32, window overlap), so at least $c$ notes must move, each by at least 1: $d \ge c$. ∎ The case $c < 0$ is symmetric. For $14 \le |c| \le 18$ the windows share no note and the pairing argument no longer applies directly; the equality $d = |c|$ there is an exhaustive check (for example F♯, $|c| = 18$, $d = 18$).

Diatonic check. For the major scale in $\mathbb{Z}_{12}$, $d(D_0, D_{7k}) = |k|$ semitones for $|k| \le 6$ (computed; for $|k| \le 5$ the same pairing proof works with window 7 and chroma 1).

A map of the 36-ET keys

"Near" means different things. A key can be near because it shares many notes, because every voice has only a little way to go, or because the two keys share chords of some kind. Listen to three neighbours of C major, one for each meaning: G major, which shares six notes; ^C major, which shares none but is (with vC major) the nearest key on another layer; and ^E♭ major, which shares no note and is farther by voice leading, but is reached through a golden chord that belongs, as the golden image of a diatonic chord, to both keys.

What the map shows (all exhaustive):

  • Western keys form three circles of fifths, one per layer, with $d = 3 \times$ (number of fifths). Across layers the nearest keys are ^C and vC major and their relatives ^A and vA minor ($d = 7$), then ^F and vG major (8). The key nearest in pitch shares nothing.
  • golden7 and C major never share more than two notes. The golden7 keys nearest to C major by voice leading are ^C golden7 and vE golden7 (7), then C golden7 (8); from C golden7 the nearest major keys are ^A♭ and vC major (7), then A♭ and C (8).
  • golden13 and silver13 share two or three notes with C major, never more. Nineteen golden13 keys share three: C, C♯, ^C♯, D, vE♭, E♭, E, vF, F, F♯, ^F♯, G, vA♭, ^A♭, A, vB♭, B♭, B and ^B.
  • Layers: a golden7 scale has 3, 2 and 2 notes on its tonic's layer and the other two; golden13 and silver13 have 5, 4 and 4. A golden key is never on one layer, so it is never cut off from a major or natural minor key: every golden7 key shares at least one note with each, every golden13 or silver13 key at least two. (Harmonic minor can be cut off: vG and ^B♭ harmonic minor share no note with C golden7.)
  • golden13 and silver13 on C share 7 notes at distance 12; the nearest silver13 keys to C golden13 are C (12), then B, C♯, ^A♭ and vE (13).

Three metrics, three maps

  1. Common tones. Western keys on other layers are infinitely far: they share nothing (Chapter 32). golden13 and silver13 keys are never far and never near: two or three notes, always.
  2. Voice leading. Everything is connected. The lift (7) is the shortest bridge between layers for Western scales; the golden13 keys form one perfect circle.
  3. Projection pivots. C major is linked to ^E♭ major and vA major by three golden pivot chords each, although by voice leading they are 10 away and by common tones infinitely far. The example's Fg is one of them: golden IV of C and golden ii of ^E♭ (Chapter 35).

Exercise 34.5. Which golden13 keys share three notes with C major and are within voice-leading distance 3 of C golden13?

Answer

Exercise 34.6 (listening). In the limma pump, every step lowers a note. Before reading the last chord's name, did you hear the arrival as higher or lower than the start?

Answer

The key is two sixth-steps higher (vC♯ against C), but the organ's voices drift down over the walk: its first chord is G3 C4 E4 C5 and its last vF3 vA♭3 vC♯4 vA♭4. Pitch class and register point in opposite directions, so either impression is defensible; which one wins for you is the point of the exercise.

Proposition (at most three). No golden13 key shares more than three notes with C major. Proof: in chain coordinates C major is $\{-15, -9, -3, 0, 6, 12, 15\}$ (G, A, B, C, D, E, F), with gaps 6, 6, 3, 6, 6, 3, 6 around the 36-cycle. A golden13 key is a window of 13 consecutive positions, spanning 12. Four positions of C major would need three consecutive gaps summing to at most 12, but every three consecutive gaps sum to at least 15. ∎ The minimum, 2, and the list of nineteen keys with 3 are exhaustive (blending.py §3).

Western keys. Major scales on the same layer are the 12-ET case scaled by 3, so $d = 3k$ for keys $k$ fifths apart (computed: F 3, D 6, E♭ 9, E 12, C♯ 15, F♯ 18). Across layers the cosets are disjoint and every voice must change layer, so $d \ge 7$, attained by the lifts ±1.
