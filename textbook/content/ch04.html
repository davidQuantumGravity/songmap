Just intervals

The harmonic series hands us a set of smooth, beat-free intervals: the octave 2:1, the fifth 3:2, the major third 5:4 and their relatives. Tuning intervals to these exact ratios is called just intonation. Modern keyboards do not use it. Listen to the difference it makes in a single major third.

The just third is calm and glassy. The piano's third shimmers with a rapid beat; we are so used to it that it sounds normal, but next to the just third it is clearly restless. A whole chord shows the same contrast.

A just interval is one whose frequency ratio is a fraction of small whole numbers. The ones that matter most come straight from the harmonic series (Chapter 2):

The just major scale. The classic way to tune a major scale justly is to make its three main triads pure: F–A–C, C–E–G and G–B–D, each 4:5:6. The result:

It has two sizes of whole tone, 9/8 and 10/9, and a semitone 16/15. Against a drone, its notes lock in with a stillness equal temperament cannot match.

But the scale has a flaw hidden inside it. The fifth from D up to A is $\frac{5/3}{9/8} = \frac{40}{27}$, which is 680.45 cents, more than 21 cents narrower than a pure fifth, and the minor third D–F is $\frac{4/3}{9/8} = \frac{32}{27}$ (294.13 c) instead of 6/5. A D minor chord in this scale is badly out of tune. Fixing it means moving D, which spoils G–B–D. That tension, between pure intervals and a fixed set of notes, runs through the whole history of tuning, and its size is the syntonic comma of the last section.

Ptolemy (2nd century CE) described a division of the fourth into steps 16/15, 9/8 and 10/9, the same three steps, in a different order, as the lower half of the just scale above. In the sixteenth century Gioseffo Zarlino (Le istitutioni harmoniche, 1558) championed the ratios built from the numbers 1 to 6, his senario, as the basis of consonance, which gave the just major and minor thirds (5/4 and 6/5) a secure place among the consonances alongside the fifth.

Definition. An interval is just if its ratio is rational. In practice "just intonation" means using ratios of small numbers, usually with prime factors from a small set (next section).

Theorem (the just major scale). Requiring F–A–C, C–E–G and G–B–D to be 4:5:6 triads, with C = 1, and folding every note into $[1, 2)$, forces the scale $1, \frac98, \frac54, \frac43, \frac32, \frac53, \frac{15}{8}$.

Proof. C–E–G gives E = 5/4, G = 3/2. G–B–D gives B = G·5/4 = 15/8 and D = G·3/2 = 9/4, folded to 9/8. F–A–C, with C as its fifth, gives F = 2/(3/2) = 4/3 and A = F·5/4 = 5/3. Every step is forced. $\square$

The broken fifth. In this scale, $A/D = \frac{5/3}{9/8} = \frac{40}{27} = \frac32\cdot\frac{80}{81}$: the pure fifth lowered by the syntonic comma $81/80$, $701.96 - 21.51 = 680.45$ c. Any just tuning of the seven notes must break at least one of the four fifths F–C, C–G, G–D, D–A or the major third F–A; the reason is the comma identity $\left(\frac32\right)^4 = \frac54\cdot 4\cdot\frac{81}{80}$ (four fifths overshoot two octaves and a major third by the comma), worked out in the last section.

Prime limits

The harmonic series does not stop at 6. Harmonic 7 gives a seventh lower than any on the piano, and the chord 4:5:6:7 is a pure, beat-free version of a "dominant seventh".

The harmonic seventh chord sounds unusually sweet and unified, a little "barbershop". It also sounds slightly out of tune to ears raised on pianos, because its seventh is far from the piano's. It belongs to a different family of intervals from the major third, and the family is named by a prime number.

Every whole number greater than 1 factors into primes in exactly one way: $12 = 2^2\cdot3$, $45 = 3^2\cdot5$, $81 = 3^4$. So every ratio can be written as a product of primes with positive or negative exponents:

$$\frac{5}{4} = 2^{-2}\cdot5,\qquad \frac{6}{5} = 2\cdot3\cdot5^{-1},$$ $$\frac{15}{8} = 2^{-3}\cdot3\cdot5,\qquad \frac{81}{80} = 2^{-4}\cdot3^4\cdot5^{-1}.$$

Each prime contributes its own kind of interval.

  • 2: the octave. Powers of 2 only move a note between octaves.
  • 3: the fifth (3/2) and fourth (4/3), and everything built from stacked fifths: Pythagorean tuning (Chapter 5).
  • 5: the just major third (5/4), minor third (6/5) and sixths (5/3, 8/5).
  • 7: the harmonic seventh (7/4, 968.83 c), the septimal minor third (7/6, 266.87 c), the septimal tritone (7/5, 582.51 c) and whole tone (8/7, 231.17 c).
  • 11 and 13: intervals such as 11/8 (551.32 c, between the fourth and the tritone) and 13/8 (840.53 c, between the minor and major sixth).

The prime limit of a tuning is the largest prime it uses. Pythagorean tuning is 3-limit; the just major scale is 5-limit; the harmonic seventh chord is 7-limit. Traditional Western harmony is usually explained in the 5-limit: its consonances are octaves, fifths, fourths, thirds and sixths. Composers and theorists of extended just intonation, such as Harry Partch, have worked with 7, 11 and beyond (Chapter 22).

Monzos. By the fundamental theorem of arithmetic, every positive rational $r$ has a unique expression $r = 2^{e_2}3^{e_3}5^{e_5}7^{e_7}\cdots$ with integer exponents, almost all zero. The exponent vector $[e_2, e_3, e_5, \dots]$ is sometimes called a monzo. Multiplying ratios adds their vectors, so the $p$-limit ratios form a group isomorphic to $\mathbb{Z}^k$, where $k$ is the number of primes up to $p$ ($\mathbb{Z}^2$ for the 3-limit, $\mathbb{Z}^3$ for the 5-limit).

Cents are linear. $c(r) = 1200\,(e_2 + e_3\log_2 3 + e_5\log_2 5 + \cdots)$, with $1200\log_2 3 \approx 1901.96$ and $1200\log_2 5 \approx 2786.31$.

Theorem (the primes are independent). The numbers $1, \log_2 3, \log_2 5, \log_2 7, \dots$ are linearly independent over the rationals. In particular $\log_2 3$ is irrational, and no stack of pure fifths ever equals a stack of octaves.

Proof. Suppose $a_0 + a_1\log_2 3 + a_2\log_2 5 + \cdots = 0$ with rational $a_i$, not all zero; clear denominators so they are integers. Exponentiating base 2 gives $2^{a_0}3^{a_1}5^{a_2}\cdots = 1$. Moving negative exponents to the other side gives two products of primes that are equal, with no prime on both sides; by unique factorization every exponent is zero, a contradiction. $\square$

The theorem says that each prime is a genuinely new direction of musical space: no combination of fifths and thirds ever reproduces an octave exactly, and no combination of fifths ever reproduces a just third. Every tuning system is a way of living with this fact.

The ratio lattice

If we forget octaves, a 5-limit note is reached from C by some number of pure fifths and some number of pure major thirds. That suggests drawing notes on a grid: fifths in one direction, thirds in the other. The next example plays the three triads that build the just major scale, F, C and G, which are three neighbouring triangles on that grid, and then the scale itself.

Octave equivalence lets us ignore the exponent of 2. A 5-limit pitch class is then fixed by two whole numbers: how many fifths ($a$) and how many major thirds ($b$) it lies from C. Plot each as the point $(a, b)$ and you get the ratio lattice, a grid of notes in which moving right multiplies by 3/2 and moving up multiplies by 5/4 (both folded back into one octave).

Reading the lattice:

  • Rows are chains of pure fifths: B♭–F–C–G–D–A is Pythagorean (Chapter 5).
  • Columns are chains of pure major thirds: A♭–C–E.
  • A major triad is a small right triangle: a root, its fifth to the right and its major third above (C, G, E). A minor triad is the triangle on the other side of the third: A, its fifth E to the right, and C below E (A–C–E). The two share the edge C–E.
  • The just major scale is the three neighbouring major triads F–A–C, C–E–G, G–B–D.

The lattice also shows what went wrong with the just scale. There are two D's in the picture. One is two fifths up from C, $9/8$, in the middle row. The other is a fifth below A in the top row, $10/9$. They differ by $\frac{9/8}{10/9} = \frac{81}{80}$: four steps right and one step down. The just scale's D is the first; the D that would make D–F–A a pure minor triad is the second. On the lattice they are different points, and no amount of re-tuning within the 5-limit makes them the same. Likewise B♭ appears as 16/9 (two fifths down) and 9/5 (two fifths up and a major third down), and F as 4/3 and 27/20.

Leonhard Euler drew a version of this lattice, with fifths and major thirds as its two directions, in his Tentamen novae theoriae musicae (1739). The lattice was taken up in the nineteenth century by Arthur von Oettingen and Hugo Riemann; in German it is the Tonnetz, "tone network".

Definition. The 5-limit pitch-class lattice is $\mathbb{Z}^2$, with $(a, b)$ standing for the pitch class of $3^a 5^b$. Its pitch in cents above C is

$$c(a, b) = \big(1901.96\,a + 2786.31\,b\big) \bmod 1200.$$

Theorem (the lattice never closes). Distinct points of the lattice are distinct pitch classes.

Proof. If $(a, b) \ne (a', b')$ gave the same class, then $3^{a - a'}5^{b - b'} = 2^e$ for some integer $e$, contradicting the independence of $1, \log_2 3, \log_2 5$ (previous section). $\square$

So the 5-limit has infinitely many pitch classes, and every closed-looking loop of pure intervals actually ends somewhere new. The step $(4, -1)$ is the syntonic comma $81/80$; $(12, 0)$ is the Pythagorean comma; $(0, 3)$, three major thirds, falls short of the octave by $128/125$.

Distance. A natural size for an interval $2^{e_2}3^{a}5^{b}$ is its Tenney height $\log_2(n d) = |e_2| + |a|\log_2 3 + |b|\log_2 5$ for the reduced fraction $n/d$: the length of its path when each step is weighted by $\log_2$ of its prime (Chapter 3). Short lattice paths are simple ratios.

The Tonnetz

On a piano, the two D's of the last section are the same key. Equal temperament folds the infinite lattice up so that it closes. The lattice drawn with triangles, folded or not, is called the Tonnetz, and it has a beautiful property: neighbouring triangles are chords that share two notes. Walk from triangle to triangle and only one note moves at a time.

The progression drifts a long way, from C to E♭, yet every step is as smooth as it could be. This kind of motion is common in nineteenth-century and film music.

The Tonnetz in its common modern form uses three directions instead of two: fifths along one axis, major thirds along another, and minor thirds along the third. Every small triangle is then a major or a minor triad, and two triangles that share an edge share two notes.

Three moves between neighbouring triangles have names, from neo-Riemannian theory:

MoveFrom C majorShared notesWhat moves
P (parallel)C minorC, Gthe third, down a semitone (E → E♭)
R (relative)A minorC, Ethe fifth, up a whole tone (G → A)
L (leading-tone exchange)E minorE, Gthe root, down a semitone (C → B)

Each move is its own inverse (applying R twice returns to C), and any major or minor triad can be reached from any other by a chain of them. The audio example alternates R and L. Chapter 10 develops these moves as harmony (Chapter 10), and Chapter 18 follows their cycles (Chapter 18).

In 12-ET the Tonnetz wraps around. Four fifths up and one major third down is a unison (the syntonic comma has disappeared), and three major thirds make an octave. The flat sheet rolls up in both directions into the surface of a doughnut, a torus, with 12 notes, 36 edges and 24 triangles (12 major and 12 minor triads). On the pitch-class circle the same triad is just three marked points:

Neo-Riemannian theory was developed from the 1980s by David Lewin and others, and extended in the 1990s, notably by Richard Cohn, as a way to analyse chromatic triadic music that functional harmony explains poorly. The name honours Hugo Riemann, whose late-nineteenth-century theory of chord relations used similar transformations.

The Tonnetz as a quotient. Map the lattice to 12-ET pitch classes by $(a, b) \mapsto 7a + 4b \pmod{12}$: a fifth is 7 semitones, a major third 4. This is a surjective group homomorphism $\mathbb{Z}^2 \to \mathbb{Z}_{12}$. Its kernel is the sublattice spanned by two vectors:

  • $(4, -1)$, four fifths up and a major third down: the syntonic comma;
  • $(0, 3)$, three major thirds: the diesis 128/125 (downward).

Check: $28 - 4 = 24 \equiv 0$ and $12 \equiv 0 \pmod{12}$; the two vectors have determinant $4\cdot3 - (-1)\cdot 0 = 12$, so they span a sublattice of index 12, which equals the kernel because the image has 12 elements. Hence $\mathbb{Z}^2/\langle(4,-1), (0,3)\rangle \cong \mathbb{Z}_{12}$: 12-ET is the 5-limit lattice with the syntonic comma and the diesis tempered out. The kernel acts on the triangulated plane by translations, and the plane divided by translations along a rank-2 lattice is a torus, so the 12-ET Tonnetz is a torus.

Theorem (the triangulated torus). The 12-ET Tonnetz has $V = 12$ vertices, $E = 36$ edges (12 fifths, 12 major thirds, 12 minor thirds) and $F = 24$ triangles, and $V - E + F = 0$, the Euler characteristic of a torus.

Proof. There are 12 major and 12 minor triads. Each triad has three edges, and each edge (an interval class of 3, 4 or 5 semitones between two specific pitch classes) lies in exactly two triads, one major and one minor; so $E = 3 \cdot 24 / 2 = 36$, twelve of each interval class. (Checked by enumeration.) $\square$

P, L, R as involutions. Writing a triad as root and quality, $P(r^+) = r^-$, $R(r^+) = (r + 9)^-$ and $L(r^+) = (r + 4)^-$, with the inverses on minor triads. Each is an involution, and $P, L, R$ generate a group acting simply transitively on the 24 triads (it is isomorphic to the dihedral group of order 24).

Small gaps: a first look at commas

We have met the same small gap three times now: between the two D's, in the broken fifth of the just scale, and in the wrapping of the Tonnetz. It is the syntonic comma, about a fifth of a semitone. Here it is in sound. First a major third reached by four pure fifths (and two octaves down), then a just major third, then the two upper notes alone, one after the other and then together.

Now something stranger. The next example plays the progression C – A minor – D minor – G – C in just intonation. Every chord is perfectly pure, and every note the chords have in common is held exactly. Yet the final C chord is not the one we started from.

A choir singing pure intervals and holding its common tones would drift downward a comma with every pass through this progression. This is the comma pump, and it shows why an instrument with fixed pitches, such as a keyboard, cannot keep every interval pure.

A comma is a small interval that measures the mismatch between two routes to "the same" note. The important ones:

Each comma is a closed-looking loop on the lattice that does not close. A tuning system has to decide what to do with them:

  • Just intonation keeps every interval pure and lets the commas show up as drift or as duplicate notes.
  • Pythagorean tuning keeps fifths pure and accepts very wide thirds: 81/64 is 407.82 c, a comma above 5/4 (Chapter 5).
  • Meantone narrows every fifth by a fraction of the syntonic comma so that thirds come out (nearly) pure (Chapter 7).
  • 12-ET tempers out both the syntonic comma and the diesis, as the Tonnetz showed, and the Pythagorean comma too, at the cost of making every third and sixth impure (Chapter 7).
Golden intervals are not on the ratio lattice at all. The golden ratio $\varphi = (1 + \sqrt5)/2$ is irrational, so no product of primes equals it, and the golden third $2/\varphi$ (366.91 c) and golden fifth $4/\varphi^2$ (733.82 c) are not just intervals of any limit (Chapter 23). Yet the golden system has commas of its own, built the same way. Thirteen golden sixths overshoot nine octaves by the golden comma $\varphi^{13}/2^9 = 30.17$ c (Chapter 23), and thirty-six golden sixths fall short of twenty-five octaves by 8.75 c, the comma that 36-ET tempers out.

Commas as lattice vectors. With octaves ignored, write each comma by its exponents of 3 and 5:

  • syntonic $81/80 = 2^{-4}3^4 5^{-1}$: $(4, -1)$;
  • Pythagorean $3^{12}/2^{19}$: $(12, 0)$;
  • lesser diesis $128/125 = 2^7 5^{-3}$: $(0, -3)$;
  • schisma $32805/32768 = 2^{-15}3^8 5$: $(8, 1) = (12, 0) - (4, -1)$, so its size is $23.46 - 21.51 = 1.95$ c.

The comma identity. $\left(\frac32\right)^4 = \frac{81}{16} = 4\cdot\frac54\cdot\frac{81}{80}$. In cents, $4 \cdot 701.955 = 2807.82 = 2400 + 386.31 + 21.51$.

Theorem (the comma pump). In 5-limit just intonation, the progression C – Am – Dm – G – C with every common tone held and every new note forming a pure fifth or major third ends a syntonic comma below where it began.

Proof. Track roots on the lattice, starting from C at $(0, 0)$. Each chord shares a note with the next, and pure intervals fix each new root:

  • C → Am: keeping C and E, the new note A makes A–C–E a pure minor triad, so A lies a pure fifth below E (and a pure minor third below C): $A = 5/3$, the point $(-1, 1)$.
  • Am → Dm: keeping A, D is a pure fifth below A, $(-2, 1)$, i.e. $10/9$.
  • Dm → G: keeping D, G is a pure fifth below D (up a fourth), $(-3, 1)$, i.e. $40/27$.
  • G → C: keeping G, C is a pure fifth below G, $(-4, 1)$, i.e. $80/81$.

The final root differs from the first by $(-4, 1)$, the syntonic comma downward, $1200\log_2(80/81) = -21.51$ c. $\square$ At C4 = 261.63 Hz the final C is $261.63 \cdot 80/81 \approx 258.40$ Hz, 3.23 Hz lower, the beat in the audio example.

In general a just progression with held common tones moves its pitch level by the lattice vector of its root path. The path C–A–D–G–C looks closed on a keyboard but is not closed on the lattice; only temperament, which identifies lattice points, can close it. The full story begins with the Pythagorean comma in Chapter 5.

1. In the just C major scale, compute the fifth D–A and the minor third D–F in cents. Which pure intervals do they miss, and by how much?

Answer

D–A $= 40/27 = 680.45$ c, a syntonic comma (21.51 c) below 3/2. D–F $= 32/27 = 294.13$ c, a syntonic comma below 6/5 (315.64 c).

2. Show that $\log_2 3$ is irrational.

Answer

If $\log_2 3 = p/q$ with whole numbers $p, q > 0$, then $3^q = 2^p$. The left side is odd and the right side is even. Contradiction.

3. Find F♯ on the lattice as 45/32 (a fifth above B, or a major third above D: two fifths and a third above C) and G♭ as 64/45. How far apart are they?

Answer

45/32 $= 3^2\cdot5/2^5$ is $(2, 1)$, 590.22 c. 64/45 $= 2^6/(3^2\cdot5)$ is $(-2, -1)$, 609.78 c. They differ by $\frac{64/45}{45/32} = \frac{2048}{2025}$, 19.55 c (the diaschisma), the vector $(-4, -2)$ (plus octaves).

4. Which neo-Riemannian move takes A minor to F major? Write the progression C – Am – F – Dm as a word in P, L and R.

Answer

A minor → F major keeps A and C and moves E up to F: that is L (applied to a minor triad, the fifth moves up a semitone). C – Am – F – Dm is R, then L, then R.

5. Using the lattice vectors, show that the Pythagorean comma, the syntonic comma and the schisma satisfy Pythagorean = syntonic + schisma, and check it in cents.

Answer

$(12, 0) = (4, -1) + (8, 1)$, and $21.51 + 1.95 = 23.46$ c (more precisely $21.5063 + 1.9537 = 23.4600$).

6. (Listening.) In the comma-pump example, can you hear the drift during the progression itself, or only in the side-by-side comparison? Why might choirs not drift as much in practice?

Answer

Most listeners hear it only in the comparison: each step is pure, and 21.51 c spread over four chords is gradual. Real singers do not hold every common tone with perfect exactness and also hold on to a remembered reference pitch, so they compromise between pure intervals and a stable pitch level; how they do so is a question of performance practice and measurement, not of theory.
