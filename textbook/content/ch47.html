Blending: the craft

The chapters before this one took golden harmony apart: one cadence, one theorem, one example at a time. This chapter puts it back together in five complete songs. Each is an ordinary piece of Western music, a lullaby, a pop song, a lo-fi groove, a flamenco cadence and a cinematic theme, with one golden technique woven into it. Western harmony stays home in all five; golden chords come in as passing light, as sparkle, as colour, as a descent, and once as a home of their own.

Listen to the songs whole before reading about them. The question to ask is not "where are the golden chords?" but "does anything sound wrong?" If the craft works, the golden moments sound like part of the music: a glide, a shimmer, a bend, never a mistake.

Every song in the chapter follows the same three rules. They are stated here once, and each section shows them at work.

Rule 1: short and weak. A golden chord that passes between two Western chords lasts one beat or less and sits on a weak beat (2 or 4). A golden colour chord, one that replaces a Western chord rather than joining two, lasts half a bar. The only long golden stretch in the five songs is the golden home of §47.6, and it changes the instruments first.

Rule 2: the melody never fights the gold. When the harmony turns golden, the melody either rests or holds a note the golden chord keeps. Every golden passing chord and every golden projection shares at least one note with a Western neighbour, so a common tone is always available: C over vFg/C, F over Fg, E over Eg and vAg.

Rule 3: whatever sounds must change with the chord. A pad or a held chord that is still sounding the old Western chord when the golden one arrives produces two notes a sixth-step apart: 33.33 cents, a sour rub that no listener hears as intended. So the accompaniment uses the voiced styles of Chapter 43, which move every voice through the passing chords, or chord-relative figures (rel), which resolve each note against the chord sounding when it starts.

Instruments. All five songs use the stable instruments of Chapter 44: a piano, electric piano, pad and guitar with no detune, drift or tremolo, and clean additive leads and basses. A chorus effect that wobbles each note by ten cents would smear the very sixth-steps the songs are about.

Why a common tone always exists. For passing chords this is part of the path construction of Chapter 27: a passing chord is a voice-by-voice weighted mean $P_1 = (2X + Y)/3$ of its neighbours, so any voice that holds in the progression holds through it. For colour chords it is the projection theorem of Chapter 35 (theorem): the nearest golden triad to a major triad $M_r$ is $G_r$, at voice-leading distance 2, keeping the root and moving the third and fifth by one sixth-step each; for a minor triad $m_r$ it is $G_{r-1}$, keeping the fifth.

The rub check (measurement). Rule 3 can be checked mechanically. A short script, tools/rubs.py, expands a song into its notes and lists every pair of notes that sound together for at least a quarter beat at an interval of 1, 2, 34 or 35 sixth-steps modulo the octave, that is 33.33 or 66.67 cents away from a unison or octave. Golden chords themselves never contain those intervals: their steps are 11, 22 and 33 (and 3, 14, 25 between their upper notes), so every hit is an accident of layering. All five songs pass, with one deliberate exception explained in §47.2.

Exercise 1. A golden triad's notes are 0, 11 and 22 sixth-steps above its root, and a golden seventh adds 33. List every interval between two of these four notes, reduced modulo 36. Show that none is 1, 2, 34 or 35, so a golden seventh chord can never trip the rub check by itself.

Answer

The differences are 11, 22, 33 (from the root), 11, 22 (from the third) and 11 (from the fifth). Reduced modulo 36 and taken both ways round, they are 11, 22, 33 and their complements 25, 14, 3. None is 1, 2, 34 or 35. The smallest interval in the chord is 3 sixth-steps, a 12-ET semitone, between the golden seventh and the octave of the root.

Passing light: “Golden Hour Lullaby”

The lullaby is in C major at 72 beats per minute. A piano plays held chords, a second piano sings the tune, a guitar rippling in eighth notes and a pad join from the second verse, and a glass bell doubles the tune in the bridge. The chords are the plainest in Western music, I, IV, V and vi, but nearly every change slides: on the fourth beat of the bar the piano's voices move a third of a semitone at a time into the next chord.

Listen to four bars from the verse. In the second bar, F climbs to G through two golden chords, the golden escalator; in the third, G falls into C through one. The tune rests during the escalator and holds G, a note the next golden chord keeps, in the third bar.

The bridge ends on the full golden authentic cadence, four chords over a held G in the bass.

The harmony. The verse is one eight-bar progression, written with passing placeholders:

C:0.75 ~:0.25  F:0.75 ~:0.25  C:0.75 ~:0.25  G:1  Am:1  F:0.5 ~g:0.25 ~g:0.25  G:0.75 ~:0.25  C:1

A ~ asks for the one strict golden chord between its neighbours (System 1), and ~g ~g for the two chords of a natural path (System 2). The engine resolves them to C – vFg/C – F, F – vFg/F – C, C – vCg/C – G, the escalator F – vF♯g – ^F♯g – G, and G – vCg/G – C. All of them fall on the fourth beat of a bar.

Uneven steps. In the escalator the top voice climbs F – vF♯ – ^F♯ – G in three equal steps of two sixth-steps (66.67 cents each). The inner voices split their whole step unevenly: A – ^A – B♭ – B moves by 1, 2 and 3 sixth-steps, and C – C♯ – vD – D by 3, 2 and 1. That unevenness is what System 2 allows and System 1 forbids (Chapter 28).

The melody. The tune is diatonic throughout. It rests through the escalator, and over every one-chord golden passage it holds a note the golden chord shares with its neighbours: C over C – vFg/C and G over G – vCg/G. The bridge melody stops on beat 3 of its last bar, so the four-chord cadence sounds with the piano alone.

The deliberate exception. The rub check flags one kind of moment in this song: a golden chord over the held bass of the chord before it (vFg/C over C, vCg/G over G). The bass note and the golden root sit a sixth-step apart for one beat. That is the golden six-four of Chapter 27, a passing dissonance over a pedal, and it is left in on purpose; nothing in the song rubs for longer than a beat.

Where the passing chords come from (theorem, Chapter 27). Strict golden passing chords join only triads of the same quality whose roots are a third or a fifth apart, and each such pair has exactly one. The lullaby's strict passages, I – IV, IV – I, I – V and V – I, are all major–major pairs a fourth or fifth apart, so each has its single golden chord; the bridge adds two minor pairs, vi – iii and ii – vi, which the engine resolves to Am – Eg/A – Em and Dm – Ag/D – Am. The IV – V step is a whole step, outside System 1 entirely; System 2 splits it into the two golden chords on the chromatic root between, the escalator (Chapter 28).

The four-chord cadence. G – vE♭T13/G – vCg/G – C is the strict V → I path with both passing chords. One is bronze (T13), the other golden: by the partner theorem a strict path never holds two golden chords (Chapter 27). The voices move B → C in three sixth-steps of 33.33 cents, and D → E in three steps of 66.67 cents, each arriving exactly together.

Sparkle: “Deceptive Light”

The second song is a pop song in G major at 120 beats per minute, built on the most worn-out progression in popular music: I – V – vi – IV, G – D – Em – C. Drums, bass, electric piano, a pad, a plucked arpeggio and a lead melody play it as such songs are played. The golden harmony lives in one place: every time D moves to Em, it does so through two golden chords lasting an eighth note each.

Listen to the same two bars twice: first the plain V – vi, then the golden one. The bass, which plays eighth notes on the chord root, makes the difference obvious. The first time it jumps from D to E; the second time it climbs D – vE♭ – E♭ – E like a pop walk-up, with a step only a third of a semitone wide at the start.

The golden deceptive cadence is V – v♭VIg – ♭VIg – vi (Chapter 29): in G major, D – vE♭g – E♭g – Em. Both golden chords have roots on ♭VI, so the cadence passes through the key's ♭VI in golden form and lands on vi. In the song it is written

D:0.75 ~g:0.125 ~g:0.125 Em:1

and the electric piano voices it as follows.

The top voice rises F♯ – ^F♯ – vG – G by single sixth-steps. The lowest voice, doubled by the bass, rises D – vE♭ – E♭ – E by 2, 1 and 3 sixth-steps. Because the bass part is chord-relative eighth notes (R8 R+ R R+ R R+ R R, with plain roots on the last two eighths), it follows the golden roots automatically.

The other golden moments. The pre-chorus climbs the escalator C – vC♯g – ^C♯g – D and lands on vGg/D, the golden passing chord of D → G, as a quarter-note upbeat into the chorus. The bridge slows the deceptive cadence to whole beats so it can be heard clearly, and loops it, V back to vi. The ending is golden modal mixture (Chapter 29): C – Cg – Cm – G, where Cg sits halfway between the major and minor subdominant.

Shape. The loudness of each section (measurement, from the render) rises from −21.5 LUFS in the verses to −17.6 in the choruses, drops to −24.9 in the bridge, and peaks at −12.9 in the last chorus, 4.7 dB above any other section, after a one-bar drop that leaves only the electric piano and pad.

Why vE♭g and E♭g (exhaustive, Chapter 28). D major and E minor differ in quality, so the quality theorem rules out a strict golden path between them. System 2 admits natural paths in which a whole step may split unevenly; for V → vi the engine's natural path puts both golden chords on the chromatic root between D and E, a sixth-step apart: vE♭g = (8, 19, 30) and E♭g = (9, 20, 31) in sixth-steps above C.

Golden modal mixture. Cg is the average of C, Cm and Caug (the averaging principle, Chapter 25): its third, 11 sixth-steps, lies between the minor third (9) and the major third (12). So C – Cg – Cm moves the third 12 → 11 → 9 while C and G hold.

Exercise 2. Listen to the bridge of “Deceptive Light”, where the cadence is slowed to whole beats. Then write the golden deceptive cadence in C major, naming the four chords and the bass line.

Answer

G – vA♭g – A♭g – Am. The golden chords sit on ♭VI = A♭, a sixth-step apart; the bass climbs G – vA♭ – A♭ – A.

Colour: “Tape Bloom”

“Tape Bloom” is a lo-fi piece in A minor at 90 beats per minute: swung hi-hats on a triplet grid, a lazy kick, an electric piano comping in rootless ninth chords, a bass that slides, and a soft piano melody. Its golden chords are not passing chords at all. A Western chord bends into its golden counterpart and stays there for half a bar, the way a borrowed chord colours a progression. Two notes of the chord slide by a third of a semitone and the rest hold, so the effect resembles the slow pitch wobble of a worn tape: felt more than announced.

The song ends with a golden Picardy third. In A minor the final chord turns major, but on the way the third climbs through the silver and golden thirds, one sixth-step at a time, under a melody that holds A.

Colour chords by projection (Chapter 38). Replace a diatonic triad X by the golden triad nearest to it, π(X). The song uses three:

F (F A C)Fg (F vA ^C)FA ↓ to vA, C ↑ to ^C

The electric piano does not play triads. It voices each chord as its third, seventh and ninth ([3,7,9]), resolving them against the chord sounding at that moment:

Over F, the seventh is E. The golden seventh, 33 sixth-steps above the root, is exactly a 12-ET major seventh, so the E stays and Fmaj7 → Fg is the golden seventh chord Fg7 with only its third moving, A → vA. Over E7 the seventh is D; Eg has no seventh of its own, so the voicing takes the golden one, D♯, and E7 → Eg moves two voices: the third sinks G♯ → vG♯ and the seventh rises D → D♯.

Rules 1–3 in practice. The colour chords last half a bar. The melody holds F over Fg and E over Eg and vAg, the notes each projection keeps. The electric piano strikes on beat 3, where the colour begins: an early draft of the comping rhythm skipped beat 3, and the bend was then heard only in the bass.

The projection (theorem, Chapter 35). For a major triad $M_r$ the unique nearest golden triad is $G_r$; for a minor triad $m_r$ it is $G_{r-1}$; both lie at distance 2 and no Western triad is at distance 1 from a golden one. In sixth-steps above C: F = {15, 27, 0} and Fg = {15, 26, 1}; Am = {27, 0, 12} and vAg = {26, 1, 12}. Each pair shares one note and moves the other two by one step in opposite directions.

The golden Picardy (Chapter 29). The third above A takes the values 9, 10, 11 and 12 sixth-steps: 300, 333.33, 366.67 and 400 cents. 10 is the silver third, the third of As; 11 renders the golden third 2/φ = 366.91 c. In the chord symbols, Am(^3) raises only the third; Ag also raises the fifth to ^E (22 steps), so the fifth touches ^E once and returns.

Exercise 3. Using the projection theorem, find π(Dm) and say which note it keeps and which two it bends.

Answer

Dm = m_6 (D = 6 sixth-steps above C), so π(Dm) = G_5 = vDg = vD ^F A. It keeps A, the fifth; D sinks to vD and F rises to ^F.

Descent: “Andalusian Gold”

The Andalusian cadence, A minor falling to G, F and E, is the harmonic signature of flamenco and of a thousand songs since. “Andalusian Gold” plays it on a guitar strummed in a flamenco rhythm, with handclaps, a bass and a singing lead. The song first plays the cadence plainly, so the ear knows it, and then golden: each step down takes two golden chords, and the bass walks the whole descent in sixth-steps.

In the golden sections the voice sings for two beats and stops; the guitar answers in gold for the other two. Call and response between the two worlds runs through the whole song.

The golden Andalusian cadence (Chapter 29) is vi – ♭VIg – v♭VIg – V – ^♯IVg – v♯IVg – IV – vIV – ^III – III; in A minor:

Am:0.5 ~g:0.25 ~g:0.25  G:0.5 ~g:0.25 ~g:0.25  F:0.5 vF:0.25 ^E:0.25  E:1

The bass plays the chord root on every beat, so its line is the cadence in one voice:

From A to G the bass falls a semitone, then a sixth-step, then two: 3 + 1 + 2 sixth-steps. From G to F it falls in three equal steps of two sixth-steps, 2 + 2 + 2. From F to E it falls one sixth-step at a time, 1 + 1 + 1, but the chords on that last step are not golden. vF and ^E are Western major triads moved onto another layer: the whole chord slides in parallel. That is the half-step theorem at work.

Form. After a four-bar introduction on the guitar alone: the plain cadence (8 bars), golden (8), a falseta in the relative major (8), the golden cadence at full strength with a glass bell doubling the voice an octave higher (8), and a coda on the golden cadence that hangs on E, the Phrygian half cadence that flamenco ends on.

The half-step theorem (theorem, Chapter 28). Chords a half step apart in parallel motion can never pass through a golden triad: each voice moves by three sixth-steps, and a uniform split of three is 1 + 1 + 1, which keeps every voice on a single path through the layers. The passing chords are therefore planing, the same Western chord shifted by one sixth-step and then two: F → vF → ^E → E.

Why the two golden chords per whole step. Am → G and G → F are stepwise moves between triads a whole step apart. In System 2, as in the escalator, both golden chords stand on the chromatic root between them: A♭g and vA♭g (one sixth-step apart) between A and G, ^F♯g and vF♯g (two sixth-steps apart) between G and F.

Exercise 4. Why does the song not end with a golden chord between F and E?

Answer

F and E are a half step apart. By the half-step theorem no golden triad lies on a path between chords a half step apart in parallel motion; the passing chords are the planed Western chords vF and ^E.

Pivot and home: “The Silver Third”

The last song does three things Western harmony cannot do. Its theme, in C major, modulates to a key that shares not a single note with C: ^E♭ major, 333.33 cents higher, on another layer of 36-ET. The theme returns there unchanged, a "silver third" up. Then the Western instruments fall silent, and a pad and bell with golden spectra breathe through a chain of golden chords, a golden home. A G7 chord arrives as a visitor and leads back to C. The finale walks Coltrane's major-third cycle through its golden passing chords.

The pivot. C major and ^E♭ major are 10 sixth-steps apart. They share no note, but they share three golden chords: Cg, Fg and Gg are the golden I, IV and V of C and the golden vi, ii and iii of ^E♭ (Chapter 35). The song uses Fg:

F → Fg keeps F and bends A and C by a sixth-step each; Fg → ^Fm keeps ^C and bends the other two. Every voice moves by at most one sixth-step at the pivot, and after it the progression is an ordinary ii – V7 – I in the new key. The melody holds F into the pivot and rests while the keys change, then plays the theme again in ^E♭.

Golden home (Chapter 31, Chapter 26). For eight bars the piano and pad stop, and a pad and a bell whose partials follow $2^m\varphi^n$ take over. The chords breathe along the golden circle: a golden triad, then its golden seventh, which adds the next tone of the golden chain, then the next triad, which drops the oldest tone. A glass bell plays chord tones only.

On harmonic instruments a Western chord is the deepest release there is; here G7 arrives as a visitor after the golden passage and resolves home to C. The golden-spectrum timbres are a laboratory setting (Chapter 26): on them the exact golden triad measures smoother than major, which is why the passage can rest on golden chords at all. Treating the golden triad as a delicate, metastable home is the author's model and listening experience; roughness measured on harmonic tones does not show it.

Golden giant steps. The finale's progression is

C:1 ~:0.5 Ab:1 ~:0.5 E:1 ~:0.5 C:1.5

Each golden chord keeps the root of the chord before it: C in Cg, A♭ in A♭g, E in Eg. Each of those roots is also a note of the next chord (C in A♭, A♭ = G♯ in E, E in C), so the melody holds one note across each step: C6, A♭5, E5, C5. The section peaks below the homecoming before it, as a coda should: −18.0 against −16.2 LUFS (measurement).

Projection pivots (exhaustive, Chapter 35). Keys a silver third apart (±10 sixth-steps) share exactly three golden chords, the most possible for keys on different layers, and each pivot is a geodesic of total motion 2 + 2 = 4 sixth-steps with every voice monotone. In sixth-steps above C: F = {15, 27, 0}, Fg = {15, 26, 1}, ^Fm = {16, 25, 1}. Fg is π(F) and also π(^Fm), since $\pi(m_r) = G_{r-1}$ with $r = 16$.

Golden giant steps (exhaustive, Chapter 37). C → A♭ → E → C is a cycle of same-quality pairs a major third apart, so by the quality theorem each step has exactly one strict golden passing chord, on the upper root: Cg, A♭g, Eg. They sit at positions 0, 12 and −12 of the 36-chord golden circle, dividing it in three as the major-third cycle divides the octave.

Exercise 5. Name the three golden chords that C major shares with vA major, and give their functions in each key.

Answer

vDg, vEg and vAg: the golden ii, iii and vi of C, and the golden IV, V and I of vA.

Exercise 6. Write golden giant steps starting on G, and the note the melody holds at each step.

Answer

G – Gg – E♭ – E♭g – B – Bg – G. The melody holds G, then E♭ (= D♯ in B major), then B, then G.
