The chapters before this one took golden harmony apart: one cadence, one theorem, one example at a time. This chapter puts it back together in five complete songs, each two to three minutes long. Each is an ordinary piece of Western music (a lullaby, a pop song, a lo-fi groove, a flamenco piece and a cinematic theme) with one golden technique woven into it. Western harmony stays home in all five. Golden chords come in as passing light, as sparkle, as colour, as a descent, and once as a home of their own.

Each section starts with the full song and a listening guide with timings. Short excerpts follow, each isolating one golden moment for analysis. Listen to the songs whole first. The question to ask is not "where are the golden chords?" but "does anything sound wrong?" If the craft works, the golden moments sound like part of the music: a glide, a shimmer, a bend, never a mistake.

The craft: three rules. All five songs follow the same three rules.

  1. Short and weak. A golden chord that passes between two Western chords lasts one beat or less and sits on a weak beat. A golden colour chord, one that replaces a Western chord rather than joining two, lasts half a bar. The only long golden passage, in §47.5, changes the instruments first.
  2. The melody never fights the gold. When the harmony turns golden, the melody either rests or holds a note the golden chord keeps: C over vFg/C, F over Fg, E over Eg and vAg. Golden passing chords and golden projections always share at least one note with a Western neighbour, so such a note always exists.
  3. Whatever sounds must change with the chord. A pad still sounding the old Western chord when the golden one arrives makes two notes a sixth-step apart: 33.33 cents, a sour rub that no listener hears as intended. So the accompaniment uses the voiced styles of Chapter 43, which move every voice through the passing chords, or chord-relative figures (rel), which resolve each note against the chord sounding when it starts.

Instruments. All five songs use the stable instruments of Chapter 44: piano, electric piano, pads and plucked strings with no detune, drift or tremolo, plus clean additive leads, basses, bells and organ tones. A chorus effect that wobbles each note by ten cents would smear the very sixth-steps the songs are about. Every mix is only peak-normalized (master clean), so section loudness differences are the arrangement's, not a compressor's.

Golden Hour Lullaby: passing light

A bedtime song in C major at 72 beats per minute. A music box opens it; a felt piano sings the tune alone; harp, bass and a flute gather in the second verse; strings swell in the bridge; the flute takes the tune in an instrumental interlude; everything joins in the finale; and the coda settles back to sleep. The chords are the plainest in Western music, I, IV, V and vi, but nearly every change slides: on the last beat of the bar the piano, strings and bass move a third of a semitone at a time into the next chord.

A ~ asks for the one strict golden chord between its neighbours (System 1), and ~g ~g for the two chords of a natural path (System 2). The engine resolves them to C – vFg/C – F, F – vFg/F – C, C – vCg/C – G, the escalator F – vF♯g – ^F♯g – G, and G – vCg/G – C. All of them fall on the fourth beat of a bar. In the escalator the top voice climbs F – vF♯ – ^F♯ – G in three equal steps of two sixth-steps (66.67 cents each). The inner voices split their whole step unevenly: A – ^A – B♭ – B moves by 1, 2 and 3 sixth-steps, and C – C♯ – vD – D by 3, 2 and 1. That unevenness is what System 2 allows and System 1 forbids (Chapter 28).

Zoom in: the golden authentic cadence. The bridge ends with the full four-chord cadence over a held G in the bass. The bridge tune stops on beat 3 of its last bar, so the cadence sounds with the instruments alone.

The melodies. The tune, the flute's countermelody and the music box are all diatonic. Over every golden passage they rest or hold a note the golden chord shares with its neighbours: C over C – vFg/C – F and F – vFg/F – C, G over C – vCg/C – G and G – vCg/G – C.

The deliberate exception. One kind of moment survives the rub check (rule 3 above): a golden chord over the held bass of the chord before it (vFg/C over C, vCg/G over G). The bass note and the golden root sit a sixth-step apart for one beat. That is the golden six-four of Chapter 27, a passing dissonance over a pedal, and it is left in on purpose; nothing in the song rubs for longer than a beat.

Where the passing chords come from (theorem, Chapter 27). Strict golden passing chords join only triads of the same quality whose roots are a third or a fifth apart, and each such pair has exactly one. The lullaby's strict passages, I – IV, IV – I, I – V and V – I, are major–major pairs a fourth or fifth apart, so each has its single golden chord; the bridge adds two minor pairs, vi – iii and ii – vi, which the engine resolves to Am – Eg/A – Em and Dm – Ag/D – Am. The IV – V step is a whole step, outside System 1 entirely; System 2 splits it into the two golden chords on the chromatic root between, the escalator (Chapter 28).

The four-chord cadence. G – vE♭T13/G – vCg/G – C is the strict V → I path with both passing chords. One is bronze (T13), the other golden: by the partner theorem a strict path never holds two golden chords (Chapter 27). The voices move B → C in three sixth-steps of 33.33 cents and D → E in three steps of 66.67 cents, each arriving together.

The rub check (measurement). Rule 3 can be checked mechanically. A short script, tools/rubs.py, expands a song into its notes and lists every pair of notes that sound together for at least a quarter beat at an interval of 1, 2, 34 or 35 sixth-steps modulo the octave, that is 33.33 or 66.67 cents away from a unison or octave. All five songs pass, apart from the one-beat pedals described above.

Exercise 1. A golden triad's notes are 0, 11 and 22 sixth-steps above its root, and a golden seventh adds 33. List every interval between two of these four notes, reduced modulo 36. Show that none is 1, 2, 34 or 35, so a golden seventh chord can never trip the rub check by itself.

Answer

The differences are 11, 22, 33 (from the root), 11, 22 (from the third) and 11 (from the fifth). Reduced modulo 36 and taken both ways round, they are 11, 22, 33 and their complements 25, 14, 3. None is 1, 2, 34 or 35. The smallest interval in the chord is 3 sixth-steps, a 12-ET semitone, between the golden seventh and the octave of the root.

Deceptive Light: the golden deceptive cadence

A bright, driving pop song in G major at 120 beats per minute, built on the most worn-out progression in popular music: I – V – vi – IV, G – D – Em – C. Drums with claps and fills, bass, electric piano, strummed guitar, a plucked arpeggio, a pad, a lead melody and a glass bell play it the way such songs are played. The golden harmony lives in one move: every time D goes to Em, it goes through two golden chords lasting an eighth note each, and the bass walks up D – vE♭ – E♭ – E. The same move is also the song's big surprise: the second and third choruses end on it, so instead of going home to G the music lands on Em, first for the bridge, then for the outro.

Zoom in: plain, then golden. The same two bars twice, first the plain V – vi, then the golden one. The bass, which plays eighth notes on the chord root, makes the difference obvious: the first time it jumps from D to E; the second time it climbs D – vE♭ – E♭ – E like a pop walk-up, with a step only a third of a semitone wide at the start.

The golden deceptive cadence is V – v♭VIg – ♭VIg – vi (Chapter 29): in G major, D – vE♭g – E♭g – Em. Both golden chords have roots on ♭VI, so the cadence passes through the key's ♭VI in golden form and lands on vi. In the song it is written D:0.75 ~g:0.125 ~g:0.125 Em:1, and the electric piano voices it as follows.

The top voice rises F♯ – ^F♯ – vG – G by single sixth-steps. The lowest voice, doubled by the bass, rises D – vE♭ – E♭ – E by 2, 1 and 3 sixth-steps. Because the bass part is chord-relative eighth notes with plain roots on the last two eighths, it follows the golden roots automatically.

The other golden moments. The pre-chorus climbs the escalator C – vC♯g – ^C♯g – D and lands on vGg/D, the golden passing chord of D → G, as a quarter-note upbeat into the chorus. The bridge slows the deceptive cadence to whole beats and loops it, V back to vi. The ending is golden modal mixture (Chapter 29): C – Cg – Cm – G, with the lead holding C, the note all three C chords share.

Shape (measurement, from the render). Section loudness rises from about −20 LUFS in the verses to −16 in the choruses, drops to −23.6 in the bridge, and peaks at −13.0 in the final chorus, 3.0 dB above any other section, after a one-bar drop that leaves only the electric piano, pad and lead.

Why vE♭g and E♭g (exhaustive, Chapter 28). D major and E minor differ in quality, so the quality theorem rules out a strict golden path between them. System 2 admits natural paths in which a whole step may split unevenly; for V → vi the engine's natural path puts both golden chords on the chromatic root between D and E, a sixth-step apart: vE♭g = (8, 19, 30) and E♭g = (9, 20, 31) in sixth-steps above C.

Golden modal mixture. Cg is the average of C, Cm and Caug (the averaging principle, Chapter 25): its third, 11 sixth-steps, lies between the minor third (9) and the major third (12). So C – Cg – Cm moves the third 12 → 11 → 9 while C and G hold.

Answer

G – vA♭g – A♭g – Am. The golden chords sit on ♭VI = A♭, a sixth-step apart; the bass climbs G – vA♭ – A♭ – A.

Tape Bloom: golden colour chords

A lo-fi piece in A minor at 90 beats per minute: swung hi-hats on a triplet grid, a lazy kick, vinyl crackle, an electric piano comping in rootless chords, a sliding bass, a soft piano melody, nylon-guitar ripples, glass sparkles and a flute solo. Its golden chords are not passing chords. A Western chord bends into its golden counterpart for half a bar, the way a borrowed chord colours a progression: two notes slide by a third of a semitone and the rest hold, so the effect resembles the slow pitch wobble of a worn tape, felt more than announced. The song ends with a golden Picardy third.

The electric piano does not play triads. It voices each chord from its third, seventh and ninth (leaving out the ninth where the melody needs room), resolving them against the chord sounding at that moment:

Over F, the seventh is E. The golden seventh, 33 sixth-steps above the root, is exactly a 12-ET major seventh, so the E stays and Fmaj7 → Fg is the golden seventh chord Fg7 with only its third moving, A → vA. Over E7 the seventh is D; Eg has no seventh of its own, so the voicing takes the golden one, D♯, and E7 → Eg moves two voices: the third sinks G♯ → vG♯ and the seventh rises D → D♯.

Rules 1–3 in practice. The colour chords last half a bar. The melody and the flute hold F over Fg and E over Eg and vAg, the notes each projection keeps. The electric piano strikes on beat 3, where the colour begins: an early draft of the comping rhythm skipped beat 3, and the bend was then heard only in the bass.

Zoom in: the golden Picardy. In A minor the final chord turns major, but on the way the third climbs through the silver and golden thirds, one sixth-step at a time, under a melody that holds A.

The projection (theorem, Chapter 35). For a major triad $M_r$ the unique nearest golden triad is $G_r$; for a minor triad $m_r$ it is $G_{r-1}$; both lie at distance 2, and no Western triad is at distance 1 from a golden one. In sixth-steps above C: F = {15, 27, 0} and Fg = {15, 26, 1}; Am = {27, 0, 12} and vAg = {26, 1, 12}. Each pair shares one note and moves the other two by one step in opposite directions. This is also why rule 2 can always be kept: the shared note is the melody's safe harbour.

The golden Picardy (Chapter 29). The third above A takes the values 9, 10, 11 and 12 sixth-steps: 300, 333.33, 366.67 and 400 cents. 10 is the silver third, the third of As; 11 renders the golden third 2/φ = 366.91 c. In the chord symbols, Am(^3) raises only the third; Ag also raises the fifth to ^E (22 steps), so the fifth touches ^E once and returns.

Andalusian Gold: the golden Andalusian cadence

The Andalusian cadence, A minor falling to G, F and E, is the harmonic signature of flamenco and of a thousand songs since. “Andalusian Gold” plays it at 100 beats per minute on two guitars, one strumming rasgueado and one playing picado runs, with palmas (handclaps), a cajón-like kick and snare, a walking bass, strings, a singing lead, a violin-like line and a bell. It plays the cadence plainly first, so the ear knows it, and then golden: each step down takes two golden chords, and the bass walks the whole descent in sixth-steps. In the golden sections the voice sings for two beats and stops, and the guitar answers in gold.

The golden Andalusian cadence (Chapter 29) is vi – ♭VIg – v♭VIg – V – ^♯IVg – v♯IVg – IV – vIV – ^III – III; in A minor:

Am:0.5 ~g:0.25 ~g:0.25  G:0.5 ~g:0.25 ~g:0.25  F:0.5 vF:0.25 ^E:0.25  E:1

The bass plays the chord root on every beat, so its line is the cadence in one voice:

From A to G the bass falls a semitone, then a sixth-step, then two: 3 + 1 + 2 sixth-steps. From G to F it falls in three equal steps of two sixth-steps, 2 + 2 + 2. From F to E it falls one sixth-step at a time, 1 + 1 + 1, but the chords on that last step are not golden. vF and ^E are Western major triads moved onto another layer: the whole chord slides in parallel. That is the half-step theorem at work. The strings and the violin-like line are voice-led too, so in the climax they descend through the same golden chords.

The half-step theorem (theorem, Chapter 28). Chords a half step apart in parallel motion can never pass through a golden triad: each voice moves by three sixth-steps, and a uniform split of three is 1 + 1 + 1, which keeps every voice on a single path through the layers. The passing chords are therefore planing, the same Western chord shifted by one sixth-step and then two: F → vF → ^E → E.

Why two golden chords per whole step. Am → G and G → F are stepwise moves between triads a whole step apart. In System 2, as in the escalator, both golden chords stand on the chromatic root between them: A♭g and vA♭g (one sixth-step apart) between A and G, ^F♯g and vF♯g (two sixth-steps apart) between G and F.

Exercise 4. Why does the song not end with a golden chord between F and E?

Answer

F and E are a half step apart. By the half-step theorem no golden triad lies on a path between chords a half step apart in parallel motion; the passing chords are the planed Western chords vF and ^E.

The Silver Third: pivot, golden home, giant steps

A cinematic piece at 90 beats per minute, scored like an expedition finding a new world, for piano, strings, a harp ostinato, low drums and toms, bass, a lead, a horn-like counter-line, an organ choir, a glass bell, and a golden-spectrum pad. It does three things Western harmony cannot do. Its theme, in C major, modulates to ^E♭ major, 333.33 cents higher, a key on another layer of 36-ET that shares no note with C, and returns there a "silver third" up. Then the Western instruments fall silent and golden-spectrum timbres breathe through a chain of golden chords until a G7 arrives as a visitor and leads home to C. The finale walks Coltrane's major-third cycle through its golden passing chords.

Zoom in: the pivot. C major and ^E♭ major are 10 sixth-steps apart. They share no note, but they share three golden chords: Cg, Fg and Gg are the golden I, IV and V of C and the golden vi, ii and iii of ^E♭ (Chapter 35). The song uses Fg:

F → Fg keeps F and bends A and C by a sixth-step each; Fg → ^Fm keeps ^C and bends the other two. Every voice moves by at most one sixth-step at the pivot, and after it the progression is an ordinary ii – V7 – I in the new key. In the song the lead holds F, the one note F and Fg share; the bell rings vA, Fg's golden third; and the horn climbs F – ^C – ^D – ^E♭ into the new key.

Zoom in: golden home (Chapter 31, Chapter 26). For about twenty seconds the piano and strings stop, and a pad and bells whose partials follow $2^m\varphi^n$ take over. The chords breathe along the golden circle: a golden triad, then its golden seventh, which adds the next tone of the golden chain, then the next triad, which drops the oldest tone. The bell plays chord tones only.

On harmonic instruments a Western chord is the deepest release there is; here G7 arrives as a visitor after the golden passage and resolves home to C. The golden-spectrum timbres are a laboratory setting (Chapter 26): on them the exact golden triad measures smoother than major, which is why the passage can rest on golden chords at all. Treating the golden triad as a delicate, metastable home is the author's model and listening experience; roughness measured on harmonic tones does not show it.

Zoom in: golden giant steps. The finale's progression is C:1 ~:0.5 Ab:1 ~:0.5 E:1 ~:0.5 C:1.5.

Each golden chord keeps the root of the chord before it: C in Cg, A♭ in A♭g, E in Eg. Each of those roots is also a note of the next chord (C in A♭, A♭ = G♯ in E, E in C), so the melody holds one note across each step: C6, A♭5, E5, C5. In the song, the finale sits 3 dB below the homecoming before it, as a closing section should (measurement).

Projection pivots (exhaustive, Chapter 35). Keys a silver third apart (±10 sixth-steps) share exactly three golden chords, the most possible for keys on different layers, and each pivot is a geodesic of total motion 2 + 2 = 4 sixth-steps with every voice monotone. In sixth-steps above C: F = {15, 27, 0}, Fg = {15, 26, 1}, ^Fm = {16, 25, 1}. Fg is π(F) and also π(^Fm), since $\pi(m_r) = G_{r-1}$ with $r = 16$.

Golden giant steps (exhaustive, Chapter 37). C → A♭ → E → C is a cycle of same-quality pairs a major third apart, so by the quality theorem each step has exactly one strict golden passing chord, on the upper root: Cg, A♭g, Eg. They sit at positions 0, 12 and −12 of the 36-chord golden circle, dividing it in three as the major-third cycle divides the octave.
