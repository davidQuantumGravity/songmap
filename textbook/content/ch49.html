Chapter 47 put golden harmony into five acoustic genres. This chapter takes it to the dance floor: deep house, UK garage, progressive house, tech house and melodic house. Each song is built on one golden technique from the book, used the way that genre already uses harmony. Deep house lives on the chord stab, so the stab bends into gold. Garage lives on the bass walk-up, so the walk-up becomes the golden deceptive cadence. Progressive house lives on the build, so the build climbs the golden escalator. Tech house lives on the groove, so the groove becomes quasi-periodic. Melodic house lives on the key change for the last drop, so the key change is a silver third.

Dance music is a good host for golden harmony for a simple reason: it repeats. A loop plays the same four bars dozens of times, so a golden moment heard once as a surprise is heard the tenth time as part of the song. Golden harmony on the dance floor

Electronic music brings three habits that golden harmony has to survive. Its synthesizers are thick: a classic "supersaw" stacks several sawtooth waves a few cents apart, and that detuning blurs the very differences of a third of a semitone that golden chords are made of. Its mixes pump: the pad and the bass duck every time the kick hits, so a chord is heard in pulses rather than as a held sound. And its arrangements loop: four bars, again and again, built up and stripped down.

The first excerpt is the cleanest demonstration of how the songs answer all three. It is the deep-house loop of "Colour Field", four bars plain and then four bars golden. Listen to the organ stab on beat 3 of the third and fourth bars: the first time it is an ordinary chord, the second time it bends.

Four production rules. All five songs follow them, and they are what make the gold audible rather than smeared.

  1. Stable synths. The saw pad and lead run with three voices detuned by 3 cents and no slow pitch drift; a sixth-step is 33.33 cents, so a 3-cent spread leaves it clear. The pluck, the bass, the organ stabs and the bells have no detune at all.
  2. Whatever sounds must change with the chord (Chapter 47's third rule). Pads are voice-led, so every voice moves into the golden chord. Basses and stabs are chord-relative, so each note is taken from the chord sounding when it starts. Leads either hold a tone the golden chord keeps or rest. The book's rub checker finds no two notes a sixth-step or two apart overlapping for a quarter beat or more, in any of the five songs.
  3. Strike on the golden chord. A golden chord that arrives under a held stab goes unheard. In each song, something attacks exactly where the golden chord starts: a stab, a bass note, a pad.
  4. Sidechain and space. Pads, basses and stabs duck under the kick; delays are tempo-synced. The pumping is part of the style; the voice-leading makes sure that every pulse of the pad lands on the right chord.

Mastering. All five are normalized to −14 LUFS, the usual streaming level, with no glue compression; only the limiter touches the loudest peaks. That keeps the drops standing out: in "Colour Field" the peak is 5.6 dB louder than any other section before mastering, and with glue compression at −12 LUFS that margin shrank to about 1 dB.

Why 3 cents of detune is safe. Two saws detuned by $\pm d$ cents beat against each other at $f\,(2^{d/1200} - 2^{-d/1200}) \approx f\,d\ln 2/600$ hertz: at $d = 3$ and $f = 440$ Hz, 1.5 Hz, a slow shimmer. The pitch spread itself, 6 cents end to end, is less than a fifth of a sixth-step, so two chords a sixth-step apart stay distinct. The textbook's stable instruments have $d = 0$; the default synth pad has four voices at 14 cents, a spread of nearly a sixth-step, which is why the songs override it.

Colour Field: the golden stab

Deep house in A minor, lightly swung. A kick on every beat, a rim and offbeat hats, a filtered sub bass, a soft pad and, above them, the deep-house organ stab. The loop is Am7 – Dm9 – Fmaj7 – E7, and on beat 3 of the last two bars the stab bends into gold. In the riff a felt piano takes over from the stabs with a four-bar hook; in the peak both play. The ending turns the minor home into its golden Picardy third.

Projection in a stab. A golden colour chord (Chapter 38) replaces a diatonic chord by its golden projection: one or two notes stay, the others move by a sixth-step. Fmaj7 (F A C E) becomes Fg7 (F vA ^C E): A sinks a sixth-step, C rises one, F and E stay. E7 (E G♯ B D) becomes the golden triad Eg (E vG♯ ^B): its third sinks, its fifth rises. The piano hook holds E over both bends, the one note both pairs share. The pad's voicings, as the song plays them:

The golden Picardy. A minor song may end on the major tonic, the Picardy third. Here the third climbs to it through the golden chords of A: C (Am), ^C (Am(^3), the silver third), vC♯ (Ag) and C♯ (A), a sixth-step at a time, half a bar each (Chapter 29).

Projection, exactly. In sixth-steps above the root, a major triad is (0, 12, 21), a minor triad (0, 9, 21) and the golden triad (0, 11, 22). The projection keeps the root and moves each other tone to the nearest golden tone: 12 → 11 and 21 → 22 for major, 9 → 11 (two sixth-steps) for minor. A major seventh, 33 sixth-steps, is already the golden seventh, so Fmaj7 → Fg7 moves only two notes. A dominant seventh's 30 has no golden counterpart within one sixth-step, so E7 projects to the triad Eg and its D falls silent.

Exercise 1. In the breakdown, Am7 bends into vAg. Write both chords in sixth-steps (C = 0), and say which notes stay and which move.

Answer

Am7 is A, C, E, G: 27, 0, 12, 21. vAg is vA, ^C, E: 26, 1, 12. E stays; A sinks one sixth-step (27 → 26) and C rises one (0 → 1); G falls silent. The piano holds E across the bend, as it held E across Fmaj7 → Fg7.

Two-Step Gold: the golden walk-up

UK garage at 132 BPM: the "2-step" beat, whose kick skips beat 3 and lands on the swung 16th before it, shuffled hats, a deep bass, an electric piano and a chopped lead. The loop is Cmaj7 – D – Em7 – Bm7. In every D bar the last two swung 8th notes carry two golden chords, and the bass walks up through them into Em: D, vE♭, E♭, E.

The golden deceptive cadence (Chapter 29) takes the dominant D, which expects G, up to Em instead, through vE♭g and E♭g. In a G-major song it is V → vi; here, in E minor, it is the familiar ♭VII → i lift of garage and house, made to glide. Each golden chord lasts one swung 8th, and the whole ensemble changes with it: the pad by voice-leading, the bass by taking each chord's root, while the stabs and the lead have already stopped.

The bass's steps. In sixth-steps, D = 6, vE♭ = 8, E♭ = 9, E = 12. The bass rises 2, 1 and 3 sixth-steps: a ninth of an octave in two swung 8th notes, and then an ordinary semitone onto the downbeat.

A pitfall worth recording. The passing engine's natural placeholders ~g ~g between D and Em7 choose vB♭T13/D and E♭g7, not vE♭g and E♭g, because the target is a seventh chord. The song spells the golden chords out. Always check what a placeholder became when the cadence has a name you mean to hear.

Exercise 2. At 132 BPM with 16th notes swung at 0.62, how long does each golden chord last, in milliseconds?

Answer

A beat is 60/132 = 454.5 ms. Each golden chord lasts an 8th note, half a beat: 227 ms. (Swing changes where the off-beat 16ths fall inside each 8th, not the length of the 8th itself.)

Escalator: building on golden steps

Progressive house at 126 BPM, built in long arcs: a sidechained saw pad, a pluck arpeggio with a tempo-synced delay, a rolling off-beat bass, and builds that take their time. The loop is Am – F – G – C, and every time F climbs to G it does so on two golden steps, one beat each. In the build and the drop the G reaches C through the golden authentic cadence. The lead holds G across it all, resting only on the second escalator chord.

The golden escalator (Chapter 28) fills the whole step from IV to V with golden chords: F – vF♯g – ^F♯g – G, each root two sixth-steps above the last. The passing engine voices it so that every voice moves by one to three sixth-steps. The golden authentic cadence (Chapter 29) does the same for V → I: G – vE♭T13/G – vCg/G – C, with G held in the top voice of the pad.

Where the lead rests. G is a tone of every chord of the authentic cadence, so the lead can hold it. Over vF♯g (vF♯, ^A, C♯) it is more than a semitone from every chord tone, so it can hold there too. But ^F♯g has ^F♯, which lies two sixth-steps below G: 67 cents, a sour rub. The lead rests on that one beat.

Voice motion (measured from the song's pad). From F to vF♯g the four voices move +2, +1, +3 and +2 sixth-steps; from vF♯g to ^F♯g, +2 each; from ^F♯g to G, +2, +3, +1 and +2. No voice moves by more than a 12-ET semitone, which is why the escalator sounds like a glide, not a series of chords.

Exercise 3. Using the voicings in the figure, list the sixth-step intervals between the lead's G4 and each note of ^F♯g. Which one is the rub?

Answer

Within the octave (C = 0), G is 21. ^F♯g is ^F♯ (19), A♯ (30) and vD (5). G against ^F♯: 2 sixth-steps (67 cents), the rub. Against A♯: 9 (a minor third). Against vD: 16, or 20 the other way. Only the first is within the rub checker's 1–2 sixth-steps.

Quasicrystal: a groove that never repeats

Tech house in D minor at 124 BPM, dead straight. The kick never moves. Around it, a rim and a shaker play a pattern that seems to repeat and never quite does. A dub-style organ chord echoes through a delay over a one-chord vamp. In the breakdown the beat drops away to the shaker alone, a golden-spectrum pad walks the golden circle, and a low organ holds a golden seventh whose own beating ticks against the shaker. Then Dg slides into Dm9 and the groove returns.

Quasi-periodic percussion. The rim and the shaker read the Fibonacci word (Chapter 48) one letter per 16th note: the rim hits every b, the shaker every a. The word is never periodic, but it nearly repeats itself after a Fibonacci number of letters. At the scale of bars, the rim's patterns run 1 to 9 and then 1 to 7 again, because the word almost repeats after 144 sixteenths, nine bars. Dance music loops every 1, 2 or 4 bars; this groove's shortest near-repeat is 9.

Golden beats against the tempo. A close golden seventh on harmonic tones beats at rates fixed by its root (§41.4, Chapter 26). Rooted on D3 (146.83 Hz), the exact chord's slowest beat is $146.83\,\varphi^{-6} = 8.18$ Hz, and the 16th notes at 124 BPM tick at $124 \cdot 4/60 = 8.27$ Hz: the chord's inner pulse sits a hair under the groove's.

The golden circle and golden modal mixture. The breakdown walks golden chords whose roots lie a golden third apart (Chapter 31); each shares three of its notes with the next, so the pad barely moves. The return to the groove is Dg → Dm9: the golden triad's third falls two sixth-steps (vF♯ → F) and its fifth one (^A → A), golden modal mixture read backwards (Chapter 29).

The beat rates, exact and in 36-ET (measurement). For the exact chord on $f_0 = 146.83$ Hz the rates are $f_0\varphi^{-6}(1, 2/\varphi, 4/\varphi^2)$ and $2f_0\varphi^{-6}$: 8.18, 10.11, 12.50 and 16.37 Hz. The song plays 36-ET notes (146.83, 181.47, 224.28 and 277.18 Hz), and a count of near-coincident partial pairs (eight harmonics each, differences under 25 Hz) splits each rate: 8.06 and 8.28, 9.96 and 10.24, 12.65, 16.11 and 16.48 Hz. The strongest slow beat, 8.06 Hz, drifts a full cycle against the 16ths every $1/(8.27 - 8.06) \approx 4.8$ s. A weaker one, 8.28 Hz, is within 0.02 Hz of them and almost locks: 36-ET has moved one beat onto the groove's rate.

The near-repeat (theorem). The Fibonacci word $w$ satisfies $w_{n+144} = w_n$ for $0 \le n \le 230$ and first differs at $n = 231$: a prefix of length $F_{12} = 144$ almost repeats, as the word's Fibonacci prefixes always do, and then breaks. In the song each 16-bar lane (256 sixteenths) restarts with its section.

Exercise 4. At what tempo would the exact golden seventh on D3 have its slowest beat exactly at the 16th-note rate?

Answer

The 16th-note rate is $4T/60$ Hz at $T$ BPM. Setting $4T/60 = 8.18$ gives $T = 122.7$ BPM. At 124 BPM the groove runs 1% faster than the chord's beat.

Silver Lift: up a silver third

Melodic progressive house at 128 BPM: a saw lead, a pluck arpeggio, a sidechained pad, a rolling bass, a piano in the breakdown. The first drop states the anthem in C. The build walks Coltrane's cycle of major thirds, each step through its golden chord, with the lead holding the step's common tone. Then comes the move every trance and pop producer knows, the key change for the last drop, done the golden way: not up a semitone or a tone, but up a silver third, to a key that shares no note with C.

Golden giant steps (Chapter 37): C, A♭ and E divide the octave into three major thirds, and the golden passing chord between each pair shares one note with both neighbours. The lead holds it: C over C and Cg, A♭ over A♭ and A♭g, E over E and Eg.

The pivot between layers (Chapter 35). C major and ^E♭ major lie a silver third apart, 10 sixth-steps, and share no note. They do share golden chords: Fg is golden IV in C and golden ii in ^E♭. So the song turns on it: F – Fg (still in C) – ^Fm – ^B♭7 (already in ^E♭) – ^E♭. The lead holds F over F and Fg, ^F over ^Fm, and then ^D, the new key's leading tone, over ^B♭7. The anthem's melody is written in scale degrees, so key=^Eb re-keys it for the last drop.

How far the lift goes. In 36-ET the new key is 10 sixth-steps higher: $10 \times 33.33 = 333.33$ cents, between a minor third (300) and a major third (400). The exact silver third of the book's theory is 336.74 cents (Chapter 35). A pop key change of a semitone moves the tonic 100 cents; this one moves it a little more than three times as far, yet every step of the pivot moves some voice by one or two sixth-steps.

Exercise 5. Show that C major and ^E♭ major share no pitch class in 36-ET.

Answer

C major in sixth-steps is {0, 6, 12, 15, 21, 27, 33}. Adding 10 gives ^E♭ major: {10, 16, 22, 25, 31, 1, 7}. Every element of the first set is a multiple of 3 (it lies on the 12-ET grid); every element of the second is congruent to 1 mod 3. The sets are disjoint.

Exercise 6. In giant steps the lead holds A♭ over A♭g. Check that A♭ is a tone of A♭g, and say which notes of A♭ major move.

Answer

A♭g is A♭, vC, ^E♭ (24, 35, 10 in sixth-steps): A♭ is its root. From A♭ major (24, 0, 9) the third sinks one sixth-step (C → vC, 0 → 35) and the fifth rises one (E♭ → ^E♭, 9 → 10). The pad shows it: G♯3 stays, C4 → vC4, D♯4 → ^D♯4.
