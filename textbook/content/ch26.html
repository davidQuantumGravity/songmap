Measuring golden chords

How tense is a golden triad? Ears differ, and so do instruments, so this chapter measures. It uses the two models of consonance from Chapter 3: roughness, the buzz of nearby partials beating against each other, and harmonic entropy, how ambiguous an interval is as a ratio of small whole numbers. First listen, with an ordinary harmonic tone like an organ's, to C major, the golden triad and C diminished. Listen for whether the golden triad sounds less settled than major but less harsh than diminished: that is what the model predicts.

Roughness (the Plomp–Levelt curve in Sethares' formulation, Chapter 3). Each note is given partials, every pair of partials from different notes or the same note contributes a roughness that peaks when they are a fraction of a critical band apart, and the contributions are added. The model used here (reference/golden/explore.py) builds each chord on C4 (261.63 Hz) with either harmonic partials 1 to 8 or golden partials (the next section), amplitudes falling by a factor 0.88 per partial. Lower numbers mean smoother.

Reading the numbers (all of them measurements in a model):

  • With harmonic tones the golden triad is rougher than major, minor and augmented and smoother than diminished. It is not "the least stable chord": diminished is rougher still.
  • Its instability is concentrated in the golden fifth, the most ambiguous of the intervals compared here (69th percentile of the octave, worse than the neutral third). The golden third alone is surprisingly clear in this model: less ambiguous than the 12-ET minor third.
  • So on harmonic tones, which means on every acoustic instrument, golden chords are tense. That suits them to passing between Western chords (Chapter 27), and arriving on C major from them is release. Whether the golden triad is still a small resting place of its own, as the author hears it, is the question of the last section.

Roughness model. For two partials with frequencies $f_1 \le f_2$ and amplitudes $a_1, a_2$, $$d = \min(a_1, a_2)\,\big(e^{-3.5\,s\,(f_2 - f_1)} - e^{-5.75\,s\,(f_2 - f_1)}\big), \qquad s = \frac{0.24}{0.0207 f_1 + 18.96},$$ and the roughness of a chord is the sum of $d$ over all pairs of its partials. The factor $s$ scales the curve to the critical band at $f_1$.

Harmonic-entropy model. The candidate ratios are the reduced fractions $n/d$ with $d < 120$, $d \le n \le 4d$ and $nd \le 12000$, each with weight $1/\sqrt{nd}$. An interval of $c$ cents assigns the ratio at $r$ cents the probability $p_r \propto w_r\, e^{-(c - r)^2/(2\sigma^2)}$, $\sigma = 17$ c, and its entropy is $H(c) = -\sum p_r \ln p_r$.

Both models have parameters (number and loudness of partials, register, $\sigma$, the ratio set). The rankings above are stable under small changes, but the values are not universal constants.

Golden-spectrum timbres

Now play the same three chords on a different kind of tone, one that no physical instrument makes: it exists only as digital synthesis. Its overtones are not the harmonic series 1, 2, 3, 4, …, but golden numbers: 1, 1.618, 2, 2.618, 3.236, 4, …. It sounds like glass or a soft bell. On this tone the model's order reverses: C major and C diminished measure as the rougher ones, and the golden triad as the smoothest of the three.

A golden spectrum has partials at the numbers $2^m\varphi^n$ ($m, n \ge 0$), in increasing order:

1, 1.618, 2, 2.618, 3.236, 4, 4.236, 5.236, 6.472, 6.854, 8, 8.472, …

The idea, due to Sethares (Tuning, Timbre, Spectrum, Scale, 1998), is to match the timbre to the tuning (Chapter 3, Chapter 17). The golden triad's notes are themselves of the form $2^m\varphi^n$ (1, $2/\varphi$, $4/\varphi^2$), so the partials of its three notes fall on one shared lattice and many of them coincide exactly instead of beating. For example the third's partial $\varphi$ is $(2/\varphi)\cdot\varphi = 2$, the root's second partial; the third's partial $\varphi^2$ is $2\varphi$, the root's fifth partial; the fifth's partial $\varphi^2$ is $(4/\varphi^2)\cdot\varphi^2 = 4$. Western ratios such as 5/4 and 3/2, or their 12-ET approximations, are not on this lattice, so their partials land near but not on each other: roughness.

(The roughness model uses the eleven values below 9 with $m, n \le 3$, which leaves out $\varphi^4 = 6.854$; the book's digital golden tones, clean/glass and clean/golden_pad, use the first ten values of the list, with amplitudes falling as a power of the partial number.)

Where such tones come from. Physical instruments are rational in this sense. Strings, winds and voices have (nearly) harmonic spectra, partials at whole-number multiples of the fundamental. Bars, drum heads and bells are inharmonic, but their partials follow their own mechanics (an ideal free bar has modes at 1 : 2.756 : 5.404 : 8.933, Chapter 17), not the golden numbers. No instrument "becomes golden": a golden spectrum is a digital construct, and every golden-spectrum tone in this book is computed partial by partial. Its partials do not line up as a harmonic series, so like bells and glass it gives a less definite sense of pitch than a string or a voice (a general property of inharmonic spectra). How to design such tones is in Chapter 44.

Why golden chords fit golden spectra. The set $\Lambda = \{2^m\varphi^n : m, n \in \mathbb{Z}\}$ is a group under multiplication. If every note of a chord has frequency $f\lambda$ with $\lambda \in \Lambda$ and every partial ratio is in $\Lambda$, then every partial of the chord is in $f\Lambda$. Two partials in $f\Lambda$ either coincide or differ by a factor $2^m\varphi^n \ne 1$. Exact golden triads and sevenths (and the chain chords $1 : \varphi : \varphi^2 : \varphi^3$) satisfy the hypothesis; Western chords in just or 12-ET tuning do not, since $5/4$, $3/2$ and $2^{k/12}$ ($k$ not a multiple of 12) are not in $\Lambda$. (Theorem for the lattice; how much smoother that makes the chord than other chords on the same tone is the measurement above.)

Combination tones that land on the chain

When two loud tones sound together, the ear itself adds faint extra tones that are not in the air: the combination tones, most strongly at the difference of the two frequencies and at twice the lower minus the upper. For most chords these by-products are foreign pitches. For a golden chord spread out along the chain in exact tuning, every one of them is another golden-chain pitch. In the example the chord is played first, then (quietly) the six tones the ear would add, then both together: nothing new appears, because the chord's by-products are more of the chord.

The strongest combination tones of two tones $f_1 < f_2$ are $f_2 - f_1$ and $2f_1 - f_2$. Take the chain voicing $f_0\cdot(1, \varphi, \varphi^2, \varphi^3)$, which is Cg7 with its seventh at the bottom (Chapter 25). Because $\varphi^n - \varphi^{n-1} = \varphi^{n-2}$, its six pairs produce exactly six distinct combination tones:

$$f_0 \cdot \{\varphi^{-2},\ \varphi^{-1},\ 1,\ \varphi,\ \varphi^2,\ 2\varphi\},$$

at $f_0 = 110$ Hz: 42.02, 67.98, 110, 177.98, 287.98 and 355.97 Hz. Every one is $f_0 \cdot 2^m\varphi^n$, a pitch of the same golden chain, with zero error (theorem). Three of them (110, 177.98, 287.98) are chord tones; the others are the chain continued downward and an octave of a chord tone.

How audible combination tones are depends on loudness and on the listener; the pitches above are exact, their strength is not part of the claim.

Fibonacci frequencies

On $f_0 = 55$ Hz the golden chain is almost the Fibonacci sequence: $55\varphi^k$ = 55, 88.99, 143.99, 232.98, 376.98, 609.96 Hz against the Fibonacci numbers 55, 89, 144, 233, 377, 610. With integer Fibonacci frequencies the combination tones are again Fibonacci numbers ($F_{n+1} - F_n = F_{n-1}$ and $2F_n - F_{n+1} = F_{n-2}$), but the chord becomes exactly periodic, repeating once per second (every frequency is a whole number of hertz). The exact $\varphi$ chord never repeats. To the ear the two are the same chord: they differ by 0.008 to 0.016 Hz on the four lowest notes, one beat every one to two minutes.

Proposition. For the chain chord $\{\varphi^0, \varphi^1, \varphi^2, \varphi^3\}$ (times $f_0$), the set of differences $\varphi^b - \varphi^a$ ($a < b$) and positive values $2\varphi^a - \varphi^b$ is $\{\varphi^{-2}, \varphi^{-1}, 1, \varphi, \varphi^2, 2\varphi\}$. (Theorem.)

Proof. For $b - a = 1, 2, 3$: $\varphi^b - \varphi^a$ equals $\varphi^a(\varphi - 1) = \varphi^{a-1}$, $\varphi^a(\varphi^2 - 1) = \varphi^{a+1}$, or $\varphi^a(\varphi^3 - 1) = 2\varphi^{a+1}$ (using $\varphi^3 - 1 = 2\varphi$). For cubic tones: $2\varphi^a - \varphi^{a+1} = \varphi^a(2 - \varphi) = \varphi^{a-2}$, while $2 - \varphi^2 = 1 - \varphi < 0$ and $2 - \varphi^3 < 0$, so only adjacent pairs give positive cubic tones. With $a$ from 0 to 3 this gives the differences $\varphi^{-1}, 1, \varphi$ (adjacent pairs), $\varphi, \varphi^2$ (distance 2), $2\varphi$ (distance 3), and the cubic tones $\varphi^{-2}, \varphi^{-1}, 1$: six distinct values. ∎

Fibonacci identity. From Binet's formula ($F_n = (\varphi^n - \psi^n)/\sqrt5$, $\psi = -1/\varphi$), $$F_{10}\,\varphi^k = F_{k+10} - \varphi^{-10}F_k,$$ so $55\varphi^k$ falls short of $F_{k+10}$ by $\varphi^{-10}F_k = 0.00813\,F_k$ Hz: 0.008 Hz for 89 and 144, 0.016 Hz for 233, 0.024 Hz for 377, 0.041 Hz for 610. (Derived here; checked numerically.) Proof. $F_{10}\varphi^k = (\varphi^{k+10} - \psi^{10}\varphi^k)/\sqrt5$ and $F_{k+10} = (\varphi^{k+10} - \psi^{k+10})/\sqrt5$; the difference is $\psi^{10}(\varphi^k - \psi^k)/\sqrt5 = \varphi^{-10}F_k$, since $\psi^{10} = \varphi^{-10}$. ∎

Never periodic. A sum of two sinusoids with frequency ratio $\varphi$ has no common period, since a period $T$ would need $f_1T$ and $f_2T$ to be whole numbers, making $\varphi$ rational. (Theorem.) Why that might matter is discussed, as hypothesis, in Chapter 41.

The beats of a golden chord

Close-voiced chords on harmonic tones beat: nearby partials of different notes drift in and out of phase. A 12-ET major triad beats irregularly, at unrelated rates. A golden chord in exact tuning beats in a pattern: its beat rates are themselves golden ratios of each other. Listen to the golden triad and then the golden seventh at a low register, and then the golden seventh higher up, where the same pattern runs faster.

A beat between two partials is a slow swell at their frequency difference (Chapter 3). For a close-voiced golden seventh in exact tuning, $f_0\cdot(1, 2/\varphi, 4/\varphi^2, 8/\varphi^3)$, on harmonic tones with six partials, the beats below 15 Hz are (110 Hz is A2, so strictly this is the golden seventh on A; the rates depend only on the root frequency, not on its name):

The first three rates are $f_0\varphi^{-6}\times(1, 2/\varphi, 4/\varphi^2)$: the golden triad in rhythm. The fourth, $2f_0\varphi^{-6}$, is the golden seventh beating against the root's octave, because the seventh lies exactly $2\varphi^{-6}$ below the octave ($8/\varphi^3 = 2 - 2\varphi^{-6}$). No beat occurs at the golden seventh's own ratio, $f_0\varphi^{-6}\cdot 8/\varphi^3$ = 11.58 Hz at 110 Hz.

The golden triad alone (no seventh) beats at $f_0\varphi^{-6}\times(1, 2/\varphi)$ = 6.13 and 7.58 Hz, plus $2f_0\varphi^{-6}$ = 12.26 Hz from root h6 against fifth h4. For comparison, at the same root a 12-ET C major triad beats at 0.37, 0.74, 4.37 and 7.48 Hz (unrelated rates), a just 4:5:6 triad does not beat at all, and the chain voicing $1 : \varphi : \varphi^2$ has no beats below 15 Hz.

Register sets the band. Every rate is proportional to $f_0$. At $f_0 = 55$ Hz they halve; at $f_0 = 10\varphi^6 \approx 179.44$ Hz the base beat is exactly 10 Hz (and the others 12.36, 15.28 and 20 Hz). Whether beats at such rates do anything beyond being heard is a hypothesis, treated in Chapter 41.

In 36-ET the coincidences split: computed the same way, a 36-ET Cg7 on 110 Hz beats at 6.04 and 6.21 Hz instead of 6.13, 7.46 and 7.67 instead of 7.58, 9.48 instead of 9.37, 12.07 and 12.35 instead of 12.26, plus a beat at 14.92 Hz. The golden pattern is still there, blurred into pairs. (Measurement.)

Where $\varphi^{-6}$ comes from. Root h5 against third h4: $5 - 4\cdot\frac{2}{\varphi} = 5 - 8(\varphi - 1) = 13 - 8\varphi$. And $13 - 8\varphi = \varphi^{-6}$, because $\varphi^6 = 8\varphi + 5$ and $(13 - 8\varphi)(8\varphi + 5) = 64\varphi + 65 - 64\varphi^2 = 1$. The Fibonacci approximant $13/8 \approx \varphi$ is the source of the base rate: $f_0(13 - 8\varphi) = f_0\varphi^{-6}$.

Every other pair in the table is a golden multiple of this one: third h5 – fifth h4 and fifth h5 – seventh h4 are the same pattern on notes $2/\varphi$ and $4/\varphi^2$ higher; root h3 – fifth h2 gives $|3 - 2\cdot 4/\varphi^2| = |3 - 8(2-\varphi)| = |8\varphi - 13| = 13 - 8\varphi = \varphi^{-6}$; root h6 – fifth h4 gives $|6 - 16(2 - \varphi)| = 2\varphi^{-6}$; root h2 – seventh h1 gives $2 - 8/\varphi^3 = 2\varphi^{-6}$.

The list is complete for this model: enumerating all pairs of the six partials of the four notes, the only differences below 15 Hz at $f_0 = 110$ Hz are 6.13, 7.58, 9.37 and 12.26 Hz, and the same four appear with eight partials. (Checked by computation.) The arithmetic is exact; how loud each beat is depends on the partial amplitudes.

The timbre flip

Two facts frame this section. The first is physical: the instruments you can touch are rational. The strings, winds and voices that carry harmony have overtones at (nearly) whole-number multiples of the fundamental; bars and bells are inharmonic, but none of them is golden. On such tones C major is the deepest rest the ear knows. Arriving on it from golden harmony is the ultimate release. The second is the author's experience: the golden triad has a pull of its own. He hears it as a local minimum of tension, a delicate, metastable home: less stable than C major, yet a place one can settle into and come back to.

A picture helps, as long as it is taken as a picture. Imagine tension as hilly ground and a chord as a ball resting on it. C major is the bottom of the deepest valley. The golden triad, in the author's model, is a small hollow higher up the slope: the ball can stay there while nothing pushes it, and a push sends it down to C major. Listening to golden harmony for long stretches, the author finds that it changes how he hears the overtones and how he feels. That is his observation and a hypothesis, discussed in Chapter 41, not a measured effect.

First the familiar instrument, then a laboratory one. On a harmonic organ, Cg – C – Cg: C is the arrival, Cg the suspended chord around it. Then the same three chords on a digital golden-spectrum pad, where the model's order flips.

The second example tests the hollow directly. It walks in four equal steps from the exact golden triad to the just major triad 4:5:6, widening the third and narrowing the fifth a little at a time, first on harmonic tones and then on golden-spectrum tones. Listen for whether the first chord feels like a hollow you have to climb out of, or a point on a slope.

Global and local minima

Think of a measure of tension as a function of the chord's tuning. A minimum is a chord from which every small change of tuning raises the tension. It is global if no chord anywhere is lower, and local if it is only the lowest in its own neighbourhood, with lower ground elsewhere. Metastable is a physicist's word for a state of the second kind: stable against small disturbances, not against large ones. In the author's model of golden harmony:

  • C major is the global minimum and the ultimate release, on every real instrument.
  • The golden triad is a local minimum, a delicate home one can dwell in, unstable relative to C major.

The first point agrees with every model in this book: on harmonic tones the major triad, and above all the just major triad, is among the smoothest and least ambiguous chords there are. (Roughness alone rates the 12-ET sus4, 1.23, a little below 12-ET major, 1.32; the root, harmonicity and learned expectation make the major triad the home, Chapter 12.) The second point is the author's model and listening experience. It can be tested in two ways: by models of the sound, and by listeners. The models come first.

On harmonic tones the models show no local minimum. The exact golden triad sits on a broad, almost level shoulder: within 3 c its roughness changes by less than 0.004, and on the circles at 5 and 10 c every one of the 360 neighbours is smoother. Both roughness and triad harmonic entropy (the sum of the entropies of its three intervals, Erlich's model with $\sigma = 17$ c) fall from the golden triad toward the just major triad, where the entropy is 13.663 and the roughness 1.186, against 1.319 for 12-ET major. On the golden spectrum the golden triad is a strict local minimum (measured): every triad on every circle from 1 to 30 c is rougher, by 0.02 to 1.07. The 36-ET Cg, whose third and fifth are 0.24 c and 0.49 c flat of exact, measures 2.10, still inside the hollow. And the walk example climbs out of it: 2.08, 2.50, 2.77, 2.94, 3.05, while on harmonic tones the same walk goes downhill, 1.50, 1.48, 1.42, 1.33, 1.19.

The flip as a laboratory

The two roughness columns of the first section rank the triads in opposite ways:

Harmonic partialssus4, major, minor, augmented, bronze, golden, silver, diminishedEvery acoustic instrument. C major is the global minimum and the release; golden chords pass, or, in the author's model, dwell as a delicate local rest.
Golden partialsgolden, sus4, silver, bronze, augmented, major, diminished, minorDigital synthesis only. The laboratory: the golden triad is the measured rest, and Western triads are rougher.

This is the timbre flip: the spectrum decides which family of chords the roughness model puts at rest. It is not a claim that some instrument turns golden. It is a laboratory exercise, and it has four stages:

  1. Immerse. On golden-spectrum tones, golden harmony can be heard on its own terms, with the golden triad as the smoothest chord. What is delicate on real instruments is, in the laboratory, the floor of the valley.
  2. Ground yourself. Stay there long enough for golden homes and their motions, the golden circle, breathing and planing (Chapter 31), to become familiar rather than strange.
  3. Study. Compare what you hear with the measurements and the theorems: the lattice, the combination tones, the beats.
  4. Return. Play the same progressions on harmonic instruments, where C major is the home again and the golden triad is the delicate local rest, and keep what carries over.

The goal of the exercise is a more developed theory of harmony that has room for both kinds of rest, not the replacement of one by the other. The grammar of golden harmony is the same on both timbres. In Part VII, Chapters 27 and 28 treat Western homes with golden passing chords; Chapter 31 builds golden homes, with the golden circle, breathing and rational visitation, in which a Western chord is the moment of release before the return to the golden rest. Two cautions. First, the flip is specific to golden chords: on golden partials silver and bronze (3.08 and 3.10) sit among the Western chords (sus4 2.99, major 3.17), far from golden's 2.08. Second, "rest" in the laboratory means "smoothest in a roughness model". Western mirror. Chapter 3 shows that consonance depends on timbre (Consonance depends on timbre), and Chapter 17 that orchestration can change what counts as a stable chord (Timbre shapes harmony). The golden world is the most extreme case: the same chord is rougher than every Western triad except diminished on harmonic tones, and the smoothest triad of all on a digital golden spectrum.

Definitions. Write $R(t, f)$ for the modelled roughness of the triad with third $t$ and fifth $f$ cents above the root. A triad $p$ is a strict local minimum of $R$ if some $\rho > 0$ makes $R(q) > R(p)$ for every $q \ne p$ with $|q - p| \le \rho$, and a global minimum if $R(q) \ge R(p)$ for every $q$ in the region considered. The circle test above checks 360 directions on six radii; it is a numerical check, not a proof.

Statement. In the roughness model of the first section (8 harmonic partials or the 11 golden partials below 9, amplitudes $0.88^k$, chords on C4):

  • harmonic: $R(\text{golden}) = 1.50 > R(\text{aug}) = 1.45 > R(\text{minor}) = 1.34 > R(\text{major}) = 1.32$, and $R(\text{golden}) < R(\text{dim}) = 1.67$;
  • golden: $R(\text{golden}) = 2.08 < R(\text{sus4}) = 2.99 < R(\text{aug}) = 3.14 < R(\text{major}) = 3.17 < R(\text{dim}) = 3.19 < R(\text{minor}) = 3.26$;
  • harmonic: the exact golden triad is not a local minimum of $R$ or of the triad harmonic entropy; golden: it is a strict local minimum of $R$ for every radius tested, 1 to 30 c.

These are measurements (they depend on the model's parameters).

Why the minima are sharp. When two partials coincide exactly, a small detuning $\delta$ (in Hz) gives them the roughness $e^{-3.5\,s\delta} - e^{-5.75\,s\delta} \approx 2.25\,s\,\delta$, which grows in proportion to $\delta$, the steepest part of the curve. A chord with many exactly coincident partials therefore sits at the point of a V: every detuning, in any direction, raises its roughness in proportion to the distance. The just major triad has such coincidences on harmonic tones, and the exact golden triad has them on the golden spectrum (the lattice argument of the second section). On harmonic tones the golden triad has no exact coincidences, so nothing pins it: its neighbourhood is smooth, and the surface simply slopes. The ring numbers show the V on the golden spectrum: the rise is 0.023, 0.066 and 0.108 at 1, 3 and 5 c, close to proportional to the radius. (Derived here; consistent with the computed values.)

Answer

$f_0 = 6\varphi^6 = 6 \times 17.944 = 107.67$ Hz.

Exercise 26.3. Find the combination tones $f_2 - f_1$ and $2f_1 - f_2$ of the golden sixth $f_0\cdot(1, \varphi)$, and show they are chain pitches.

Answer

$f_0(\varphi - 1) = f_0\varphi^{-1}$ and $f_0(2 - \varphi) = f_0\varphi^{-2}$: the chain continued downward by one and two links.

Exercise 26.4. Show that the third's third partial and the seventh's second partial of an exact Cg7 beat at $(2/\varphi)\,f_0\varphi^{-6}$.

Answer

$3\cdot\frac2\varphi - 2\cdot\frac8{\varphi^3} = \frac2\varphi\big(3 - \frac8{\varphi^2}\big)$, and $3 - 8/\varphi^2 = 3 - 8(2 - \varphi) = 8\varphi - 13 = -\varphi^{-6}$. The difference is negative (the seventh's partial is the higher one), and the beat rate is its size, $(2/\varphi)\,f_0\varphi^{-6}$.

Exercise 26.5 (listening). In the timbre-flip example, which chord sounds like the resolution in each half? Does your answer agree with the roughness table?

Answer

The model predicts C in the first half (harmonic organ) and Cg in the second (digital golden pad). Listeners vary; register, loudness and the tone's attack also affect the impression.

Exercise 26.6. From the circle table, is the exact golden triad a local minimum of harmonic-tone roughness within 5 c? Of golden-spectrum roughness? What would the table have to show for the author's local minimum to appear in the harmonic model?

Answer

Harmonic: no; every triad on the 5 c circle is smoother. Golden spectrum: yes; every triad on every circle tested is rougher. For a local minimum in the harmonic model the "lowest minus golden" entries would have to be positive, with 0% lower, at least for the small radii.

Exercise 26.7 (listening, a laboratory session). Listen to the walk example several times. Then listen only to its first chord on harmonic tones for a minute, and then to its last. Write down which one you would rather stay on, and whether that changes after the minute. Repeat on the golden-spectrum half.

Answer

There is no right answer: this is a measurement of you. The roughness model predicts the last chord (just major) as the rest on harmonic tones and the first (golden) on the golden spectrum. The author's model predicts that the golden chord on harmonic tones can also be a place to stay, though a less stable one than major; whether that holds for you, and whether it changes with long listening, is the kind of observation Chapter 41 proposes to collect.
