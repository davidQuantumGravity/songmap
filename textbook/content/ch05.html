Part I ended with a lattice of ratios built from the primes 2, 3 and 5. This chapter keeps only the first two. Take the octave (2/1) and the perfect fifth (3/2), and build a whole musical system from them. The result is Pythagorean tuning: the oldest systematic tuning in the Western tradition, and the model for everything else in this book, including the golden system of Part VI. Its central fact is a small failure. Twelve fifths almost make seven octaves, but not quite. That gap, the Pythagorean comma, drives the whole history of tuning that Chapter 7 tells.

Stacking fifths

After the octave, the perfect fifth is the most stable interval there is. Its two notes share many partials, and when it is tuned exactly it does not beat at all (Chapter 3). So it is natural to ask: what happens if we build notes from fifths alone?

Start on a low C and go up a pure fifth to G. From G go up another fifth to D, then to A, then to E. Each step sounds equally stable, but the notes climb out of range quickly: four fifths already span more than two octaves. The fix is to move notes down by whole octaves, which our ears treat as "the same note" (Chapter 1). Folded into one octave, the fifths give the familiar steps of a scale. Do this with seven notes, starting one fifth below C, and you get the major scale, tuned the Pythagorean way.

Listen to the final chord. The fifth C–G is calm, but the third C–E has a fast shimmer. Hold on to that impression: it is the first sign that fifths alone cannot give everything a musician wants, and it is the starting point of Chapter 7.

A pure fifth multiplies frequency by 3/2. Stacking intervals multiplies their ratios, and adding their sizes in cents (Chapter 1). Two fifths up from C give D at $(3/2)^2 = 9/4$ of C's frequency. That is more than an octave, so we halve it: $9/4 \div 2 = 9/8$, the Pythagorean whole tone, 203.91 cents. Going down a fifth from C and up an octave gives F at $2 \cdot 2/3 = 4/3$, the pure fourth, 498.04 c.

Every note is reached by some number $k$ of fifths up (or down, if $k$ is negative), then folded by octaves. The seven notes from $k = -1$ (F) to $k = 5$ (B) give the Pythagorean major scale:

The third C–E is $81/64$ = 407.82 c, four fifths minus two octaves. The simplest major third, the one in the harmonic series, is $5/4$ = 386.31 c. The Pythagorean third is 21.51 c wider, which is why it shimmers. Fifths alone cannot make the 5/4 third, because 5 is a prime that powers of 2 and 3 never reach (Chapter 4).

Definition. Let $x = \log_2(3/2) \approx 0.5849625$ be the fifth measured in octaves. The Pythagorean note at chain position $k \in \mathbb{Z}$ is the ratio

$$r_k = \left(\tfrac{3}{2}\right)^{k} \cdot 2^{-\lfloor kx \rfloor}, \qquad 1 \le r_k < 2,$$

that is, $k$ fifths folded into the octave $[1, 2)$. In cents its size is $1200\,\{kx\}$, where $\{\cdot\}$ is the fractional part. Every $r_k$ has the form $2^a 3^b$: Pythagorean tuning is 3-limit tuning.

Deviation from 12-ET. A 12-ET fifth is 700 c, so the 12-ET note named by chain position $k$ sits at $700k \bmod 1200$. The Pythagorean note sits at $701.955\,k \bmod 1200$, so the two differ by $1.955\,k$ cents, up to a whole octave. For $|k| \le 25$ this is less than half a semitone, so the 12-ET key nearest the Pythagorean note is the one with the same name. The table's last column is this formula.

Theorem (fifths never make octaves). For integers $k \ne 0$ and $m$, $(3/2)^k \ne 2^m$.

Proof. Suppose $(3/2)^k = 2^m$ with $k > 0$ (for $k < 0$ take reciprocals). Then $3^k = 2^{m+k}$. The left side is odd and greater than 1; a power of 2 greater than 1 is even. Contradiction. ∎

Consequently all the $r_k$ are different, and $x$ is irrational: if $x = m/k$ we would have $(3/2)^k = 2^m$.

The chain of fifths

Keep going. After B comes F♯, then C♯, G♯, D♯ and on to the sharps of sharps; below F come B♭, E♭, A♭ and the flats. Laid out in order, the notes form an endless line, the chain of fifths. Twelve consecutive links give a note for every key of the keyboard. Here are the twelve from E♭ to G♯, first in chain order and then sorted into a chromatic scale.

The chromatic scale is not quite even. Some semitones are a little smaller than others. On a keyboard you would never notice the pattern by looking, but a careful ear catches the unevenness in a slow chromatic run.

Three patterns are worth noticing.

  • The letters repeat every seven links. The seven natural letters occupy positions −1 to 5 in the order F C G D A E B. Seven links further on, the same letters return with a sharp: F♯ C♯ G♯ D♯ A♯ E♯ B♯. Seven links in the other direction they return with a flat. A sharp means "seven fifths further up the chain"; a flat means "seven fifths further down".
  • Enharmonic notes are different notes. F♯ ($k = 6$) is 611.73 c; G♭ ($k = -6$) is 588.27 c. They are twelve links apart, and they differ by 23.46 c. On a modern piano they are one key. In Pythagorean tuning they are not.
  • The chain never closes. By the theorem of the last section, no two positions give the same note. The chain is a line, not a circle.

The figure draws the twelve notes on the familiar twelve-position circle, in chain order. Eleven links are pure fifths. The twelfth, from G♯ back to E♭, is not a fifth at all, as the next sections show. The circle of fifths of Chapter 8 closes only because equal temperament forces it to.

The golden system has its own chain: stack the golden sixth φ (833.09 c) instead of the fifth. Its links fold into the octave just as fifths do, and like the chain of fifths it never closes, because powers of φ are never powers of 2. Chain distance 3 is a semitone (99.27 c, within a cent of the 12-ET semitone), 9 is nearly a minor third and 12 nearly a major third (Chapter 23).

Spelling from position. Number the natural letters by their chain positions, F = −1, C = 0, G = 1, D = 2, A = 3, E = 4, B = 5. The note at position $k$ has letter $L(k)$ = the natural at position $((k+1) \bmod 7) - 1$ and carries

$$s(k) = \left\lfloor \frac{k+1}{7} \right\rfloor$$

sharps ($s > 0$) or $|s|$ flats ($s < 0$). For example $k = 6$: $s = 1$, letter F, so F♯. $k = -8$: $s = -1$, letter F, so F♭. $k = 13$: $s = 2$, letter F, so F double-sharp.

Size of a sharp. Raising a note by a sharp moves it 7 positions up the chain. In ratio terms it multiplies by $(3/2)^7$ and then folds down 4 octaves:

$$\frac{3^7}{2^7 \cdot 2^4} = \frac{2187}{2048} \approx 113.69\ \text{c}.$$

So in Pythagorean tuning every sharp raises by exactly 113.69 c and every flat lowers by the same amount. This interval is the apotome.

Two semitones: limma and apotome

In the Pythagorean scale the half step E–F is small and tight. The half step F–F♯ is wider. Both are called "semitones" in ordinary speech, but they are different intervals, and you can hear the difference when they are played one after the other. Then comes a more surprising pair: C♯ and D♭, which share a piano key, are not the same note here. C♯ is higher. Played together they beat.

The two semitones have Greek names, inherited by medieval theory through Boethius:

  • the limma ("remnant") $= 256/243 \approx$ 90.22 c, the diatonic semitone between two letters, as in E–F or B–C;
  • the apotome ("a cutting-off") $= 2187/2048 \approx$ 113.69 c, the chromatic semitone between a letter and its sharp, as in F–F♯ or C–C♯.

Their chain distances explain them. E is at position 4 and F at −1, five links apart. Going five fifths down and three octaves up gives the limma: $2^8/3^5 = 256/243$. F♯ is seven links above F, and seven fifths up less four octaves is the apotome, $3^7/2^{11} = 2187/2048$.

A whole tone is one of each:

$$\frac{256}{243} \cdot \frac{2187}{2048} = \frac{9}{8}, \qquad 90.22 + 113.69 = 203.91\ \text{c}.$$

C–C♯ is an apotome (113.69 c) and C–D♭ is a limma (90.22 c), so C♯ is higher than D♭, by 113.69 − 90.22 = 23.46 c. This is the opposite of meantone tuning (Chapter 7), where D♭ is higher than C♯. In Pythagorean tuning sharps are high and flats are low.

The twelve-note chromatic scale of the last section, E♭ to G♯, contains seven limmas and five apotomes. Which semitones are which depends on where the chain is cut, but the count is always seven and five.

The golden 13-note scale has the same two-semitone structure. The golden apotome is three golden sixths minus two octaves, $\varphi^3/4$ = 99.27 c; the golden limma is seven octaves minus ten golden sixths, $2^7/\varphi^{10}$ = 69.10 c. golden13 has ten golden apotomes and three golden limmas (Chapter 23).

Definitions. The limma is $\lambda = 2^8/3^5$ and the apotome is $\alpha = 3^7/2^{11}$. In cents, $1200\log_2\lambda = 3 \cdot 1200 - 5 \cdot 701.955\ldots \approx 90.22$ and $1200\log_2\alpha = 7 \cdot 701.955\ldots - 4 \cdot 1200 \approx 113.69$, where $701.955\ldots = 1200\log_2(3/2)$.

Proposition. (i) $\lambda\alpha = 9/8$. (ii) $\alpha/\lambda = 3^{12}/2^{19}$, the Pythagorean comma. (iii) $7\lambda$ and $5\alpha$ make an octave exactly: $\lambda^7\alpha^5 = 2$.

Proof. (i) $2^8 \cdot 3^7 / (3^5 \cdot 2^{11}) = 3^2/2^3$. (ii) $3^7 \cdot 3^5/(2^{11} \cdot 2^8) = 3^{12}/2^{19}$. (iii) Exponents of 2: $7 \cdot 8 - 5 \cdot 11 = 1$. Exponents of 3: $-7 \cdot 5 + 5 \cdot 7 = 0$. So $\lambda^7\alpha^5 = 2^1$. ∎

In cents (iii) reads $7 \times 90.22 + 5 \times 113.69 = 1200$ (exactly, before rounding). Part (ii) is the bridge to the next section: the gap between the two semitones is the comma.

The Pythagorean comma

Stack twelve perfect fifths and you nearly return to where you started, seven octaves up, but not quite. The twelfth fifth overshoots by a sliver: about a quarter of a semitone. Listen to the two notes one after another, then together, and you will hear them beat.

If a keyboard has only twelve keys per octave, that overshoot has to go somewhere. Tune eleven fifths pure and the twelfth must absorb the whole comma. It becomes the wolf: a "fifth" so narrow that it howls.

A pure fifth multiplies frequency by 3/2. Twelve of them multiply it by $(3/2)^{12} \approx 129.75$, while seven octaves multiply it by $2^7 = 128$. The ratio of the two,

$$\frac{(3/2)^{12}}{2^7} = \frac{3^{12}}{2^{19}} = \frac{531441}{524288} \approx 1.01364,$$

is the Pythagorean comma, 23.46 cents. It appears in three guises that are all the same interval:

  • twelve fifths minus seven octaves (C to B♯);
  • apotome minus limma (C–C♯ against C–D♭);
  • any enharmonic pair twelve links apart, such as G♭ and F♯ (588.27 c against 611.73 c).

In the twelve-note tuning from E♭ to G♯, the eleven fifths E♭–B♭, B♭–F, …, C♯–G♯ are pure. The twelfth interval, G♯–E♭, is really a diminished sixth: a fifth short by one comma, 701.96 − 23.46 = 678.49 c. Played as if it were a fifth, it beats hard. Medieval music, built on fifths and fourths with the wolf kept out of the way, could live with this. Music built on thirds and on many keys could not; that is the story of Chapter 7.

With the golden sixth φ instead of the fifth, thirteen steps nearly make nine octaves: $\varphi^{13} \approx 521.0$ against $2^9 = 512$. The overshoot is the golden comma, 30.17 cents, and just as here it equals golden apotome minus golden limma: 99.27 − 69.10 = 30.17 c (Chapter 23).

Definition. The Pythagorean comma is $\kappa = 3^{12}/2^{19}$. In cents, $1200\log_2\kappa = 1200\,(12\log_2 3 - 19) \approx 23.46$.

The wolf. In a 12-note tuning made from the chain positions $k = a, a+1, \dots, a+11$, the interval from position $a+11$ up to position $a$ (folded) is $(3/2)/\kappa = 2^{18}/3^{11} = 262144/177147$, which is $701.96 - 23.46 = 678.49$ c. It is the only impure fifth.

Distributing the comma. Twelve fifths must make seven octaves on any closed 12-note keyboard. If the fifths have sizes $f_1, \dots, f_{12}$ cents, then $\sum f_i = 8400$, so the total amount by which they fall short of pure is $\sum (701.955 - f_i) = 12 \times 701.955 - 8400 = 23.46$ c, one comma, whatever the tuning. Pythagorean tuning puts all of it into one fifth. Equal temperament shares it equally: each fifth is narrowed by $23.46/12 = 1.955$ c, giving 700 c. Well temperaments share it unevenly (Chapter 7).

Beats. Two pure tones at $f$ and $f\kappa$ beat at $f(\kappa - 1)$ Hz. For C4 = 261.63 Hz that is 3.57 Hz; for C3 it is 1.78 Hz. With harmonic tones the $n$th partials beat $n$ times faster.

Exercise 5.1. Compute the Pythagorean A as a ratio and in cents, from its chain position.

A is three fifths up: $(3/2)^3 = 27/8$, folded down one octave to $27/16$. In cents $3 \times 701.955 - 1200 = 905.87$.

Exercise 5.2. F♯ and G♭ in Pythagorean tuning: which is higher, and by how much? Use chain positions only.

Answer

F♯ is at $k = 6$, G♭ at $k = -6$. They are twelve links apart, so they differ by one comma. F♯ = $6 \times 701.955 - 3600 = 611.73$ c and G♭ = $4800 - 6 \times 701.955 = 588.27$ c. F♯ is higher by 23.46 c.

Continued fractions and why twelve

Since the chain never closes, any keyboard must cut it somewhere. The question is where. A good place is a number of fifths that comes very close to a whole number of octaves, so that the leftover comma is small and can be shared out without spoiling the fifths. Twelve is the first number that works well. Five fifths miss three octaves by almost a semitone. Seven miss four octaves by more than a semitone. Twelve miss seven octaves by only a quarter of a semitone. When that quarter-semitone is spread over all twelve fifths, each is narrowed by an amount so small that it is hard to hear except as a very slow beat.

There is a beautiful piece of mathematics that finds these good numbers systematically, and it says what comes after twelve: 41, then 53.

The question "how many fifths make nearly a whole number of octaves?" asks for whole numbers $n$ and $m$ with $n \cdot x \approx m$, where $x = \log_2(3/2) = 0.5849625\ldots$ is the fifth measured in octaves. In other words, we want fractions $m/n$ close to $x$. The tool for this is the continued fraction (Appendix A). Peel off the whole part, invert the rest, and repeat:

$$x = 0 + \cfrac{1}{1 + \cfrac{1}{1 + \cfrac{1}{2 + \cfrac{1}{2 + \cfrac{1}{3 + \cdots}}}}} \qquad\text{written}\qquad x = [0; 1, 1, 2, 2, 3, 1, 5, 2, 23, \ldots].$$

Cutting the expansion off at each stage gives the convergents, the best fractions of their size:

Why twelve? Three facts, all checked by computation, make 12 special.

  • For every $n$ below 12, $n$ fifths miss the nearest whole number of octaves by at least 90.22 c (the best is $n = 5$). Twelve is the first cycle with a leftover under a quarter-tone.
  • No $n$ below 41 does better than 12 at this. The leftover after 12 fifths, 23.46 c, is beaten first by 41 fifths (19.84 c). This is what it means for 7/12 to be a convergent.
  • Spread over the cycle, 12's leftover narrows each fifth by only 1.96 c. Among equal divisions with fewer than 29 notes, none has a fifth closer to pure than 12-ET's. 29-ET's fifth is 1.49 c wide, a little better, but it needs 29 notes.

So 12 is a sweet spot: few enough notes for a keyboard and a notation, and fifths within 2 c of pure. Musical history also had other reasons, which Chapter 7 explores (12-ET thirds are not good, and the thirds mattered a great deal). The mathematics only says that 12 is where the chain of fifths first nearly closes.

The golden sixth measured in octaves is $\log_2\varphi = 0.69424\ldots = [0; 1, 2, 3, 1, 2, 3, 2, 4, \ldots]$. Its convergents 2/3, 7/10, 9/13 and 25/36 leave, in turn, the golden apotome (+99.27 c), the golden limma (−69.10 c), the golden comma (+30.17 c) and the 36-comma (−8.75 c), exactly as 1/2, 3/5 and 7/12 leave the tone, the limma and the Pythagorean comma. So 13 is the golden twelve, and 36 is the next good golden cycle. Unlike 41 or 53, 36 also contains 12-ET exactly, which is why the golden system lives in 36-ET (Chapter 24).

The algorithm. Set $y_0 = x$. For $i \ge 0$ let $a_i = \lfloor y_i \rfloor$ and, if $y_i$ is not an integer, $y_{i+1} = 1/(y_i - a_i)$. Then $x = [a_0; a_1, a_2, \ldots]$. Because $x$ is irrational (the theorem of the first section), the expansion never stops. Computed to 60 digits,

$$\log_2\tfrac{3}{2} = [0; 1, 1, 2, 2, 3, 1, 5, 2, 23, 2, 2, 1, 1, 55, 1, \ldots].$$

Convergents. With $p_{-1} = 1,\ q_{-1} = 0,\ p_0 = a_0,\ q_0 = 1$, set $p_i = a_i p_{i-1} + p_{i-2}$ and $q_i = a_i q_{i-1} + q_{i-2}$. Then $p_i/q_i$ is the $i$th convergent. For $\log_2(3/2)$ they are

$$\frac{0}{1},\ \frac{1}{1},\ \frac{1}{2},\ \frac{3}{5},\ \frac{7}{12},\ \frac{24}{41},\ \frac{31}{53},\ \frac{179}{306},\ \frac{389}{665},\ \ldots$$

Theorem (best approximation). If $p_i/q_i$ is a convergent of an irrational $x$ and $0 < q < q_{i+1}$, then $|qx - p| \ge |q_i x - p_i|$ for every integer $p$, with equality only when $(p, q) = (p_i, q_i)$. Moreover the signs of $q_i x - p_i$ alternate. (Proof in Appendix A.)

In tuning language, $1200\,(qx - p)$ is the comma left after $q$ fifths and $p$ octaves. The theorem says that the convergent denominators 1, 2, 5, 12, 41, 53, 306, … are exactly the record-setting cycle lengths. A direct check over $q = 1, \dots, 59$ confirms that the records are at $q = 1, 2, 5, 12, 41, 53$.

Two kinds of "best". The theorem measures the total leftover $|qx - p|$. Dividing by $q$ measures the error per fifth in $q$-ET, $1200\,|x - p/q|$. By that measure the record-holders up to 53 are 1, 2, 3, 5, 7 and then 12 (1.96 c), 29 (1.49 c), 41 (0.48 c) and 53 (0.07 c). Here 3-ET (98.04 c) and 7-ET (16.24 c) set records although 3 and 7 are not convergent denominators. The fraction 17/29 is not a convergent but an intermediate fraction (a semiconvergent), $17/29 = (7 + 10)/(12 + 17)$. Both kinds reappear in the next chapter, on the Stern–Brocot tree.

The 53-cycle. Fifty-three fifths exceed thirty-one octaves by $1200\,(53x - 31) = 3.62$ c, a comma often named after Nicholas Mercator, who studied 53-fold division in the 17th century.

Pythagoras and after. Later writers attribute the fifth-generated scale to Pythagoras (6th century BCE); no writings of his survive. Philolaus (5th century BCE) describes the octave as a fourth (4/3) plus a fifth (3/2) and the tone as 9/8, and Plato's Timaeus (c. 360 BCE) fills the scale with tones of 9/8 and the remnant 256/243, the limma. Boethius, in De institutione musica (early 6th century CE), carried this theory into medieval Europe, together with the terms limma, apotome and comma. Medieval music theory was built on these 3-limit ratios.

Exercise 5.3. (Listening.) In the comma example the fundamentals beat at about 3.6 Hz. If the same comparison were made an octave lower, at C3, how fast would the fundamentals beat?

Answer

Half as fast: $130.81 \times (1.01364 - 1) \approx 1.78$ Hz. The comma is a fixed ratio, so its beat rate is proportional to frequency.

Exercise 5.4. Compute the next convergent after 31/53 from the continued fraction $[0; 1, 1, 2, 2, 3, 1, 5, \ldots]$ and check that it is 179/306.

Answer

The partial quotient after the one that produced 31/53 is $a_7 = 5$. With $p_5/q_5 = 24/41$ and $p_6/q_6 = 31/53$: $p_7 = 5 \cdot 31 + 24 = 179$ and $q_7 = 5 \cdot 53 + 41 = 306$.

Exercise 5.5. Prove that no number of pure fourths (4/3) can equal a whole number of octaves.

Answer

$(4/3)^k = 2^m$ with $k > 0$ means $2^{2k} = 3^k 2^m$, so $3^k = 2^{2k - m}$. The left side is odd and greater than 1, the right side is 1 or even (or a fraction). Impossible. (Equivalently, a fourth is an octave minus a fifth, so this reduces to the theorem for fifths.)
