Western modulation is built on common ground. Two keys that share a chord can hand the music from one to the other: the shared chord is heard in the old key and understood in the new one (Chapter 11). Between Western keys on different layers of 36-ET there is no common ground at all, not a single note (Chapter 32). This chapter shows that golden harmony puts the pivot chord back. Every major and minor triad has one nearest golden triad, its golden projection. Keys whose projections overlap share golden chords, and the best-connected pairs of keys on different layers turn out to lie a silver third apart and to share three golden pivot chords, one fewer than the four ordinary triads that C and G major share. The results come from docs/GOLDEN_BLENDING.md and its script reference/golden/blending.py; each is labelled as a theorem, an exhaustive check, a measurement or a proposal.

The golden projection of a triad

Play a C major chord and then bend two of its notes very slightly: the E down by a sixth-step, the G up by a sixth-step. The C stays. The result is Cg, the golden triad on C. Nothing else in 36-ET is that close to C major and golden at the same time. Do the same with D minor, but now the A stays while D bends down and F bends up: you reach vDg. Every major and minor chord has one such golden neighbour, two tiny bends away.

The golden chord does not sound like a new chord in a new key, but like the Western chord seen through slightly warped glass. That closeness makes it useful as a hinge between keys.

Recall the voice-leading distance between two chords with the same number of notes: match the notes one to one, add up how far each note moves (in sixth-steps, the short way round the octave), and take the best matching (Chapter 32). C to Cg costs 2: E moves one sixth-step down, G one up, C not at all.

Definition. The golden projection π(X) of a Western triad X is the golden triad nearest to X in voice-leading distance.

The name is borrowed from geometry. Projecting a point onto a line means finding the nearest point of the line; here the "line" is the set of 36 golden triads inside the space of all three-note chords, and π drops each Western triad onto its nearest golden one. A golden triad is its own projection. (The book already uses shadow for a different relation: a golden chord a sixth-step below a diatonic root that leads up into it, such as vCg before C, Chapter 29. The shadow of C lies below it; the projection of C keeps its root.)

  • a major triad keeps its root and bends third down and fifth up: Cg = C(v3, ^5);
  • a minor triad keeps its fifth and bends root down and third up: Cm = C E♭ G goes to vCg = vC ^E♭ G.

The roots of the six projections are C, vD, vE, F, G and vA: the three major triads keep their roots on the 12-ET layer, and the three minor triads land a sixth-step below their roots, on the down layer. This split between two layers is the whole secret of the pivots in the section after next.

Two more facts complete the picture. First, no Western triad is ever at distance 1 from a golden triad: a single sixth-step bend leaves two notes on the same layer, and a golden triad has one note on each (the layer theorem). So 2 is the smallest possible distance, and major and minor triads achieve it. Second, the next-nearest golden triads to C major, ^Cg and vCg, are already at distance 3, the same as the sixth-step lift C → ^C itself. The projection is not just nearest; it is clearly nearest.

Where projection is used elsewhere. Replacing a diatonic triad by its projection as a held colour, rather than as a pivot, is golden modal mixture by projection (Chapter 38). Chapter 34 uses the same chord as the first and last step of its long modulations along the golden circle (Chapter 34).

Exercise 35.1. Find the golden projections of A♭ major and B minor, and say which note each keeps.

Answer

A♭ = A♭ C E♭ is major, so it keeps its root: π(A♭) = A♭g = A♭ vC ^E♭ (C down a sixth-step, E♭ up). B minor = B D F♯ keeps its fifth: π(Bm) = vBg = vB ^D F♯ (B down, D up).

Write pitch classes in sixth-steps mod 36, $G_r=\{r,r+11,r+22\}$ for the golden triad on $r$, $M_r=\{r,r+12,r+21\}$ and $m_r=\{r,r+9,r+21\}$ for major and minor, $A_r=\{r,r+12,r+24\}$ for augmented. Let $\lambda(p)=p \bmod 3$ be the layer and $d$ the voice-leading distance.

Theorem (projection). $\pi(M_r)=G_r$ and $\pi(m_r)=G_{r-1}$, uniquely, at distance 2. The nearest golden triads of $A_r$ are $G_{r+1}$, $G_{r+13}$, $G_{r+25}$ at distance 2; the nearest of the diminished triad on $r$ is $G_{r-2}$, at distance 4. Status: exhaustive over all 144 triads; the lower bound and the uniqueness are proved below.

Proof (major and minor). A Western triad lies on one layer; a golden triad meets every layer once. So at least two voices must change layer, each by at least one sixth-step: $d\ge 2$. If $d=2$, exactly two voices move by one sixth-step each and one holds. The two moving voices must reach the two other layers, so one moves up ($+1$) and one down ($-1$). That leaves six candidates: choose the held note (3 ways), then which of the other two goes up (2 ways). For C major $\{0,12,21\}$ they are

exactly one of which is golden. Transposition by $r$ commutes with $d$, which gives $\pi(M_r)=G_r$. For C minor $\{0,9,21\}$ the six candidates contain exactly one golden triad, $\{35,10,21\}=G_{35}$ = vCg (G held), so $\pi(m_r)=G_{r-1}$. ∎

The same table shows a little more: every major or minor triad has exactly one golden, one silver and one bronze triad at distance 2 (checked for all 72; for C major: Cg, ^Cs, ^GT13; for C minor: vCg, Cs, E♭T13). All three metallic triads have one note per layer, so each major and minor triad also has a unique nearest silver triad and a unique nearest bronze triad, both at distance 2. Whether silver projections give a pivot relation of their own is an open question.

Preimages. Every golden triad has exactly three Western triads at distance 2, one on each layer: $$G_r=\pi(M_r)=\pi(m_{r+1}),\qquad G_r \text{ is also one of the three projections of } A_{r+11}=\{r+11,r+23,r+35\}.$$ For Cg these are C (layer 0, keeps C), ^Cm (layer 1, keeps ^G) and vE vA♭ vC (layer 2, keeps vE). Proof: from the theorem, $\pi(M_s)=G_r$ forces $s=r$, $\pi(m_s)=G_r$ forces $s=r+1$, and $G_r$ is a projection of $A_a$ iff $r\in\{a+1,a+13,a+25\}$, i.e. $A_a=A_{r+11}$. The layers are $\lambda(r)$, $\lambda(r)+1$ and $\lambda(r+11)=\lambda(r)+2$. ∎

Equivariance. $\pi(X+s)=\pi(X)+s$ for every transposition $s$, because transposition preserves both $d$ and the set of golden triads. This is why every statement in this chapter can be checked on C and transferred to every key.

Neo-Riemannian moves through the golden lens

The neo-Riemannian moves of Chapter 10 change one note of a triad by a small step and turn major into minor: P (C to C minor), L (C to E minor), R (C to A minor). Now look at each pair through the golden lens: project both chords and listen to the golden pair that results.

L survives almost unchanged: C → E minor lowers C to B and keeps E and G; Cg → vEg lowers C to B and keeps vE and ^G. It is the step of the golden circle (Chapter 31). P turns into something new: the whole golden chord slides down a sixth-step, every voice together. R turns into a jump of the golden root by a silver third, the interval that names the key relation of the next section.

The famous hexatonic cycle (C, C minor, A♭, A♭ minor, E, E minor) projects onto a cycle of six golden triads that alternates a sixth-step slide with a golden-circle step.

Because π is a function on major and minor triads, every move between them has a golden image: the move from π(X) to π(Y). Since π commutes with transposition, the image of a move is always a change of golden root by a fixed amount, which depends only on the move and on the quality of the starting triad:

Three remarks.

  • L is the golden circle. This explains the observation of Chapter 31 that the circle step is the leading-tone gesture: the golden circle is the image of L under the projection. From F → Am (an L move) you get Fg → vAg, again a circle step.
  • P becomes planing. Western P keeps two notes; its golden image keeps none, but moves every voice by one sixth-step, the smallest possible motion (Chapter 31). The direction depends on where you start: from major the golden chord slides down, from minor it slides up.
  • R becomes a silver-third root move. R moves a Western root down a minor third (C → Am). Its golden image moves the root down 10 sixth-steps, 333.33 c, the silver third, the interval stacked twice in the silver triad Cs = C ^E♭ vG (Chapter 25).

The sizes of the moves survive too: in total voice motion P and L cost 3 sixth-steps (a semitone) and R costs 6 (a whole tone), in the Western original and in its golden image alike. What does not survive is the common-tone structure.

$$\begin{aligned} \pi P(M_r)&=G_{r-1}, & \pi L(M_r)&=G_{r+11}, & \pi R(M_r)&=G_{r+26}=G_{r-10},\\ \pi P(m_r)&=G_{r}=G_{(r-1)+1}, & \pi L(m_r)&=G_{r+24}=G_{(r-1)-11}, & \pi R(m_r)&=G_{r+9}=G_{(r-1)+10}. \end{aligned}$$

Proof: substitute. ∎ The script confirms all 72 cases.

Distances. $d(G_0,G_{\pm1})=3$ (every voice moves one sixth-step), $d(G_0,G_{\pm11})=3$ (two common tones, one voice moves a semitone), $d(G_0,G_{\pm10})=6$ (no common tone; for Cg → vAg the moves are C +1, vE +1, ^G +4). The Western moves cost $d(M_0,P M_0)=d(M_0, L M_0)=3$ and $d(M_0,R M_0)=6$: π preserves the cost of each move, though not its common tones.

Not a group action. π does not turn P into a single operation on golden triads: $G_r$ is the image of both $M_r$ and $m_{r+1}$, and P sends them to triads with golden roots $r-1$ and $r+1$. The moves act on the 72 Western triads; π only records where each lands.

Keys a silver third apart

Here is the main result of the chapter in sound. C major and ^E♭ major (an E♭ major raised a sixth-step) lie on different layers and share no note. Yet three golden chords belong to both, in the sense that matters: each is the golden projection of a triad in C and of a triad in ^E♭. First the three pivots on their own, each heard as a step from a chord of C, through gold, to a chord of ^E♭.

Now a real modulation. A phrase in C reaches F, the subdominant; F bends into Fg; Fg leans into ^F minor, which is the ii of ^E♭ major; a ii–V7–I in the new key confirms it.

The same works through the dominant: G bends into Gg, golden V of C and golden iii of ^E♭.

And downward: C major and vA major (an A major lowered a sixth-step) are also a silver third apart, in the other direction, and also share three golden pivots. Here the pivot is vAg, golden vi of C and golden I of vA.

On harmonic instruments, as here, the Western chords on both sides are the points of rest; the golden pivot is the brief, delicate moment between them. Arriving in ^E♭ major or vA major is a release.

Projection pivots

Recall the starting point (a theorem, Chapter 32): Western keys on different layers share no note, so they share no chord, and pivot-chord modulation between them is impossible. Call the set of projections of a key's six consonant triads π(K). For C major, π(K) = {Cg, vDg, vEg, Fg, Gg, vAg}, as in the first section. For ^E♭ major (^E♭ ^F ^G ^A♭ ^B♭ ^C ^D) the triads are ^E♭, ^Fm, ^Gm, ^A♭, ^B♭ and ^Cm, and their projections are ^E♭g, Fg, Gg, ^A♭g, ^B♭g and Cg. The two sets share three golden triads:

Why a silver third

^E♭ lies 10 sixth-steps (333.33 c) above C, vA 10 sixth-steps below. That interval is the silver third: a little wider than a minor third (300 c), a little narrower than a neutral third (350 c). In 36-ET it is the distance from C to ^E♭ and from vA to C.

A clearer way to see the relation: the relative minor of ^E♭ major is ^C minor, and the relative minor of C major is A minor, whose parallel major lowered a sixth-step is vA major. So the silver-third relation is the parallel relation shifted by a sixth-step, degree for degree:

  • C major and ^C minor share Cg, Fg and Gg as I/i, IV/iv and V/v;
  • A minor and vA major share vAg, vDg and vEg as i/I, iv/IV and v/V.

That is the projection theorem lifted from chords to keys. π identifies the major triad on r with the minor triad on r + 1 (C with ^Cm), so it identifies a major key with the minor key a sixth-step above it, chord by chord, for the three primary triads.

Exercise 35.3. Step up a silver third three times from C major: C → ^E♭ → ? → ?. Where do you land, how far is that in cents, and how many golden projections does the result share with C major?

Answer

C (0) → ^E♭ (10) → vG (20) → B♭ (30). Three silver thirds are 1000 c, so the third step lands on B♭ major, back on the 12-ET layer a whole tone below C. It shares two projections with C major (vDg and Fg, from Dm and F, the triads the two keys share). Each single step shares three; the relation does not compose. Since 10 has order 18 in $\mathbb{Z}_{36}$, eighteen silver-third steps (5 octaves) return to C through 18 major keys.

For a major key on tonic $t$ let $\pi(K_t)$ be the roots of the projections of its six consonant triads. From the projection theorem, $\pi(K_t)=t+D$ with

$$D=\{0,15,21\}\cup\{5,11,26\},$$

where the first part comes from I, IV, V (major, roots kept, layer of $t$) and the second from ii, iii, vi (minor roots 6, 12, 27 lowered by one, layer $\lambda(t)+2$).

Theorem (silver-third pivots). Two major keys on different layers share at most three golden projections, and exactly three if and only if their tonics differ by $\pm10$. Proof. Let $s\not\equiv 0 \pmod 3$ be the difference of tonics, and work from $t=0$. The set $D$ lies on layers 0 and 2; the set $s+D$ lies on layers $\lambda(s)$ and $\lambda(s)+2$.

  • If $\lambda(s)=1$, then $s+D$ lies on layers 1 and 0, and the only possible overlap is on layer 0, between $\{0,15,21\}$ and $s+\{5,11,26\}$: at most 3. Since $\{5,11,26\}=\{0,15,21\}+26$, equality means $\{0,15,21\}+(s+26)=\{0,15,21\}$. A three-element set is fixed by a translation $u\ne 0$ only if $u\in\{12,24\}$ and the set is an augmented triad, which $\{0,15,21\}$ is not. So $s+26\equiv 0$, $s=10$.
  • If $\lambda(s)=2$, then $s+D$ lies on layers 2 and 1; the overlap is on layer 2, between $\{5,11,26\}$ and $s+\{0,15,21\}$, at most 3, with equality iff $s+\{0,15,21\}=\{0,15,21\}+26$, i.e. $s=26=-10$. ∎

The count for the other differences is the number of pairs $(a,b)\in D^2$ with $b-a\equiv s$. Computed: 1 for $s=\pm4,\pm16$, 2 for $\pm5,\pm11$, 3 for $\pm10$ (layers 1 and 2), and 2 for $\pm6$, 4 for $\pm15$, 6 for 0 (layer 0); 0 for every other shift.

Geodesic pivots. Each shared projection joins a major triad $M_r$ of one key with the minor triad $m_{r+1}$ of the other, and $d(M_r,m_{r+1})=4=d(M_r,G_r)+d(G_r,m_{r+1})=2+2$. For $r=0$ the voices move

$$\text{C}: 0\to0\to1,\qquad \text{E}: 12\to11\to10,\qquad \text{G}: 21\to22\to22,$$

each monotone: C holds then rises to ^C, E falls twice (vE, then ^E♭), G rises to ^G then holds. The golden chord is a geodesic hinge: it lies on a smoothest voice leading between the two Western chords. By transposition the same holds for every $r$; in the vA direction the path is $m_{r+1}\to G_r\to M_r$, e.g. Am → vAg → vA with A $27\to26\to26$, C $0\to1\to2$, E $12\to12\to11$. Chapter 32 found C → Cg → ^Cm as the cheapest golden route from C major to another layer; the theorem shows it is one of three such pivots between C major and ^E♭ major, and names the relation.

Golden hinges and the passing engine across layers

The projection pivots are deliberate: you choose the golden chord. The passing engine of Part VII can also find golden chords between keys on its own. Give it A minor in C and the tonic of ^C major, and ask for a natural passing chord in between.

The engine can also put two different golden chords in a row between keys. From F, the IV of C, to ^E minor, the iii of ^C, it chooses vFg and then ^Eg: two golden chords, then a cadence in ^C.

The passing engine across layers

System 1 is undefined across layers. The strict path splits each voice's motion into three equal sixth-step parts (Chapter 27). A voice that changes layer moves a number of sixth-steps that is not a multiple of 3, which cannot be split evenly. So there is no strict path between keys on different layers. SongMap's engine falls back to moving each voice as early as an even split allows; in all 108 pairs tested (the six consonant triads of C major to the six of ^C, vC♯ and ^E♭ major) that fallback produced no golden chord.

System 2 works. The natural path (Chapter 28) lets each voice move unevenly, and it finds golden chords across layers often:

Exercise 35.4. Am → ^C (distance 7) has a golden hinge, but C → ^C (distance 3) has none. Explain the difference using the moves of each voice.

Answer

In C → ^C every voice must change layer and the smoothest motion moves each by exactly one sixth-step. A hinge would have each of its notes between a note of C and a note of ^C, that is, on one of the two layers, so it touches two layers and cannot be golden. Am → ^C is not a lift: A has to travel to ^G (5 sixth-steps), and on that longer road there is room for an intermediate chord on all three layers. vAg = vA ^C E takes A one sixth-step down, C one up and keeps E (2); then vA → ^G, ^C held, E → ^E (5). Total 7, the direct cost.

Theorem (layer betweenness). Let X be a pc-set on one layer and Y = X + 1. Then d(X, Y) = |X|, and every geodesic hinge between X and Y is a subset of X ∪ Y; so no golden, silver or bronze triad lies on a smoothest voice leading from X to X + 1. Proof sketch (in full in Chapter 34): every voice must change layer, so $d\ge |X|$, and the uniform $+1$ attains it; composing the two matchings through a hinge gives a matching $X\to Y$ of cost $|X|$, so every note of the hinge lies on a one-sixth-step arc and equals one of its two ends. Hence $G\subseteq X\cup Y$, which touches two layers; metallic triads touch three. ∎ (Checked over all 48 twelve-ET triads of the four kinds: 0 golden hinges.)

Strict paths. System 1 moves voice $i$ by $\delta_i/3$ at each of the three stages, which requires $3\mid\delta_i$. Between layers $\delta_i\equiv\lambda(Y)-\lambda(X)\not\equiv0\pmod 3$ for every voice, so no strict path exists.

Method. Hinges were found by minimizing $d(X,G)+d(G,Y)$ over the 36 golden triads; paths by SongMap's four-part natural engine. "Two different golden chords" excludes one golden chord held over both passing slots.

One golden chord, three meanings

A German augmented sixth and a dominant seventh sound the same on a piano; only where they go tells you which one you heard (Chapter 11). A golden triad has the same ambiguity, three ways over. Listen to Cg three times, each time followed by a different continuation: back to C, on to ^C minor and ^E♭ major, and through an augmented chord into vA major.

From the golden chord alone you cannot tell which story is being told. The ear waits for the next chord, as it does in any good modulation.

The preimage theorem of the first section says that Cg is the projection of exactly three Western triads at distance 2, one on each layer. Each of them lives in several keys, so Cg carries a meaning in every one of them:

Among major keys, Cg is a projection in exactly six: three on the 12-ET layer (through the C major triad) and three on the up layer (through ^C minor). It belongs to no major key on the down layer. There, its only Western source is the augmented triad, which occurs in harmonic and melodic minor keys and as a chromatic augmented dominant. This is the golden analogue of enharmonic reinterpretation: one sound, several spellings, and the spelling is decided by the continuation.

  • As I of C (or IV of G, V of F): Cg → C, the release of golden mixture.
  • As vi of ^E♭ (or i of ^C minor): Cg → ^Cm, the second half of the projection pivot. ^G is the note held.
  • As the augmented dominant of vA: Cg → vE+ (C sinks to vC, ^G rises to vA♭, vE holds), then vE+ → vA, an ordinary augmented-dominant resolution on the down layer. vA major is the other silver-third key of C.

Because π commutes with transposition, every golden triad has the same three-fold structure: Fg is I of F, IV of C and V of B♭ (through F major), ii of ^E♭, iii of ^C♯ and vi of ^A♭ (through ^F minor), and one of the three projections of the augmented triad vA vC♯ vF.

Status. The lists of keys are exhaustive computations; the claim that the continuation decides how Cg is heard is a hypothesis about perception, to be tested.

Exercise 35.5. Which three Western triads have Gg as their projection at distance 2, and on which layers are they? In which major key is Gg golden I, and in which is it golden vi?

Answer

Transpose the Cg case by 21 (C → G): G major (layer 0, keeps G), ^G minor = ^G ^B♭ ^D (layer 1, keeps ^D), and the augmented triad vB vE♭ vG (layer 2, keeps vB). Gg is golden I of G major and golden vi of ^B♭ major, whose vi is ^G minor (it is also golden i of ^G minor). On the up layer it can never be a golden I, because its preimage there is a minor triad.

Proposition (meanings of a golden triad). $G_r\in\pi(K_t)$ for a major key $K_t$ iff $r-t\in D$, i.e. $t\in r-D=\{r,\,r-5,\,r-11,\,r-15,\,r-21,\,r-26\}$. These are six keys: $r$, $r-15$, $r-21$ on the layer of $r$ (degrees I, IV, V) and $r-5$, $r-11$, $r-26$ on layer $\lambda(r)+1$ (degrees ii, iii, vi). Proof: $\pi(K_t)=t+D$, and the layers follow from those of $D$. ∎ For $r=0$: C, G, F and ^B♭, ^A♭, ^E♭. No major key on layer $\lambda(r)+2$ contains a preimage of $G_r$, because that preimage, $A_{r+11}$, is augmented.

Augmented triads. In 12-ET the augmented triad on the third degree of harmonic minor is shared by three keys (C E G♯ is III+ of A, C♯ and F harmonic minor). Lowered a sixth-step, vE vA♭ vC $=A_{11}$ is III+ of vA, vC♯ and vF harmonic minor (computed). Its three nearest golden triads are $G_{12}$ = Eg, $G_{24}$ = A♭g and $G_{36}=G_0$ = Cg, all with roots on the 12-ET layer: the projections of an augmented triad lie one layer above it.

Between golden keys and Western keys

So far the pivots joined two Western keys. A golden key, such as C golden7, has its own chords: five golden triads (Cg, C♯g, vEg, vFg, ^A♭g; Chapter 32). Which Western key leads most naturally into it? The surprising answer is F harmonic minor, whose dominant is C. Listen to F minor's i, VI, III+ and V, then V bending into Cg and a walk through C♯g, vFg and ^A♭g back to Cg: golden images of those same four chords. Then the way back.

Exercise 35.6. F harmonic minor misses vEg. Which Western triads project onto vEg, and why is none of them in F harmonic minor?
