Chromaticism inside tonality

Play a short chain of chords that stays inside the C major scale, and then the same plan again with a few "wrong" notes slipped in: a B♭ here, an A♭ there, a C♯ on the way to D minor. The second version is no less in C major. Its home is the same and its cadence is the same, but it has gained a line you can sing along the top: C, B♭, A, A♭, G, sliding down by semitones. That is the core of chromatic harmony: notes from outside the scale that do not leave the key, but make its voices move by the smallest step the keyboard has.

Romantic music from Schubert to Wagner, Liszt, Franck and Richard Strauss used such chords more and more densely, until in some passages almost every chord contains a note foreign to the key. Listen for two things that chromatic harmony does at once: it colors (a minor subdominant in a major key sounds shaded, a secondary dominant sounds bright and urgent), and it connects, because the foreign notes almost always sit a semitone from the notes they replace.

Most chromatic chords of the tonal era belong to a few families you have met already:

All of these chords have one property in common: each foreign note lies a semitone from a note of the chords around it. In the example above, B♭ lies between C and A, A♭ between A and G, and C♯ between C and D. The chromatic note is a passing or leading note blown up into a chord. The augmented sixth makes this literal: A♭ and F♯ exist only to move a semitone outward to G.

So chromatic harmony is mainly a matter of voice leading. Two chords sound close when their voices can move to each other by small steps, whatever their positions in the key. That principle, measured in Chapter 14, becomes the main tool of the rest of this chapter.

When. Chromatic chords are old: augmented sixths and the Neapolitan were standard by the 18th century, and Gesualdo's madrigals (around 1600) already move by startling chromatic steps. What changed in the 19th century was density and independence: chromatic chords began to follow each other directly, with the tonic reached late or only implied.
Golden mirror. Chromaticism splits the whole tone into two semitones and lets voices travel by the smaller step. 36-ET splits the semitone again, into three sixth-steps, and the same smooth voice leading can then pass through golden chords (Chapter 27). The full chromatic toolkit of this Part, carried into 36 pitch classes, is Chapter 33.

A measure of chromatic distance. Write a chord as a list of pitch classes in $\mathbb{Z}_{12}$. The voice-leading distance from $X = (x_1,\dots,x_n)$ to $Y$ is the smallest total motion

$$d(X,Y) = \min_{\sigma} \sum_{i=1}^{n} \bigl| y_{\sigma(i)} - x_i \bigr|_{12},$$

where $\sigma$ runs over the ways of assigning the notes of $Y$ to the voices of $X$, and $|k|_{12}$ is the shorter way round the circle (between 0 and 6 semitones). This is the "taxicab" distance of Chapter 14, folded into pitch classes.

Closeness of keys (common notes in the scale, Chapter 8) and closeness of chords (small voice motion) are different things. Tonal harmony rests on the first; Romantic chromaticism increasingly used the second.

The Tristan chord

Wagner's Tristan und Isolde (composed 1857–59, first performed in Munich in 1865) opens with a phrase that ends on a chord of four notes: F, B, D♯ and G♯. It is probably the most discussed chord in Western music. Listen to it alone first. It is not a major or minor triad, nor a dominant seventh, and it does not sound like a destination. Then listen to it move: the top voice rises G♯, A, A♯, B while the lower notes slip by semitones, and the music lands on a dominant seventh chord on E. That chord does not resolve either. In the opera the phrase stops there, in silence.

Now hear the same four notes treated two ways. First they go as in Tristan, to E7. Then they are respelled F–A♭–C♭–E♭ and treated as an ordinary "half-diminished" chord on the second degree of E♭ minor, moving to B♭7 and E♭ minor. The sound is identical, but the meaning changes completely.

What kind of chord is it? Several answers have been defended for more than a century, and each is partly right:

  • A half-diminished seventh. Respelled, F–A♭–C♭–E♭ stacks minor third, minor third, major third: the "half-diminished" seventh chord (Fm7♭5). This reading accounts for its sound, but not for where it goes.
  • An augmented sixth chord with an appoggiatura. Take the top G♯ as an appoggiatura (an accented non-chord tone, Chapter 12) to A. When the top voice reaches A, the chord is F–A–B–D♯, a French augmented sixth in A minor, which goes normally to E (the dominant of A minor). This reading explains the destination and the voice leading.
  • A chord of voice leading. Each voice moves by semitone into the next chord, and the chord's identity matters less than the motion. This is the reading this chapter develops.

The key of A minor is implied by the E7 and never confirmed by an A minor chord at this point. That suspended tonality is a large part of the effect. Harmony that implies a tonic and then withholds it is one of the paths by which Romantic chromaticism leads to the music of the next chapters.

A caution about the score. The audio and the figure here are a four-voice reduction written for this book, so that you can hear the chord and its motion clearly. They are not Wagner's orchestration or exact voicing. What is certain is the chord's pitch content (F, B, D♯, G♯), the top line's rise G♯–A–A♯–B, and the arrival on a dominant seventh on E.
Golden mirror. The two semitone moves that take the Tristan chord to E7 (F → E and D♯ → D) would each take three sixth-steps in 36-ET, and the chords in between are new four-note sonorities. None of them is golden, even if the two voices move at different times (computed): both moving voices fall, and a golden passing chord needs one voice that holds, one that rises and one that falls. That rule, and the general machinery of sub-chromatic passing chords, are in Chapter 27. Set-class thinking in 36 pitch classes, which is how one would classify a "golden Tristan chord", is in Chapter 33.

The chord as a set class. As pitch classes, the Tristan chord is $\{5, 11, 3, 8\}$. Its prime form (the most compact representative up to transposition and inversion, Chapter 20) is $\{0,2,5,8\}$, Forte's set class 4-27. The dominant seventh $\{0,4,7,10\}$ and the half-diminished seventh $\{0,3,6,10\}$ also belong to this class, because each is the inversion of the other. Its interval vector is $\langle 0,1,2,1,1,1\rangle$ (computed). So the Tristan chord is, abstractly, an inverted dominant seventh, which is one reason it sounds tense but not chaotic.

The voice leading (computed). The smallest voice leading from $\{F, B, D♯, G♯\}$ to the E7 chord $\{E, G♯, B, D\}$ is

$$F \to E,\quad B \to B,\quad D♯ \to D,\quad G♯ \to G♯,$$

a total of 2 semitones: two voices fall by a semitone and two hold. Measured by the distance of the previous section, the Tristan chord is as close to E7 as C major is to A♭ major. The famous rising top line is an embellishment above this skeleton.

Hexatonic and octatonic cycles

Start on C major and change one note at a time, by a single semitone: lower E to E♭ (C minor), raise G to A♭ (A♭ major), lower C to B (A♭ minor, spelled G♯ minor), raise E♭ to E (E major), lower A♭ to G (E minor), raise B to C, and you are home. Six chords, each a hair's breadth from the last, and a full circle through keys that are far apart in the circle of fifths. Listen to the cycle, and then to its most surprising pair: C major straight to A♭ minor, where every voice moves a semitone.

A second cycle alternates a semitone move with a whole-tone move: C, C minor, E♭ major, E♭ minor, F♯ major, F♯ minor, A major, A minor, and back to C. It takes eight chords, and the roots climb by minor thirds.

The first cycle uses only six pitch classes: C, E♭, E, G, A♭, B. That collection is a hexatonic scale, which alternates semitones and minor thirds. All six chords of the cycle (three major, three minor) live inside it. The second cycle uses eight pitch classes, C, C♯, E♭, E, F♯, G, A, B♭, the octatonic scale, which alternates semitones and whole tones (Chapter 19).

The music theorist Richard Cohn, in work from the 1990s, showed how much late-Romantic music is organized by such cycles, and named their systems:

  • There are exactly four hexatonic systems, each containing six triads. Every major or minor triad belongs to exactly one.
  • There are exactly three octatonic systems, each containing eight triads. Again every triad belongs to exactly one.
  • Within a hexatonic system, the two triads that share no note, such as C major and A♭ minor, are hexatonic poles. They are as far apart as two chords of the system can be, yet only three semitones of motion separate them. Cohn associated this pole relation with uncanny or otherworldly moments in late-Romantic music.

The augmented triad sits at the center of each hexatonic system. Move any one of its notes by a semitone, up or down, and you get a major or minor triad: from C–E–G♯ you reach C, A minor, A♭ (spelled with G♯), F minor, E and C♯ minor, six triads in all (computed). Cohn calls these six the augmented triad's Weitzmann region, after Carl Friedrich Weitzmann's 1853 study of the augmented triad. The augmented triad itself is symmetric (Chapter 9), which is why it can lead equally well to all six.

Roots in a hexatonic system move by major thirds (C, A♭, E). This is the familiar chromatic mediant of Chapter 11, now seen as part of a cycle. In an octatonic system the roots move by minor thirds (C, E♭, F♯, A), the relation behind many Romantic sequences.

Golden mirror. Collapse each pair of moves in the hexatonic cycle (C → Cm → A♭) into one major-third root move, C → A♭. The smoothest voice leading holds C and moves E down and G up a semitone each, and in 36-ET its uniform path is C – Cg – ^E♭T13 – A♭ (computed with reference/golden/passing.py): a golden triad on the first root, then its bronze partner. The same holds for A♭ → E and E → C, so the hexatonic cycle of major chords becomes a cycle of golden passing chords. This is the quality theorem at work (same-quality triads a third apart, Chapter 27), and Chapter 29 makes music of it. The golden counterpart of the hexatonic collection itself, the nine notes of Cg, Eg and A♭g, is in Chapter 33.

Theorem (computed exhaustively). Let $\mathcal{T}$ be the 24 major and minor triads.

  1. The unions $H_k = \{k, k+3, k+4, k+7, k+8, k+11\}$, $k = 0,1,2,3$, are the four hexatonic collections. Each is a union of two augmented triads, $\{k, k+4, k+8\} \cup \{k+3, k+7, k+11\}$, and they partition $\mathcal{T}$ into four classes of six.
  2. The unions $O_k = \{k, k+1, k+3, k+4, k+6, k+7, k+9, k+10\}$, $k = 0,1,2$, are the three octatonic collections. Each is a union of two diminished seventh chords, and they partition $\mathcal{T}$ into three classes of eight.

Why the numbers. A hexatonic collection is determined by its augmented-triad halves, which are cosets of the subgroup $\{0,4,8\}$ of $\mathbb{Z}_{12}$. There are four such cosets, and adjacent pairs give four collections. An octatonic collection is a union of two cosets of $\{0,3,6,9\}$, of which there are three, so there are three collections. The group theory behind this is Chapter 19.

The hexatonic pole (computed). C major $\{0,4,7\}$ and A♭ minor $\{8,11,3\}$ share no pitch class, and the smallest voice leading between them is $C \to B$, $E \to E♭$, $G \to A♭$: three voices by one semitone each, a total of 3. That is the minimum for two triads with no common tone (computed over all 132 disjoint pairs of major and minor triads). The only other disjoint pairs that reach it are same-quality triads a semitone apart, such as C and C♯, where all three voices slide in parallel. The pole reaches the same distance with contrary motion and a change of quality.

Parsimonious voice leading

Here are the three smallest moves a major triad can make to another consonant triad, each there and back. In the first, E falls to E♭: C major becomes C minor. In the second, C falls to B: C major becomes E minor. In the third, G rises to A: C major becomes A minor. In each case two notes stay and one moves by step. These moves are the parsimonious moves, from the Latin for "thrifty".

Neo-Riemannian theory (Chapter 10) names the three moves:

MoveNameFrom C majorVoice that movesCommon tones
PparallelC → Cmthird, by a semitone (E → E♭)C, G
Lleading-tone exchangeC → Emroot, by a semitone (C → B)E, G
RrelativeC → Amfifth, by a whole tone (G → A)C, E

Each move is its own undo: doing P twice returns you to the start. Combining them gives every chromatic relation of the previous sections:

  • alternating P and L gives the hexatonic cycle (six chords);
  • alternating P and R gives the octatonic cycle (eight chords);
  • alternating L and R gives a chain of rising thirds, C, Em, G, Bm, D, …, which visits all 24 triads before it returns;
  • compound moves name the chromatic mediants: C → A♭ is P then L, and C → E is L then P.

Cohn's insight, building on earlier work by David Lewin and Brian Hyer, was that these moves describe triadic music that no single key explains. A passage can be perfectly smooth and perfectly logical in P, L and R while its keys wander through all twelve. "Smooth" here has the exact meaning given by voice-leading distance: P and L move 1 semitone, R moves 2.

Golden mirror. In 36-ET a "parsimonious" move can be a single sixth-step, a third of a semitone. The averaging principle (Chapter 25) is parsimony refined: each note of the golden triad Cg lies within two sixth-steps of the corresponding note of C major, C minor and C augmented (total motion 2, 3 and 3 sixth-steps). The arithmetic of 36 pitch classes (its subgroups, multipliers and symmetric sets) is the subject of Chapter 33.

Definition. Write a major triad as $(r, +)$ with pitch classes $\{r, r+4, r+7\}$ and a minor triad as $(r,-)$ with $\{r, r+3, r+7\}$. Then

$$P(r,\pm) = (r,\mp),\qquad L(r,+) = (r+4,-),\ L(r,-) = (r+8,+),\qquad R(r,+) = (r+9,-),\ R(r,-) = (r+3,+),$$

with roots mod 12. Each of $P, L, R$ is an involution ($X^2 = \text{identity}$), and each keeps two pitch classes and moves the third by 1 (P, L) or 2 (R) semitones.

Theorem (computed). The permutations $P$, $L$, $R$ of the 24 triads generate a group of order 24, with 13 elements of order 2 and 4 of order 12. These are the counts of the dihedral group of order 24, the symmetry group of a regular 12-gon, and the group is indeed dihedral. This is the well-known "neo-Riemannian group". It has the same size and shape as the group of transpositions and inversions of Chapter 20, but acts differently: $T_n$ and $I_n$ move chords as rigid shapes, while $P$, $L$, $R$ are defined by common tones, and the two actions commute.

Cycle lengths (computed). $(PL)^3 = (PR)^4 = (LR)^{12} = \text{identity}$, and no smaller power is, so the PL-cycle has 6 triads, the PR-cycle 8 and the LR-cycle all 24.

Exercise 1 (listening). In the octatonic cycle, which moves are a semitone and which a whole tone? Name the voice that moves at each step.

Answer

C→Cm: E→E♭ (semitone, P). Cm→E♭: C→B♭ (whole tone, R). E♭→E♭m: G→G♭ (semitone, P). E♭m→G♭: E♭→D♭ (whole tone, R). And so on: P moves are semitones, R moves whole tones, alternating.

Exercise 2. Which hexatonic system contains D major? List its six triads.

Answer

D = {2, 6, 9}. The collection is {1, 2, 5, 6, 9, 10} (C♯, D, F, F♯, A, B♭), with triads D, Dm, B♭, B♭m, F♯, F♯m.

Exercise 3. Show that C → A♭ equals P followed by L, and compute the voice-leading distance.

Answer

P(C) = Cm = {C, E♭, G}; L(Cm) = L(0,−) = (8,+) = A♭ = {A♭, C, E♭}. Directly, C–E–G → C–E♭–A♭ holds C, moves E down 1 and G up 1: distance 2.

Exercise 4. Find the hexatonic pole of E minor.

Answer

The triad in the same hexatonic system with no common tone: E minor = {4, 7, 11} lies in {0, 3, 4, 7, 8, 11}, and the major triad there with no common tone is A♭ = {8, 0, 3}. Voice leading E→E♭, G→A♭, B→C.

Exercise 5. Explain why the Tristan chord and the dominant seventh have the same interval vector.

Answer

The Tristan chord, respelled F–A♭–C♭–E♭, is a half-diminished seventh {0, 3, 6, 10}, which is the inversion of the dominant seventh {0, 4, 7, 10}: $I_{10}$ maps 0, 4, 7, 10 to 10, 6, 3, 0. Inversion preserves interval classes, so the vectors agree.
