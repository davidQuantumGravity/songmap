Chapter 23 built the golden scales from one interval, the golden sixth, exactly as Chapter 6 built the pentatonic, diatonic and chromatic scales from the fifth. This chapter takes the golden family apart scale by scale and mode by mode. It starts with the smallest golden scales (two, three and four notes), works through all seven modes of golden7, and then follows a proposal of the author's: split each large step of golden7 in two, and see what ten-note scale comes out. The answer is a golden decatonic with a remarkably clean structure. It is golden10 of Chapter 23, it is a "double pentatonic", and its two natural readings, 35 and 53, are modes of one scale. The chapter then shows that golden13, and not golden7, is maximally even in 36-ET, and ends with melodies drawn from these scales and the golden chords that harmonize them. Every count below was computed by enumeration; mode names are proposals, and so are all the progressions.

Two, three and four notes: the smallest golden scales

Before golden7 there are three smaller well-formed golden scales. With two notes the octave holds a single golden third and its complement, the golden sixth. With three it holds a golden triad, spread out as a scale. With four, a note a semitone above the golden fifth joins in, and the four notes together make a soft, slightly veiled "added-sixth" chord. Listen to each scale as a line and then as a chord, and at the end compare the four-note golden scale with its Western look-alike, C6 (C E G A).

A golden scale of $n$ notes is a window of $n$ consecutive notes of the golden chain (Chapter 23). The window is well formed (two step sizes) only for $n$ = 2, 3, 4, 7, 10, 13, 23 and 36 (Chapter 23). The three smallest, placed on C as SongMap places golden3 and golden4 (SongMap has no golden2), are:

Definition. golden$n$ on C is the set $\{25j \bmod 36 : j_0 \le j \le j_0 + n - 1\}$ in sixth-steps, a window of $n$ consecutive chain positions. Proposition (chords in a window). A window of $k$ chain notes contains exactly $k-2$ golden triads and $k-3$ golden sevenths (Chapter 23). So golden3 has 1 triad and no seventh, golden4 has 2 and 1. (Theorem.)

Exact steps. Chain position $j$ sits at $1200\{j\log_2\varphi\}$ cents. In 36-ET the same steps are 11, 11, 3, 11 sixth-steps, within 0.73 c of exact.

The seven modes of golden7

golden7, C C♯ vE vF ^G ^A♭ B, has seven modes, one on each of its notes, just as the white keys have seven (Chapter 6). Move each mode to C so that only the pattern changes, and play them from the brightest to the darkest. Each mode lowers one note of the one before, by 168 c, about five sixths of a whole tone. After each mode you hear the chord on its first, third and fifth degrees: in five modes it is the golden triad Cg, and in the two darkest it is something else.

A tune makes the difference clearer. The same tune, written only as scale degrees, in four of the modes:

golden7 on C is the chain window from $-3$ to $3$: three chain notes below the tonic (vE, ^G, B, at $-1, -2, -3$) and three above (^A♭, vF, C♯, at $1, 2, 3$). A mode is the same seven notes heard from another tonic. Moved to C, a mode is again a window of seven chain notes containing 0, with $b$ notes below the tonic and $a = 6 - b$ above.

Proposal: naming the modes. We name a golden mode by its window, writing $b|a$ for "$b$ chain notes below the tonic, $a$ above". golden7 itself is the 3|3 mode. The name works for every golden scale (the decatonic modes below are 9|0 … 0|9) and says at once which chords sit on the tonic. In the chain positions of GOLDEN_BLENDING.md §8.1, the mode on the note at chain position $j$ of golden7 is $(3+j)|(3-j)$. We do not borrow the Greek names: the brightness order does not line up with the Greek modes' chord properties, and "golden Ionian" is already used for a different scale (Chapter 38).

What the modes contain

As sets, all seven modes are the same seven notes, so they all contain the same chords: the five golden triads and four golden sevenths of golden7, and nothing else of the seven named triad types. There is no major, minor, augmented, diminished, silver or bronze triad in any mode, because golden7's widest chain distance is 6, while a Western triad spans at least 18 links of the chain (major and minor 21, augmented 24, diminished 18), and a silver or a bronze triad 22. What changes from mode to mode is which chord sits on the tonic:

  • The five brighter modes (6|0 to 2|4) have the golden triad Cg on C.
  • The four brightest (6|0 to 3|3) also have the golden seventh Cg7 = C vE ^G B.
  • The two darkest have no golden tonic. Their tonic triads, C vE vF♯ and C D vF♯, are the two "odd" degree triads of golden7 moved to C.

Those odd triads are worth a closer look. Stack scale thirds (degrees 1-3-5) on each note of golden7 on C and you get five golden triads, Cg, C♯g, vEg, vFg and ^A♭g, and two others: ^G B C♯ (golden third + whole tone, shape 0 11 17) and B C♯ vF (whole tone + golden third, shape 0 6 17). Both have a defective fifth of 17 sixth-steps (exact 565.45 c) in place of the golden fifth (733.82 c). They are golden7's counterpart of the diatonic's one diminished triad, B D F, whose diminished fifth is the one odd size of the diatonic's generic fifth. golden7 has two of them because its generic fifth takes its odd size twice (Chapter 30).

Symmetry: golden7 is the golden Dorian

golden7 on C reads the same upside down. Its step pattern 3 8 3 8 3 8 3 is a palindrome, and turning every interval above C into the same interval below C maps the scale onto itself. Among the diatonic modes only Dorian has this property (T s T T T s T), because D is the center of the white-key chain F C G D A E B. golden7 on C is the centered window, so as a mode it plays the role of Dorian, not Ionian: the middle of the brightness order, symmetric, with its tonic in the middle of its chain. The other modes pair up under inversion: 6|0 with 0|6, 5|1 with 1|5, 4|2 with 2|4.

Proposition (modes along the chain). Let $W_b = \{25j : -b \le j \le 6-b\}$ be the mode $b|(6-b)$ on C. Passing from $W_b$ to $W_{b-1}$ removes chain position $-b$ and adds $7-b$. These are 7 links apart, and $25 \cdot 7 = 175 \equiv -5 \pmod{36}$: the new note is the old note lowered by 5 sixth-steps. In exact tuning the change is chain distance 7, $7\log_2\varphi - 5$ octaves $= -168.37$ c, the chroma of golden7 (its large step minus its small step, $267.64 - 99.27$). (Theorem; the brightness values were computed exhaustively.)

Proposition (tonic chords). The tonic golden triad of C is chain $\{0, -1, -2\}$ and the tonic golden seventh $\{0, -1, -2, -3\}$. So the mode $b|a$ contains Cg if and only if $b \ge 2$, and Cg7 if and only if $b \ge 3$. (Theorem.)

Proposition (inversion). The inversion $x \mapsto -x$ about C maps chain position $j$ to $-j$ (because $-25j = 25(-j)$), so it maps the mode $b|a$ to $a|b$. The only self-inverse mode is 3|3. More generally every chain window $[\ell, h]$ is inversion-symmetric, by the inversion that maps chain position $j$ to $\ell + h - j$, as every diatonic scale is symmetric about its own "D". (Theorem.)

Degree triads. The generic third (two scale steps) of golden7 is 11 sixth-steps six times and 6 once (B–C♯); the generic fifth is 22 five times and 17 twice. A degree triad is golden exactly when both of its thirds are 11, which fails at the degrees ^G and B. (Checked by enumeration.)

The golden decatonic: splitting the large step

golden7 walks with a limp: semitone, wide step, semitone, wide step. Its wide step, 267.64 c, is almost a small minor third (7/6 is 266.87 c), so a golden7 melody keeps leaping. The author proposed a remedy: put one more note inside each wide step. A wide step is eight sixth-steps, which can be split as three plus five, five plus three or four plus four, so there are three uniform ways to do it, and they give three ten-note scales, written here by their splits as 35, 53 and 44. Listen to golden7 and then to the three.

All three are smoother than golden7, with every step at most a 12-ET whole tone. The 35 and 53 scales move by semitones and by a step of about five sixths of a whole tone. The 44 scale moves by semitones and by steps of a third of a semitone wider than a semitone (133 c), and it puts the plain F♯, the exact middle of the octave, in its center.

35 and 53 are one scale

The author asked whether the modes of 53 contain 35. They do. Read the two step patterns around the circle, starting at their first 5:

35: 3 3 5 3 3 5 3 3 5 3 → 5 3 3 5 3 3 5 3 3 3
53: 3 5 3 3 5 3 3 5 3 3 → 5 3 3 5 3 3 5 3 3 3

The same cyclic pattern: 35 and 53 are two rotations, two modes, of a single scale. Concretely, 53 on C has exactly the notes of 35 on B. And that single scale is an old friend: the cyclic pattern of seven 3s and three 5s spaced as evenly as possible is golden10, the ten-note well-formed golden scale of Chapter 23. In chain terms, 35 on C is the chain window from $-3$ to $6$ and 53 on C the window from $-6$ to $3$; SongMap's "C golden10" is the window from $-4$ to $5$, a third mode of the same scale. So the author's "35 series" and "53 series" are one mode family, and one set of chords serves both.

The construction explains why. A 35 split inserts a note a golden apotome (3 sixth-steps, exact 99.27 c) above the lower end of a large step: C♯ → D, vF → vF♯, ^A♭ → ^A. Each new note is a golden sixth up the chain from the last note of golden7 (chain 4, 5, 6). A 53 split inserts a note a golden apotome below the upper end: vE → vE♭, ^G → ^F♯, B → B♭, which extends the chain downward (chain $-4$, $-5$, $-6$). Either way the result is ten consecutive chain notes.

Mixed splits

Nothing forces the three large steps to be split the same way. Choosing 35, 53 or 44 for each of the three independently gives $3^3 = 27$ scales, all containing golden7. Computed over all 27:

  • They fall into 18 classes up to rotation.
  • Exactly 4 are well formed (two step sizes and Myhill's property), and all four are modes of golden10. Going up from C♯, they are 35·35·35, 53·35·35, 53·53·35 and 53·53·53: every 53 comes before every 35. These are the chain windows $[-3, 6]$, $[-4, 5]$, $[-5, 4]$ and $[-6, 3]$, the four modes of golden10 that contain golden7 on C.
  • The other four splits that use only 3s and 5s (35·53·35, 53·35·53, 35·35·53, 35·53·53) are irregular; they stay inside golden13 but contain only 5 or 6 golden triads, against golden10's 8.
  • Every split that uses a 44 leaves golden13. None of these 19 is well formed. Seven of them contain a Western major or minor triad (F♯, Bm, ^D or vE♭m): a 44 note can complete a Western triad with notes added by the other splits.
  • None of the 27 is maximally even in 36-ET (see below). The 3-and-5 splits have step sizes 2 apart, and in every split the two semitones B–C–C♯ stay adjacent, while the evenest ten-note set of 36-ET (steps 3 4 3 4 4 3 4 3 4 4) never puts two 3s together.

44, its own beast

The 44 split cuts every large step exactly in half: steps of 3 and 4 sixth-steps (100 and 133.33 c). The scale is perfectly symmetric about C (its steps read the same backward), and its new notes ^D, F♯ and vB♭ form a golden triad of their own, ^Dg. So 44 is golden7 plus one more golden triad: 6 golden triads and 4 golden sevenths. But that triad comes from the far side of the golden circle, 17 of its 36 steps away from Cg, and its notes are chain positions $-17$, $-18$ and $17$, the farthest the 36-ET chain can reach from C. They lie in neither golden13 nor golden23. In exact golden tuning the halfway points of golden7's large steps are 233.09 c, 600.00 c and 966.91 c: the middle one is the 12-ET tritone itself, and none of the three is a golden interval at all (Derive). The 44 scale has two step sizes but is not generated by any single interval, it is not well formed, and its ten modes are not ordered by single changes (several pairs tie in brightness).

Why the 35 series. The author's choice is to make the 35/53 family the primary golden decatonic, the scale for extending golden chords and harmony, and to treat 44 as a separate 36-ET colour. The facts behind the choice: 35/53 is a chain of golden sixths (golden10), well formed and Myhill, a subset of golden13, exactly tunable in golden intervals (steps 99.27 and 168.37 c), with 8 golden triads, 7 golden sevenths and 6 golden ninths; its modes are totally ordered by brightness. 44 is none of these: it is a 36-ET object made by halving, as silver13 is a 36-ET object made by mirroring (Chapter 23).

Theorem (35 = 53). The step sequences $s = (3,3,5,3,3,5,3,3,5,3)$ and $t = (3,5,3,3,5,3,3,5,3,3)$ satisfy $t_i = s_{i+1}$ (indices mod 10), so the scales are rotations of each other. As sets on C, $53 = 35 - 3$ sixth-steps, i.e. 53 on C equals 35 on B. Both are chain windows of length 10: $35 = \{25j: -3 \le j \le 6\}$ and $53 = \{25j : -6 \le j \le 3\}$. (Theorem, checked directly.)

Theorem (which splits are golden10). Number golden7's large steps 1, 2, 3 from the bottom (C♯–vE, vF–^G, ^A♭–B). A 35 split of step $i$ adds chain position $7 - i$ (D = 6, vF♯ = 5, ^A = 4); a 53 split adds chain position $-3 - i$ (vE♭ = $-4$, ^F♯ = $-5$, B♭ = $-6$). The ten notes are a chain window exactly when the added positions are contiguous with $[-3, 3]$, that is, when the positions above 3 are $4, \dots, 3 + c$ and those below $-3$ are $-4, \dots, -3 - (3 - c)$. This forces steps $1, \dots, 3-c$ to be split 53 and steps $4 - c, \dots, 3$ to be split 35. So there are exactly four golden10 splits, one for each $c = 0, \dots, 3$. (Theorem; the enumeration of all 27 agrees.)

Exact steps of golden10. The small step is the golden apotome $\varphi^3/4 = 99.27$ c and the large step is chain distance 7 inverted, $32/\varphi^7 = 168.37$ c, which is apotome + limma ($99.27 + 69.10$). Seven small and three large steps fill the octave: $7 \times 99.27 + 3 \times 168.37 = 1200$. In 36-ET they are 3 and 5 sixth-steps (100 and 166.67 c).

Proposition (the 44 notes are not golden). The golden intervals are the ratios $2^m\varphi^n$ (Chapter 23). The exact midpoint (in cents) of two golden pitches $x = 2^{m_1}\varphi^{n_1}$ and $y = 2^{m_2}\varphi^{n_2}$ is $\sqrt{xy}$, which is golden only if $m_1 + m_2$ and $n_1 + n_2$ are both even. For golden7's large steps the products are $\tfrac{\varphi^3}{4}\cdot\tfrac{2}{\varphi} = \varphi^2/2$, $\tfrac{\varphi^2}{2}\cdot\tfrac{4}{\varphi^2} = 2$ and $\varphi \cdot \tfrac{8}{\varphi^3} = 8/\varphi^2$: the power of 2 is odd each time, so the midpoints $\varphi/\sqrt2$, $\sqrt2$ and $2\sqrt2/\varphi$ (233.09, 600.00, 966.91 c) are not golden. (Theorem.) In 36-ET they become chain positions $-17$, $-18$ and $17$.

44 is not a chain. No generator $g$ and start $x_0$ give $\{x_0 + gj : 0 \le j \le 9\} = 44$ (checked for all 35 generators and 36 starts). Its generic seconds are 3 or 4, but its generic thirds take three sizes (6, 7, 8), so it fails Myhill's property.

Modes of the decatonic: 35, 53 and 44

The golden decatonic has ten modes. Here they are on C, from the brightest to the darkest, each one note lower than the one before. The fourth is 53 on C and the seventh is 35 on C.

The decatonic is also a double pentatonic. Take every other note of 35 and you get a five-note scale C D vF ^G ^A; the remaining notes C♯ vE vF♯ ^A♭ B are the same pentatonic a golden third higher. Each pentatonic is a tidy, singable scale of whole tones and small minor thirds, and the decatonic is the two of them interleaved like two hands on one keyboard.

Finally, the same tune in 35 and in 44, over the same golden chords. Only three degrees differ, each by a sixth-step; listen for the plain F♯ that opens the second bar in the 44 version, and for ^D and vB♭ in place of D and ^A.

What the decatonic contains

Every mode has the same notes up to transposition, so the chord content is the same for all ten (computed):

  • 8 golden triads and 7 golden sevenths (the window count $k-2$, $k-3$). In 35 on C the triads are, in chain order, vEg, Cg, ^A♭g, vFg, C♯g, ^Ag, vF♯g and Dg: golden7's five plus three new ones on the notes the split added.
  • 6 golden ninths: five consecutive chain notes stacked as four golden thirds, for example C♯ vF ^A♭ C vE (root C♯). The golden ninth's top interval above the root is 16/φ⁴ = 1467.64 c, an octave plus golden7's large step. These are the "extended chords" the decatonic makes possible.
  • No Western, silver or bronze triad. The decatonic's widest chain distance is 9. It has whole tones (chain 6, 198.54 c, four of them) and exactly one minor third (chain 9, 297.81 c), but no major third (chain 12) and no perfect fourth or fifth (chain 15). So no mode contains any Western triad, let alone a Western and a golden triad together.

One structural surprise: in the decatonic a golden third spans three scale steps, not two. Its generic "fourth" (three steps) is 11 sixth-steps nine times out of ten, and its generic "third" (two steps) is a whole tone or a small minor third. So the golden triads of the decatonic are built by skipping two notes, degrees 1-4-7, as quartal chords are built in the diatonic. In each mode eight of these degree chords are golden. This is the step-count shadow of the Stern–Brocot fractions: the golden sixth spans 5 of golden7's 7 steps, 7 of golden10's 10 and 9 of golden13's 13.

The double pentatonic

Split the decatonic into alternate notes. In 35 on C the even-numbered notes are C D vF ^G ^A and the odd-numbered ones are C♯ vE vF♯ ^A♭ B. They are transpositions of one scale, the second a golden third above the first.

The 44 modes, briefly

The 44 scale also has ten distinct modes (its step pattern 3 4 4 3 4 4 3 4 4 3 is not periodic). Computed: the tonic golden triad is present in 6 of them and the tonic golden seventh in 4; their brightness values (168 down to 156) include three ties, so the modes do not form a single chain of one-note changes. They are colourful, but they do not behave like a well-formed family. The tune above shows the practical difference: 44 replaces D, vF♯ and ^A by ^D, F♯ and vB♭. Theorem (double pentatonic). In golden10 every step changes the chain position by an odd number: a small step (3 sixth-steps) is $+3$ and a large step (5) is $-7$, since $25 \cdot 3 = 75 \equiv 3$ and $25 \cdot (-7) = -175 \equiv 5 \pmod{36}$. Hence two steps change the chain position by an even number, and alternate notes of the scale are exactly the notes of even and of odd chain position. For the window $[-3, 6]$ these are $\{-2, 0, 2, 4, 6\}$ and $\{-3, -1, 1, 3, 5\}$: two chains of five notes linked by golden fifths (chain step 2), the second one chain step below the first, i.e. a golden third higher. (Theorem; uniqueness among the 252 five-note subsets checked by enumeration.)

The golden pentatonic is well formed. The generator is two golden sixths, $2\log_2\varphi - 1 = 0.38848$ octaves (466.18 c). Its chain has two step sizes at $n$ = 2, 3, 5, 8, 13 (and three sizes at 4, 6, 7, 9–12), computed from exact φ. At $n = 5$ the steps are $8/\varphi^4 = 267.64$ c and $\varphi^6/16 = 198.54$ c.

golden13 and maximal evenness

A scale can be well formed and still lumpy. golden7 has steps of 100 and 267 c, so it alternates tight and wide. golden13 has steps of 100 and 67 c, as close to equal as thirteen notes in 36 steps can be. Listen to golden7, then golden13 and its brightest and darkest modes, and finally the evenest possible seven-note scale of 36-ET, which turns out to be silver rather than golden.

A scale of $n$ notes in an EDO is maximally even (Clough and Douthett, 1991) if every generic interval comes in one size or in two sizes one step apart. In 12-ET the diatonic scale is maximally even: its seconds are 1 or 2 semitones, its thirds 3 or 4, and so on. In 36-ET the picture changes:

The rule is simple: a well-formed scale is maximally even exactly when its chroma is one step of the tuning (Derive). golden13's chroma is the golden comma, which 36-ET renders as one sixth-step; golden23's is 38.92 c, also one sixth-step. golden10's chroma is the limma (2 sixth-steps) and golden7's is 168.37 c (5). So in 36-ET, golden13 is the golden counterpart of the diatonic in a sense golden7 is not: in its own tuning it is both well formed and maximally even, as the diatonic is in 12-ET. The evenest 13-note set of 36-ET, $\lfloor 36k/13 \rfloor$ = C vC♯ vD vE♭ vE ^E ^F ^F♯ ^G A♭ A B♭ B, is golden13, transposed to B♭ (as a mode on C it is the D mode, golden13's darkest).

All 13 contain 11 golden triads and 10 golden sevenths, and no Western triad.

Seven notes, maximally even. 36-ET does have a maximally even 7-note scale: steps of 5 and 6 sixth-steps, for example C vD ^E♭ F vG ^A♭ B♭ (unique up to transposition; computed over all 1,623,160 seven-note scales containing C). It contains three silver triads and no other named triad. A transposition of it, the same scale on ^B♭ (C vD ^E♭ F vG ^A♭ ^B♭ as a set), is the degree-by-degree average (Ionian + Aeolian + Locrian)/3 of three diatonic modes on C, a story told in Chapter 38. The golden seven-note scale gives up evenness to gain five golden triads.

Definition (Clough–Douthett). An $n$-note set $S$ in $N$-ET is maximally even if for every $k = 1, \dots, n-1$ the spans of $k$ consecutive steps take one value or two consecutive values.

Theorem (chroma criterion). A well-formed scale with steps $L > s$ is maximally even if and only if $L - s = 1$ step of the EDO.

Proof. In a well-formed scale the step word is balanced: any two spans of $k$ consecutive steps contain numbers of large steps that differ by at most one (this is equivalent to Myhill's property, Chapter 6). So every generic interval takes the sizes $m L + (k - m)s$ and $(m+1)L + (k-m-1)s$, which differ by exactly $L - s$ (or a single size). They are consecutive if and only if $L - s = 1$. ∎ Checked by enumeration for every golden window (2, 3, 4, 7, 10, 13, 23) and for C major in 36-ET: the two sizes of each generic interval differ by exactly the chroma.

Corollary. In 36-ET, golden13 and golden23 are maximally even; golden2, 3, 4, 7, 10 and the diatonic are not. In 12-ET the diatonic is (chroma 1 semitone). (Theorem.)

Evenest 13 = golden13. $\{\lfloor 36k/13 \rfloor : 0 \le k \le 12\} = \{0, 2, 5, 8, 11, 13, 16, 19, 22, 24, 27, 30, 33\}$, which is golden13 transposed by 30 sixth-steps (to B♭). (Checked.)

Golden melodies and how to harmonize them

Here is the idea of this chapter put to work: a melody drawn from the decatonic, harmonized only with golden triads that the decatonic contains, ending with a release into C major. The chords walk down the golden circle one step at a time, so that between any two chords only one voice moves, by a semitone. The melody moves freely through both interleaved pentatonics.

A second tune uses the brighter 53 mode. Where the first tune's chords lie on one side of Cg on the circle, this one travels out on the other side, to Bg and vE♭g, and comes home by the parallel move Bg to Cg, every voice rising a semitone, before the release.

What roughness can and cannot say here. All golden triads are transpositions of one shape, so on harmonic tones their roughness differs only through register (measurement, in the harmonic-partial model of explore.py: in close position on roots within half an octave of middle C, from ^A♭3 up to vF♯4, the eight triads of the scale measure 1.72 down to 1.26, purely by height). Roughness therefore cannot rank one golden progression above another; voice leading can. Where roughness does speak is at the end: the move Cg → C (two voices bend by a sixth-step; both chords in close position on C4) drops the measured roughness from 1.498 to 1.319, the release into the global minimum of harmonic tension. In the author's model the golden triad is a delicate local minimum one can dwell in; on harmonic instruments the measurement does not show that minimum, and arriving on a Western chord is always release (Chapter 26).

Western chords and the decatonic

The decatonic contains no Western triad, and only two major or minor triads come close: G (G B D) and Bm (B D F♯) each have two notes, B and D, in 35 on C. Western harmony therefore enters a decatonic piece from outside, most smoothly through the golden projection (Chapter 35): each golden triad Gr is two sixth-steps from the major triad on the same root and from the minor triad a sixth-step higher. Cg → C, Dg → D and ^Ag → ^A are such releases. The proposal for writing golden melodies, then:

  1. Draw the melody from one decatonic mode (35 or 53 on C), using its two pentatonics for singable, almost Western phrases and the crossings between them for golden colour.
  2. Harmonize strong beats with a golden triad that contains the melody note (table above); prefer chords one chain step apart.
  3. Use the three-step parallel moves (C♯g → Cg, Bg → Cg) as cadential approaches; whether they function as cadences is an open question (Chapter 31).
  4. End, when a release is wanted, on the Western projection of the last golden chord.

Exercise 36.2. Show that 53 on C has the same notes as 35 on B.

Answer

35 on C is 0 3 6 11 14 17 22 25 28 33. Subtract 3 sixth-steps: 33 0 3 8 11 14 19 22 25 30, i.e. C C♯ vE♭ vE vF ^F♯ ^G ^A♭ B♭ B, which is 53 on C.

Exercise 36.3. Compute golden10's large step exactly and check that seven small and three large steps make an octave.

Answer

The large step is chain distance 7 inverted: $1200(5 - 7\log_2\varphi) = 168.37$ c. The small step is $1200(3\log_2\varphi - 2) = 99.27$ c. Then $7 \times 99.27 + 3 \times 168.37 = 694.89 + 505.11 = 1200$ (exactly: $7(3g - 2) + 3(5 - 7g) = 1$ octave, where $g = \log_2\varphi$).

Exercise 36.4. Why is golden10 not maximally even in 36-ET, although its steps (3 and 5) look nearly equal?

Answer

Its chroma is 5 − 3 = 2 sixth-steps, so each generic interval comes in two sizes 2 apart (for example its generic thirds are 6 or 8). Maximal evenness needs sizes one step apart, which by the chroma criterion needs $L - s = 1$, as in golden13 (3 and 2).

Exercise 36.5. A melody in 35 on C sustains vF on a downbeat. Name the three golden triads of the scale that contain it. Is any of them a single chain step from Cg? Which is nearest?

Answer

vF is chain 2, so the triads are rooted at chain 2, 3, 4: vFg (root), C♯g (third), ^Ag (fifth). None is one chain step from Cg (chain 0); vFg is two steps away (sharing C), C♯g three and ^Ag four. The nearest choice is vFg, voice-leading distance 6.

Exercise 36.6 (listening). In the 35-versus-44 example, on which beats of the tune do you hear the difference, and which of the two versions sounds closer to 12-ET?

Answer

Wherever the tune uses degrees 3, 6 or 9: the second note (D or ^D), the first note of bar 2 (vF♯ or F♯), the fourth note of bar 2 and the first of bar 4 (D or ^D again) and the high note of bar 3 (^A or vB♭). Only 44's F♯ is a plain 12-ET pitch; its ^D and vB♭ are a sixth-step off 12-ET, as 35's vF♯ and ^A are. The audible difference is harmonic: 35's D, vF♯ and ^A belong to golden triads of the scale (Dg, vF♯g, ^Ag), while 44's new notes belong only to ^Dg, a golden triad seventeen circle steps from Cg that the progression never touches.
