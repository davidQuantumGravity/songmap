Standing waves on a string

Pluck a guitar string and it does not simply swing as one arc. It swings as one arc, and at the same time in two halves, in three thirds, in four quarters, and so on, all at once. Each of these ways of vibrating has its own rate, and the string sounds all of them together. Where you pluck decides how much of each you get. Listen to the same string plucked in the middle and then near its end.

The pitch is the same both times; what changes is the color. A pluck at the middle cannot set going any pattern of vibration that keeps the middle still, so half of the string's possible vibrations are missing and the tone sounds round and hollow. Plucked near the end, nearly all of them are present, and the tone is bright. This chapter is about those vibrations, called partials, and the remarkable fact that on a string, and in most instruments with a clear pitch, their rates are whole-number multiples of one rate.

A string fixed at both ends can only vibrate in patterns that keep both ends still. The simplest has one arc; the next has two arcs moving in opposite directions, with a still point, a node, in the middle; the next has three arcs and two nodes; and so on. These patterns are the string's modes, or standing waves: waves running back and forth along the string, reflected at the ends, add up to a pattern that stays in place and just swells and shrinks.

Mode $n$ fits $n$ half-waves into the string's length $L$, so its wavelength is $2L/n$. Waves travel along the string at a fixed speed $v$ that depends on the string's tension and weight, so mode $n$ vibrates at

$$f_n = \frac{n\,v}{2L} = n\,f_1 .$$

The modes vibrate at exactly 1, 2, 3, 4, … times the lowest frequency $f_1$, the fundamental. For example, a guitar's open A string, 110 Hz on a vibrating length of about 0.65 m, carries waves at $v = 2Lf_1 \approx 143$ m/s, and its modes vibrate at 110, 220, 330, 440 Hz and up.

The same formula explains one of the oldest experiments in music theory. Stop a string at half its length and it sounds an octave higher; stop it at two thirds of its length and it sounds a fifth higher; at three quarters, a fourth. Shorter strings mean higher frequencies in exact inverse proportion. Together with tension and weight, these are known as Mersenne's laws, after Marin Mersenne, who published them in the 1630s:

  • frequency is inversely proportional to length;
  • it is proportional to the square root of the tension;
  • it is inversely proportional to the square root of the mass per unit length.
The experiment with string lengths and simple ratios (1/2 for the octave, 2/3 for the fifth, 3/4 for the fourth) is attributed to Pythagoras (6th century BCE) by later writers. The classic instrument for it is the monochord, a single string with a movable bridge. The physics of why it works came two thousand years later, with the wave equation for the string (d'Alembert, 1747) and Daniel Bernoulli's idea that a string's motion is a sum of its modes.

Air columns behave similarly. A pipe open at both ends (a flute, roughly) has modes at all whole-number multiples of its fundamental. A pipe closed at one end has modes only at the odd multiples, 1, 3, 5, …; the clarinet, a nearly cylindrical tube closed at the mouthpiece, has strong odd partials in its low register, which gives it its hollow sound. Drums, bells and plates, on the other hand, have modes whose frequencies are not whole-number multiples; that is why their pitch is often weak or ambiguous, and why many drums have hardly any.

The wave equation. Let $y(x, t)$ be the sideways displacement of a string of length $L$, tension $T$ and mass per unit length $\mu$. For small displacements, Newton's law applied to each small piece gives

$$\frac{\partial^2 y}{\partial t^2} = v^2\,\frac{\partial^2 y}{\partial x^2},\qquad v = \sqrt{T/\mu},$$

with boundary conditions $y(0, t) = y(L, t) = 0$.

Modes. Try a product $y = X(x)\,\Theta(t)$. Substituting and dividing by $X\Theta$ gives $\Theta''/\Theta = v^2 X''/X$. The left side depends only on $t$ and the right only on $x$, so both equal one constant; a positive or zero constant allows no nonzero $X$ vanishing at both ends, so call it $-\omega^2$. So $X'' = -(\omega/v)^2X$, whose solutions vanishing at $x = 0$ are $X = \sin(\omega x/v)$. Vanishing at $x = L$ forces $\omega L/v = n\pi$ for a whole number $n \ge 1$. Then $\Theta$ is a sine of angular frequency $\omega_n = n\pi v/L$, i.e. frequency

$$f_n = \frac{\omega_n}{2\pi} = \frac{n}{2L}\sqrt{\frac{T}{\mu}}.$$

This is Mersenne's laws in one line. The general motion is a sum of modes,

$$y(x,t) = \sum_{n\ge1} \sin\frac{n\pi x}{L}\,\big(a_n\cos 2\pi f_n t + b_n \sin 2\pi f_n t\big),$$

with coefficients set by the initial shape and velocity.

The pluck. Pull the string aside by $h$ at the point $x = pL$ ($0 < p < 1$) into a triangle and release it from rest. Expanding the triangle in the modes (a Fourier sine series, next sections) gives the mode amplitudes

$$a_n = \frac{2h\,\sin(n\pi p)}{n^2\pi^2\,p(1-p)}.$$

Two consequences:

  • Modes with a node at the plucking point, $np$ a whole number, are absent. Plucked at the middle ($p = 1/2$), every even mode vanishes.
  • The displacement amplitudes fall off like $1/n^2$ for large $n$. (The string's velocity, and the force it exerts on the bridge, which is what the instrument's body radiates, carry an extra factor $n$, so they fall off like $1/n$, with the same gaps.) For a pluck near the end, $p$ small, $\sin(n\pi p) \approx n\pi p$ for the low modes, so their displacement amplitudes fall only like $1/n$ up to about $n \approx 1/(2p)$: that is why such a pluck sounds brighter.

The audio example uses exactly this formula with $p = 1/2$ and $p = 1/10$, giving higher modes shorter decay times, as real strings do. Real strings are also slightly stiff, which pushes their high modes a little sharp of exact multiples (inharmonicity, important in pianos); the ideal string here has none.

Partials and the harmonic series

A tone with a clear pitch is a chord in disguise. Its components blend so well that we hear one note, but they can be pulled apart. The next example plays the first sixteen components of a low C one at a time, as pure tones, and then all sixteen together.

The first few leap by large intervals: an octave, then a fifth, then a fourth. Higher up the steps shrink, until near the top they are barely wider than a piano's semitone, and some notes sound "between the keys". When everything sounds together, the tower collapses back into a single pitch: the low C.

Vocabulary.

  • A partial is any one of the sine components of a sound.
  • A harmonic is a partial whose frequency is a whole-number multiple $n f_1$ of the fundamental; $n$ is its harmonic number. The fundamental is harmonic 1.
  • An overtone is any partial above the fundamental. The numbering is off by one: the first overtone is the second harmonic. This book counts harmonics.
  • The harmonic series on a fundamental $f_1$ is the sequence $f_1, 2f_1, 3f_1, \dots$

Harmonic and inharmonic. Strings and air columns are, in effect, one-dimensional vibrators whose modes lie at (nearly) whole-number multiples of the lowest, so strings and wind instruments produce (nearly) harmonic spectra. When a tone is sustained by bowing, blowing or singing (the voice is driven by the regular puffs of air from the vocal folds), the periodic drive locks the partials to exact multiples. Bells, gongs and drums produce inharmonic partials. A tone whose partials are exactly harmonic repeats exactly once per fundamental period, and that periodicity is a main cue the ear uses for pitch (Chapter 3).

Periodicity. Let $x(t) = \sum_n A_n \sin(2\pi n f_1 t + \theta_n)$. Every term repeats after $T = 1/f_1$, because $\sin(2\pi n f_1 (t + T) + \theta_n) = \sin(2\pi n f_1 t + \theta_n + 2\pi n)$. So $x$ has period $1/f_1$ whatever the amplitudes and phases; and if $A_1 = 0$ the period is still $1/f_1$ as long as the harmonic numbers present have greatest common divisor 1. (The period of a sum is set by the gcd of its harmonic numbers, not by its lowest partial; this is why the "missing fundamental" of Chapter 3 is still heard.)

Octave reduction. Harmonic $n$ lies $1200\log_2 n$ cents above the fundamental. Its position within the octave, its pitch class, is

$$1200\,\big(\log_2 n - \lfloor \log_2 n\rfloor\big)\ \text{cents},$$

the last column of the table. Writing $n = 2^k m$ with $m$ odd, harmonic $n$ has the same pitch class as harmonic $m$. So the new pitch classes of the series come from its odd harmonics: 1, 3, 5, 7, 9, 11, 13, 15, … Among the first sixteen harmonics there are exactly eight pitch classes, one for each odd number below 16.

Theorem. The harmonics $n$ and $m$ are distinct pitch classes whenever their odd parts differ.

Proof. Suppose $n = 2^a u$ and $m = 2^b w$ with $u \ne w$ odd. If they were octave-equivalent, $n/m = 2^{a-b}u/w$ would be a power of 2, so $u/w$ would be a power of 2. Two distinct odd numbers cannot have a power of 2 as their ratio. $\square$

Adding sines: Fourier's idea

If a tone is a sum of sines, we should be able to build one by adding sines. The next example does exactly that. It starts with a single pure tone on C3, then adds the second harmonic at half the strength, the third at a third, and so on, about one per second, until there are ten. At the end you hear the sum of forty.

The pitch is C3 from the first second to the last. Each new harmonic adds brightness, and by the end the tone has the buzzy edge of a bowed string or a brass instrument. Now compare three classic waveforms, all built from sines and all on the same C.

Joseph Fourier, in his work on heat (published in 1822), claimed that any repeating pattern can be written as a sum of sines whose frequencies are whole-number multiples of the repetition rate. With suitable conditions on the pattern this is a theorem, and it is the reason the harmonic series is so central: any periodic sound is a sum of harmonics of its repetition rate. The list of their amplitudes is the sound's spectrum.

The figure shows the sawtooth emerging as sines are added. One sine is a smooth hump; three sines already lean like a ramp; twelve make a sharp-edged ramp with a little ripple. The ripple next to each jump never quite goes away (the Gibbs phenomenon), but it gets narrower, and the ear does not notice it.

The three waveforms in the audio example have these spectra:

The square and triangle both contain only odd harmonics, like a closed pipe; the triangle's fall off much faster ($1/k^2$), so it is darker. The sawtooth has every harmonic, falling off as $1/k$, and is the brightest.

Phase. Each sine in the sum also has a starting point in its cycle, its phase. Change the phases and the waveform can look completely different, while the spectrum, the list of amplitudes, stays the same. Remarkably, for steady tones the ear mostly ignores the phases. The idea that the ear analyzes a tone into its sine components goes back to Georg Ohm (1843); Hermann von Helmholtz developed it and argued that the phases make little difference. That is approximately true, not exactly, since low tones with many strong harmonics can sound a little different in buzz when the phases change.

Fourier series. Let $g(x)$ be a function of period $2\pi$ that is piecewise smooth. Then

$$g(x) = \frac{a_0}{2} + \sum_{k\ge1}\big(a_k\cos kx + b_k\sin kx\big),$$

where

$$a_k = \frac1\pi\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}g(x)\cos kx\,dx,\qquad b_k = \frac1\pi\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}g(x)\sin kx\,dx,$$

with the series converging to $g(x)$ wherever $g$ is continuous, and to the midpoint of the jump where it is not (Dirichlet's theorem). The coefficient formulas follow from orthogonality: for whole numbers $j, k \ge 1$, $\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\sin jx\,\sin kx\,dx = \pi$ if $j = k$ and $0$ otherwise, and similarly for cosines, while every sine is orthogonal to every cosine. Multiply the series by $\sin kx$, integrate, and only $b_k$ survives. For a sound with fundamental $f_1$, put $x = 2\pi f_1 t$. See Appendix A.

The sawtooth. Take $g(x) = x$ on $(-\pi, \pi)$, repeated. It is odd, so all $a_k = 0$, and integrating by parts,

$$b_k = \frac1\pi\int_{-\pi}^{\pi} x\sin kx\,dx = \frac{2(-1)^{k+1}}{k},$$

so that, on $(-\pi, \pi)$,

$$x = 2\Big(\sin x - \frac{\sin 2x}{2} + \frac{\sin 3x}{3} - \cdots\Big).$$

The amplitudes are $2/k$: the $1/k$ law of the audio example. (The alternating signs are phases, which, as above, barely matter to the ear.)

The square and triangle. The square wave $\operatorname{sign}(x)$ has $b_k = 4/(\pi k)$ for odd $k$ and $0$ for even $k$. The triangle wave of height $\pm1$ is

$$\frac{8}{\pi^2}\Big(\sin x - \frac{\sin 3x}{9} + \frac{\sin 5x}{25} - \cdots\Big).$$

Energy (Parseval's theorem). The average power of $g$ equals the sum of the powers of its harmonics: $\frac1{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi} g^2\,dx = \frac{a_0^2}{4} + \frac12\sum_k (a_k^2 + b_k^2)$. For the sawtooth the harmonic powers are proportional to $1/k^2$, and since $\sum_k 1/k^2 = \pi^2/6$ the fundamental carries $6/\pi^2 \approx 60.79\%$ of the power. The first ten harmonics carry 94.21%.

The Gibbs phenomenon. Near a jump, the partial sums overshoot by an amount that does not shrink as more terms are added: about 8.95% of the size of the jump (exactly $\frac1\pi\int_0^\pi \frac{\sin u}{u}du - \frac12$). The overshoot gets narrower, so its energy tends to zero.

Timbre as a spectrum

Two instruments playing the same note at the same loudness still sound different. That difference is timbre, or tone color. Here is one pitch with five different spectra.

The first four are all harmonic and all have the clear pitch C3; they differ in brightness and hollowness. The last one is built from partials in golden-ratio proportions rather than whole-number ones. It still suggests a pitch, but a less certain one, with a metallic shimmer. Which spectrum a tone has turns out to strongly affect which chords sound smooth on it, the subject of Chapter 3.

For steady tones, timbre is mostly the spectrum: which partials are present and how strong they are. Two simple descriptions capture much of it.

  • Brightness follows the balance between high and low partials. A common measure is the spectral centroid, the amplitude-weighted average of the partial frequencies. For sixteen harmonics on C3 it is about 4.73 times the fundamental (619 Hz) with $1/k$ amplitudes, but only 2.13 times (279 Hz) with $1/k^2$ amplitudes.
  • Hollowness comes from missing even harmonics, as in the clarinet's low register and the square wave.

Real instruments add more:

  • the envelope, how the sound starts, holds and stops (a piano's sharp attack and slow decay, an organ's steady hold);
  • how the spectrum changes during a note (the high partials of a plucked string die first);
  • formants, fixed bands of emphasis set by the instrument's body or the vocal tract, which stay put while the pitch moves (the vowels "ee" and "ah" are different formant patterns);
  • noise, such as breath, bow scrape and hammer thump.

The tones in this book's examples are deliberately simple: exact partials with smooth envelopes and no vibrato or noise, so that what you hear in an interval comes from tuning alone.

The golden spectrum in the last example has partials at $2^m\varphi^n$ times the fundamental: 1, 1.618, 2, 2.618, 3.236, 4, 4.236, … ($\varphi = 1.618\ldots$). On harmonic tones the golden triad measures rougher than the major and minor triads played on the same tones; on golden-spectrum tones the order reverses, and the golden triad measures smoother than major, minor and the other triads tested on those tones. This timbre flip (a measurement, from a model of roughness) is presented in Chapter 26.

Spectral centroid. For partials at frequencies $f_k$ with amplitudes $A_k$, $c = \sum_k A_k f_k / \sum_k A_k$ (power-weighted versions use $A_k^2$). For harmonics $f_k = k f_1$, $k = 1, \dots, K$:

  • $A_k = 1/k$ gives $c = K f_1 / H_K$, where $H_K = 1 + \frac12 + \cdots + \frac1K$; for $K = 16$, $H_{16} \approx 3.3807$ and $c \approx 4.73 f_1$;
  • $A_k = 1/k^2$ gives $c = f_1 H_K / \sum_{k\le K} k^{-2} \approx 2.13 f_1$ for $K = 16$;
  • odd harmonics $k = 1, 3, \dots, 15$ with $A_k = 1/k$ give $c = 8 f_1 / (1 + \frac13 + \cdots + \frac1{15}) \approx 3.96 f_1$.

Harmonic and golden spectra as sets. The harmonic spectrum is the set $\{n : n \ge 1\}$ of whole numbers. Multiplying it by a whole number $k$ maps it into itself, so every partial of a tone $k$ times higher lands on a partial of the lower tone. More generally, for two such tones a ratio $m/n$ apart (in lowest terms), partial $jm$ of the lower tone coincides with partial $jn$ of the upper one, so every $m$-th partial of the lower tone is shared (Chapter 3). The golden spectrum $\{2^m\varphi^n : m, n \ge 0\}$ has the same kind of property for the factors $2$ and $\varphi$: multiplying it by $\varphi$ (or by 2) maps it into itself, so every partial of a tone a factor $\varphi$ higher lands on a partial of the lower tone. That structural parallel is why one expects golden intervals to be smooth on golden tones, and the roughness measurements of Chapter 26 bear this out.

Intervals hidden in the series

The harmonic series contains, between its neighbouring members, the intervals Western music is built on. The next example plays neighbouring harmonics of C2 as two-note chords, 1 and 2, then 2 and 3, and so on, and ends with two chords taken straight from the series.

The first intervals are the smoothest: octave, fifth, fourth. Then come the two thirds that make major and minor chords. The pairs 6:7 and 7:8, a narrow minor third and a wide whole tone, sit between the piano keys. The two final chords are the major triad and a seventh chord exactly as nature tunes them, each a slice of a single harmonic series.

The interval between harmonics $n$ and $m$ is the ratio $m/n$. Neighbouring harmonics give the superparticular ratios $(n+1)/n$, which shrink as $n$ grows:

  • Harmonics 4:5:6 form the major triad C–E–G (harmonics 4, 5, 6 of C2 are C4, E4, G4).
  • 4:5:6:7 adds a seventh 31.17 c lower than the piano's B♭: the harmonic seventh chord.
  • The minor triad needs bigger numbers. Tuned purely, C–E♭–G is 10:12:15, harmonics 10, 12 and 15 of an A♭ three octaves and a major third below.

The series thus offers a natural account of why the major triad sounds settled and the minor triad a little less so. How far that account goes, and what it leaves out, is the subject of Chapter 3.

The Fibonacci ratios $2/1, 3/2, 5/3, 8/5, 13/8, 21/13, \dots$ are all intervals in the harmonic series, between harmonics that are consecutive Fibonacci numbers. They approach the golden ratio $\varphi = 833.09$ c from alternate sides: 8/5 is 813.69 c, 13/8 is 840.53 c, 21/13 is 830.25 c, 34/21 is 834.17 c. The harmonic series climbs toward $\varphi$ but never reaches it, because $\varphi$ is irrational. The golden system starts where the series stops (Chapter 23).

Theorem (every ratio is in the series). Every ratio $m/n > 1$ of whole numbers in lowest terms is the interval between harmonics $n$ and $m$ of any fundamental, and these are the lowest harmonics with that interval.

Proof. Harmonics $n$ and $m$ have frequencies $nf_1$ and $mf_1$, ratio $m/n$. If harmonics $a < b$ also have ratio $m/n$, then $bn = am$; since $\gcd(m, n) = 1$, $n$ divides $a$, so $a \ge n$. $\square$

Theorem (superparticular ratios shrink and cover everything). $c\big((n+1)/n\big)$ decreases strictly to 0, and every interval of the series is a sum of superparticular steps: $\frac{m}{n} = \frac{n+1}{n}\cdot\frac{n+2}{n+1}\cdots\frac{m}{m-1}$.

The product telescopes. For example $5/4 = 9/8 \cdot 10/9$, so a pure major third is a major whole tone plus a minor whole tone, $203.91 + 182.40 = 386.31$ c. The two whole tones differ by $81/80$, the syntonic comma (21.51 c), which reappears in Chapter 4.

Fibonacci ratios. With $F_1 = F_2 = 1$ and $F_{k+1} = F_k + F_{k-1}$, the ratio $F_{k+1}/F_k$ is the interval between harmonics $F_k$ and $F_{k+1}$. By Binet's formula $F_k = (\varphi^k - (-1/\varphi)^k)/\sqrt5$, so $F_{k+1}/F_k \to \varphi$, with errors alternating in sign and shrinking by a factor of about $\varphi^2$ each step. No harmonic interval equals $\varphi$, since $\varphi = (1 + \sqrt5)/2$ is irrational.

1. A string 0.65 m long sounds 110 Hz. Where must it be stopped to sound 220 Hz? 165 Hz? What frequency does its fifth harmonic have?

Answer

Frequency is inversely proportional to length: 220 Hz needs half the length, 0.325 m; 165 Hz $= \frac32 \cdot 110$ needs two thirds, about 0.433 m. The fifth harmonic is $5 \cdot 110 = 550$ Hz.

2. A string is plucked one third of the way along. Which harmonics are missing?

Answer

Those with a node at $x = L/3$, i.e. with $n/3$ a whole number: harmonics 3, 6, 9, … ($\sin(n\pi/3) = 0$ exactly for multiples of 3.)

3. Which harmonics of C2 up to 32 are B♭'s, and how far are they from the 12-ET B♭?

Answer

Harmonics with odd part 7: 7, 14, 28. All are 31.17 c flat of 12-ET, since octave transposition does not change the deviation. One more harmonic has B♭ as its nearest note: harmonic 29, which lies 29.58 c sharp of B♭ (1029.58 c above C). It is a different pitch class from harmonic 7, 60.75 c higher, since its odd part is 29.

4. Write the pure major sixth 5/3 and the pure minor sixth 8/5 each as a perfect fourth or fifth plus a pure third, and check the sizes in cents. What does a fifth plus a minor third give?

Answer

$4/3 \cdot 5/4 = 5/3$: a fourth plus a major third is the major sixth, $498.04 + 386.31 = 884.36$ c. And $8/5 = 4/3 \cdot 6/5$: a fourth plus a minor third is the minor sixth, $498.04 + 315.64 = 813.69$ c. A fifth plus a minor third is $3/2 \cdot 6/5 = 9/5$, a minor seventh (1017.60 c).

5. (Listening.) In the sawtooth-building example, does the pitch ever change? At which stage does the tone stop sounding "pure"?

Answer

The pitch stays C3 throughout, because every added partial is a harmonic of 130.81 Hz and the period never changes. Many listeners hear the purity go as soon as the second harmonic arrives, and the buzz grow over the first five or six.
