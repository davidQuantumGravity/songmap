Sixth-steps

Chapter 23 measured every golden interval in cents and kept noting a second number: how many "36-ET steps" it spans. This chapter is about that grid. Divide each semitone into three equal parts and you get 36 notes per octave. The smallest step, a third of a semitone, is small but clearly audible. In a melody it sounds like a deliberate bend; in a chord, like the same chord seen in different light.

36-ET (36-tone equal temperament) divides the octave into 36 equal steps. Each step is

$$\frac{1200}{36} = 33.33\ \text{cents}, \qquad\text{frequency ratio } 2^{1/36} \approx 1.019441.$$

Three steps make a semitone and six make a whole tone, so the step is a sixth of a whole tone: a sixth-tone. This book calls it a sixth-step. In sixth-steps, the golden intervals of Chapter 23 are whole numbers:

History. Ferruccio Busoni, in his Sketch of a New Esthetic of Music (1907), proposed dividing the whole tone into thirds and combining two such series, which amounts to sixth-tones, i.e. 36-ET. Alois Hába composed in quarter-, third- and sixth-tones in the 1920s and 1930s (Chapter 22). The golden reasons for 36 given in this chapter are this book's.

Definition. The pitch classes of 36-ET are $\mathbb{Z}_{36}$, the integers mod 36. Step $k$ above a reference has frequency ratio $2^{k/36}$.

Sub-tunings. The subgroups of $\mathbb{Z}_{36}$ are generated by the divisors of 36, with orders 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 9, 12, 18 and 36. So 36-ET contains 12-ET exactly (the multiples of 3) and 18-ET (the multiples of 2, third-tones): Busoni's "two series of third-tones" can be read as the two cosets $2\mathbb{Z}_{36}$ and $1 + 2\mathbb{Z}_{36}$. It also contains 9-ET, 6-ET (whole tones), 4-ET (minor thirds) and 3-ET (major thirds).

Three layers of 12-ET

Because 36 is three times 12, 36-ET contains three complete copies of the ordinary 12-note keyboard: the usual one, a copy a third of a semitone higher, and a copy two thirds of a semitone higher. Western music can be played on any of the three copies, and inside each copy it sounds exactly as usual. Only the comparison between copies reveals the shift.

Every 36-ET pitch class $p$ has a layer, the remainder of $p$ divided by 3:

Each layer is a full 12-ET. A Western chord or key lies on one layer, and moving it to another layer is just transposition by one or two sixth-steps: ^C major is C major, a third of a semitone higher. Its notes form the same shape on the 36-dot circle, rotated by one dot.

Golden harmony uses the layers in a different way. The golden sixth is 25 sixth-steps, one more than a minor sixth (24), so each golden sixth moves to the next layer. Three golden sixths return to the starting layer, having gained two octaves and a semitone: $3 \times 25 = 75 = 72 + 3$. That is why the golden chain of Chapter 23 cycles through the layers, and why a golden triad has one note on each layer (the layer theorem). A Western chord is rational and lives on one layer; a golden chord is spread over all three.

Western mirror. Chapter 8 treats the twelve pitch classes as the group $\mathbb{Z}_{12}$ (pitch classes). Here $\mathbb{Z}_{12}$ reappears as one third of $\mathbb{Z}_{36}$, the layer-0 subgroup, and the other two layers are its copies.

Definition. The layer map is $\lambda: \mathbb{Z}_{36} \to \mathbb{Z}_3$, $\lambda(p) = p \bmod 3$. It is a group homomorphism (well defined because 3 divides 36), its kernel is $3\mathbb{Z}_{36} = \{0, 3, \ldots, 33\} \cong \mathbb{Z}_{12}$, and the three layers are the cosets $r + 3\mathbb{Z}_{36}$, $r = 0, 1, 2$.

A subtlety. $\mathbb{Z}_{36}$ is not the product $\mathbb{Z}_{12} \times \mathbb{Z}_3$ (that would need 12 and 3 to be coprime; in fact $\mathbb{Z}_{36} \cong \mathbb{Z}_4 \times \mathbb{Z}_9$). The layer is not an independent coordinate: three steps up through the layers carry over into one semitone. In the chain this is the identity $3 \cdot 25 \equiv 3 \pmod{36}$: three golden sixths are a semitone.

Layers of the chain. $\lambda(25j) = j \bmod 3$, because $25 \equiv 1 \pmod 3$. (Theorem.)

Ups and downs: the complete notation

A notation for 36 notes has to be readable at a glance. The one used here keeps every familiar note name and adds two small signs, an up arrow and a down arrow, for the notes in between. ^C is "C, a little up"; vE is "E, a little down". You have already heard these: the melody at the start of this chapter climbed C, ^C, vC♯, C♯. Every 36-ET note is a plain note, a plain note nudged up, or a plain note nudged down.

The signs. ^ raises a note by one sixth-step and v lowers it by one. They are written as a prefix: ^C, vE♭, ^F♯. They combine with sharps and flats and with each other (^^C is two sixth-steps above C). The notation follows Kite Giedraitis' "ups and downs" notation for equal divisions of the octave.

The standard spelling. Each step is written with at most one arrow. A step one above a 12-ET note takes ^ on that note; a step one below takes v on the note above. So step 2 is vC♯, not ^^C.

$$\text{steps} = 3\,\big(12(o+1) + \ell + a\big) + u - w$$

sixth-steps above the C five octaves below middle C (so middle C, C4, is step $3 \times 60 = 180$, i.e. MIDI note number times 3).

Standard spelling. Write a pitch class as $p = 3q + r$ with $r \in \{0, 1, 2\}$ and $q$ a 12-ET pitch class. The standard name is the 12-ET name of $q$ if $r = 0$; ^ plus the name of $q$ if $r = 1$; v plus the name of $q + 1$ if $r = 2$. Every pitch class has infinitely many spellings (add ^v pairs, or trade three ^ for a ♯); the standard one uses the fewest arrows, at most one.

Why 36

Two listening tests explain the choice. First, the golden sixth in exact tuning and in 36-ET are the same to the ear: they differ by a quarter of a cent. The 13-note equal tuning, the golden counterpart of 12-ET, misses by more than two cents, enough to beat slowly against the exact interval. Second, 36 golden sixths do not quite make 25 octaves. They fall short by 8.75 cents, the 36-comma, and 36-ET is the tuning that quietly closes that gap.

Three facts make 36 the natural home of golden harmony.

1. 25/36 is a convergent of $\log_2\varphi$. The golden sixth is $0.694242\ldots$ of an octave, and the continued fraction $[0; 1, 2, 3, 1, 2, 3, \ldots]$ has convergents 1/1, 2/3, 7/10, 9/13, 25/36, 84/121. A golden sixth of 25 steps out of 36 is 833.33 c, only 0.24 c wide, and the golden third, fifth and seventh are within 0.24, 0.49 and 0.73 c. Among all equal divisions of the octave, 36 is the smallest whose golden sixth is within 1 cent of exact:

2. $\varphi^3 \approx 4 \cdot 2^{1/12}$. Three golden sixths make two octaves and almost exactly one semitone: $\varphi^3/4 = 1.05902$ against $2^{1/12} = 1.05946$, a difference of 0.73 c. That is the golden apotome again, 99.27 c. So the golden chain rides on a 12-ET chromatic skeleton: every third link lands within a few cents of a 12-ET note, and the links in between land between them, one third and two thirds of the way. A tuning that adds two notes inside each semitone, a 36-note tuning, is exactly what that pattern asks for. In 36-ET the identity becomes exact: $3 \times 25 = 75 = 2 \times 36 + 3$.

3. The 36-comma. Thirty-six golden sixths fall just short of 25 octaves:

$$\frac{\varphi^{36}}{2^{25}} \approx 0.99496, \qquad 1200\log_2\frac{\varphi^{36}}{2^{25}} = -8.75\ \text{c}.$$

36-ET removes this comma, so its golden chain closes into a circle after exactly 36 links: the golden circle of keys and of chords (Chapter 31). And because 36 is a multiple of 12, 36-ET contains 12-ET exactly: every Western chord and progression is available unchanged.

No other small tuning does both. 13-ET removes the golden comma but contains no Western interval except the octave; 12-ET contains Western harmony but its golden sixth is 33 c off. 36-ET is to the golden chain roughly what 53-ET is to the chain of fifths (it removes the second comma: 53 fifths miss 31 octaves by 3.6 c), with the bonus of containing 12-ET. It does not improve Western intonation: its major third is still the 12-ET 400 c, 13.69 c above the just 5/4; the nearest other step, 366.67 c, is 19.65 c below it. In 36-ET, "Western" means 12-ET.

Western mirror. Chapter 7 explains why 12-ET won for fifths, and which other divisions (19, 31, 53) serve meantone and Pythagorean tuning better (Chapter 7). The golden table above is the same kind of comparison, made for $\log_2\varphi$ instead of $\log_2\tfrac32$.

Rendering errors. With golden sixth $g = 833.0903$ c and 36-ET step $s = 33.3333$ c, chain distance $d$ is rendered as $25d$ steps, too sharp by $d(25s - g) = 0.2430\,d$ c (before folding). The golden third is chain distance $-1$ plus an octave, so it is 0.24 c flat; the fifth ($-2$) 0.49 c flat; the seventh ($-3$) 0.73 c flat. The error after 36 links is $36 \times 0.2430 = 8.75$ c: the 36-comma $36g - 25 \times 1200 = -8.75$ c is exactly what 36-ET throws away.

Proposition. In 36-ET, (i) the golden chain $j \mapsto 25j$ is a permutation of $\mathbb{Z}_{36}$; (ii) $75 \equiv 3$: three golden sixths are one semitone; (iii) $\lambda(25j) = j \bmod 3$. (Theorem; each part is one line of modular arithmetic.)

Why 36 is the smallest EDO within a cent. Checking every $n$ from 1 to 35: the best golden sixth of an $n$-EDO, $\mathrm{round}(n\log_2\varphi)\cdot 1200/n$, is never within 1 c of 833.09 (the closest are 23, off by 1.69 c, and 13 and 26, off by 2.32 c). For $n = 36$ the error is 0.24 c. (Checked by computation.)

13-ET and 36-ET

There is a purer golden tuning than 36-ET, and it is worth hearing what it gives up. In 13-ET the octave has 13 equal steps and golden13 becomes perfectly even: no small and large steps, no seams. It is the exact golden counterpart of 12-ET. Listen to golden13 three ways: in exact golden ratios, in 36-ET, and in 13-ET.

In 12-ET the Pythagorean comma vanishes: twelve fifths make exactly seven octaves, and the limma and apotome become one semitone (Chapter 7). In 13-ET the golden comma vanishes: the golden sixth is 9 steps, thirteen of them make exactly nine octaves, and the golden apotome and limma become one step of 92.31 c. golden7 in 13-ET has steps of 1 and 3 (92.31 and 276.92 c).

13-ET has a serious cost. Because 13 and 12 share no factor, the only 12-ET interval in 13-ET is the octave. Its nearest fifths are 646.15 and 738.46 c (the latter 36.51 c above 3/2), its nearest major third is 369.23 c and its minor third 276.92 c. It is a purely golden world, with golden errors about ten times those of 36-ET. 36-ET keeps the comma as a real interval (one sixth-step), so golden13 keeps its two step sizes, 100 and 66.67 c, close to the exact 99.27 and 69.10; and it keeps Western harmony intact. That is why this book composes in 36-ET, and uses exact $\varphi$ when exact ratios are the point.

The key system: 72 keys and 72 golden scales

12-ET has 24 major and minor keys. 36-ET has three times as many, because every key exists on each of the three layers. Then come the golden scales. golden13 can start on any of the 36 notes, and so can silver13. Neighbouring golden keys are close relatives: golden13 on C and golden13 a golden third higher share twelve of their thirteen notes.

Western keys. 36 tonics (C, ^C, vC♯, C♯, …, rising by sixth-steps), each with a major and a minor key: 72 keys. Bach's Well-Tempered Clavier (Book I 1722, Book II 1742) went through the 24 keys of a well temperament (Chapter 7); 36-ET triples that set. Keys on the same layer relate exactly as in 12-ET: circle of fifths, relative and parallel keys (Chapter 8). Keys on different layers share no notes at all, so moving between layers needs golden pivot chords (Chapter 32).

Golden scales. golden13 has 36 distinct transpositions, and so does silver13: 72 golden scales. With the 72 Western keys that makes the 144 tonal fields of the Well-Tempered Golden Clavier (Chapter 31).

Near and far golden keys. Two golden13 scales share more notes the closer their tonics are on the golden chain. Moving the tonic one golden sixth (25 sixth-steps up, or equivalently a golden third, 11 steps, down) slides the 13-note chain window by one link, dropping one note and adding one. golden13 on vE (11 steps up) replaces D by vD; golden13 on ^A♭ (25 up) replaces B♭ by ^B♭. In both cases the changed note moves by one sixth-step, the 36-ET golden comma, as moving from C major to G major moves F by a chromatic semitone.

silver13 behaves differently, since it is not a chain. Its closest transpositions, by a semitone (±3 sixth-steps) or by a bronze third (±13), share ten notes; no transposition shares more. golden13 and silver13 on the same tonic share seven notes (C, C♯, D, vF♯, ^F♯, B♭, B), the analogue of parallel major and minor.

Western mirror. In 12-ET, diatonic keys a fifth apart share six of seven notes and keys a semitone apart share two (near and far keys). The golden table has the same shape, with the golden sixth in place of the fifth.

Proposition (golden key distance). Let golden13 on tonic $25s$ be the chain window $s-6, \ldots, s+6$ (mod 36). For $|s| \le 18$ it shares $$\max(0,\ 13 - |s|)$$ notes with golden13 on C. (Theorem. The two windows are arcs of 13 consecutive indices on a cycle of 36 whose starting points differ by $|s|$; they overlap in $13 - |s|$ indices when $|s| < 13$. They could also meet around the far side of the cycle only if $36 - |s| < 13$, which is impossible for $|s| \le 18$. Since the 36-ET chain is a permutation, common indices are exactly common notes.)

Counts. golden13 shares 13 notes with itself only at shift 0, so it has no transpositional symmetry and 36 distinct transpositions; the same holds for silver13 and for golden7 (checked by enumeration). Hence 36 × 2 Western keys + 36 × 2 golden scales = 144.

Exercise 24.1. Give the 36-ET steps and standard names of the notes a golden third and a golden fifth above ^C.

Answer

^C is step 1. A golden third is 11 steps: step 12, E. A golden fifth is 22 steps: step 23, vA♭. So ^Cg = ^C E vA♭.

Exercise 24.2. Using $A = 3k - 2n$, $L = 7n - 10k$, $K = 13k - 9n$, find the golden apotome, limma and comma of 23-ET (golden sixth 16 steps), in steps and cents. Is golden13 still well-formed there?

Answer

$A = 2$, $L = 1$, $K = 1$ steps, i.e. 104.35, 52.17 and 52.17 c (step 52.17 c). golden13 has steps of 2 and 1: still two sizes, so yes; and golden23 is the equal scale of 23-ET.

Exercise 24.3. Which golden13 scales share exactly 11 notes with golden13 on C?

Answer

Those two chain links away: tonics $\pm 50 \equiv 14$ or 22 sixth-steps, i.e. on vF and on ^G.

Exercise 24.4 (listening). In the layers example, do you hear the second and third cadences as "out of tune"? What changes when the three cadences are played back to back?

Answer

Each cadence on its own is in tune, since each layer is a complete 12-ET. Back to back, you hear the key rise by a third of a semitone each time: a transposition by a sixth-step, not a mistuning.

Exercise 24.5. Prove that three golden sixths in 36-ET are a semitone, and that six of them are a whole tone. What are nine of them, and how far is that from the exact chain distance 9?

Answer

$3 \times 25 = 75 = 72 + 3$, i.e. 3 steps; $6 \times 25 = 150 = 144 + 6$, i.e. 6 steps. Nine golden sixths are $225 = 216 + 9$, 9 steps = 300 c (E♭), while the exact chain distance 9 is 297.81 c: 2.19 c lower.
