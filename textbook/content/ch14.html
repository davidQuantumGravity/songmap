Four-part writing

A chord progression is usually heard as a series of chords. But each chord is played or sung by several voices, and each voice has its own path from chord to chord. Voice leading is the art of those paths. Listen to a six-chord progression in four parts: first the lowest voice alone, then the highest, then all four together.

On its own the bass sounds like a series of strong leaps, the skeleton of the harmony. The soprano sounds like a small melody: E, E, F, F, D, C. The inner voices barely move. Together they make a smooth, singing whole in which you can still follow any single line if you try.

Four-part writing (often called SATB, for soprano, alto, tenor, bass) is the classic laboratory for voice leading. Its model is the chorale harmonization of the Baroque, above all J. S. Bach's several hundred four-part chorales, which were collected and published after his death. The conventions taught in harmony courses are a distillation of that style, and they are worth learning even for music that ignores them, because they make the forces between voices audible.

The progression above is I–vi–IV–ii–V–I in C major (C, Am, F, Dm, G, C):

The conventions at work here:

  • Ranges. Each part stays in a singable range. Textbooks give slightly different limits; roughly, soprano C4–G5, alto G3–D5, tenor C3–G4, bass E2–C4.
  • Spacing. Adjacent upper voices (soprano–alto, alto–tenor) stay within an octave of each other. The bass may be farther from the tenor. Wide gaps between upper voices thin the sound.
  • Doubling. A triad has three notes and four voices, so one note is doubled. In root position the root is the usual choice (C in the first chord, A in the second). The leading tone is not doubled, because it has a single strong destination, and two voices going there would move in octaves.
  • Common tones and steps. A note shared by two chords is usually kept in the same voice (the tenor's C over the first three chords). Other voices move to the nearest available note.
  • The bass is different. It carries the roots or the inversions, and it may leap; the upper voices move little. This division of labour is why the progression sounds both strong and smooth.

Definition. A voicing of a chord in $v$ voices is an ordered tuple of pitches $X = (x_1, \dots, x_v)$ with $x_1 \le x_2 \le \dots \le x_v$ (bass first). A voice leading from $X$ to $Y$ assigns each voice of $X$ a note of $Y$; in four-part writing it is simply the pair $(X, Y)$ read voice by voice, with displacement vector $Y - X$.

For the progression above the displacement vectors (bass, tenor, alto, soprano, in semitones) are

$$(-3, 0, +2, 0),\ (-4, 0, 0, +1),\ (-3, +2, 0, 0),\ (+5, -3, -2, -3),\ (+5, +1, -3, -2).$$

The upper three voices move by at most 3 semitones at every change, and in total 19 semitones over five changes; the bass alone moves 20.

The classical rules and their reasons

The most famous rule of voice leading forbids parallel fifths and octaves: two voices a fifth (or an octave) apart should not both move to another fifth (or octave). Listen to I–IV–V–I twice. The first time the voices move independently. The second time each chord is simply the previous one shifted bodily, so pairs of voices travel in fifths and octaves together.

The block version is not ugly. It is the sound of a lot of rock guitar and of medieval organum. But the four voices stop sounding like four people. Now listen to one melody with a second voice added in four different ways.

With octaves the second voice almost disappears: it sounds like a change of tone colour. With fifths it is audible but fused, like an organ stop. With thirds you hear two voices in a duet. With contrary motion you hear two independent lines.

The traditional rules for common-practice part writing can be grouped by what they protect.

A hidden (or direct) fifth or octave is reached by similar motion: both voices move in the same direction into a perfect interval, without having been in one before. The first version of the example ends with one: bass G2 → C3 up and soprano B4 → C5 up, arriving at an octave. It is allowed by the usual rule because the soprano moves by a half step.

Why these rules? Most of them follow from how the auditory system groups sounds, as David Huron argued in "Tone and voice: a derivation of the rules of voice-leading from perceptual principles" (2001). The central ideas:

  • Tonal fusion. Two tones an octave apart, and to a lesser degree a fifth apart, tend to be heard as one richer tone. Carl Stumpf studied this "fusion" (Verschmelzung) in the late nineteenth century and found the octave fuses most, then the fifth, then the fourth, then thirds and sixths. The derive layer shows the acoustic reason. A single perfect interval between two voices is fine; it is staying fused that merges them.
  • Co-modulation. Sounds that change together are grouped together. Two voices moving in the same direction by the same amount, in parallel, are a strong cue that they belong to one source. Parallel motion in a fusing interval is the strongest merging cue of all, which is exactly what the rule forbids.
  • Pitch proximity. A voice that moves by step stays one stream (Chapter 13). Crossing voices invites the ear to follow pitch instead of voice, and to swap the lines.

So the rules are not laws of nature. They are what you do if you want several independent voices. When a style wants fused, thickened lines, it does the opposite. Early medieval organum (9th–10th centuries) sang a chant melody with a second voice in parallel fourths or fifths. Organ mixture stops add several ranks of pipes sounding octaves and fifths (such as the twelfth and the fifteenth) above every note, so that whole chords move in parallel fifths and octaves and fuse into one bright timbre (Chapter 17). Debussy's parallel chords and the guitar "power chord" are other examples.

The textbook rules. Prohibitions of parallel perfect consonances are found in counterpoint treatises from the late Middle Ages onward and were codified in Johann Joseph Fux's Gradus ad Parnassum (1725), discussed in Chapter 15. They describe a style (roughly 1500–1900 choral and keyboard writing), not every music.

Theorem (coinciding partials). Let two tones have ideal harmonic spectra, with partials at $f, 2f, 3f, \dots$ and $g, 2g, 3g, \dots$, and let $g/f = p/q$ in lowest terms. Then the $n$-th partial of the upper tone coincides with a partial of the lower tone if and only if $q$ divides $n$. So, in the long run, a fraction $1/q$ of the upper tone's partials coincide with the lower tone's.

Proof. $n g = n (p/q) f$ is an integer multiple of $f$ exactly when $q \mid np$; since $\gcd(p, q) = 1$, this holds exactly when $q \mid n$. ∎

The upper tone of an octave adds no new partial frequencies at all; it only reinforces the even partials of the lower tone. That is the acoustic core of fusion, and the ordering octave, fifth, fourth, thirds matches Stumpf's. (In 12-ET the ratios are approximate, so "coincide" means "lie within a few cents", close enough to fuse; the perceptual strength of fusion is a measurement, not a theorem.)

Detecting parallels. For voices $i < j$ with pitches $x_i, x_j$ moving to $y_i, y_j$, the pair moves in parallel perfect intervals when $(x_j - x_i) \bmod 12 = (y_j - y_i) \bmod 12 \in \{0, 7\}$ and $y_i - x_i$ and $y_j - x_j$ are nonzero with the same sign. A script applying this test to all six voice pairs finds none in the hymn and in the first version of the I–IV–V–I example, and six in the block version (bass–tenor fifths and bass–alto octaves at each of its three changes).

Smoothness as distance

What makes voice leading smooth? Mostly, that voices move as little as possible. Listen to the same six chords twice in three voices. The first time each chord is played in root position in whatever register it lands. The second time each voice moves to the nearest note of the next chord.

The jumpy version is a list of chords. The smooth version is a texture: you hear three lines gently rising and falling, and the chords seem to grow out of each other.

We can measure smoothness by adding up how far the voices move. In the jumpy version (C4 E4 G4, A4 C5 E5, F3 A3 C4, D4 F4 A4, G3 B3 D4, C5 E5 G5), the voices move 26, 47, 26, 20 and 51 semitones: 170 in all. In the smooth version (G3 C4 E4, A3 C4 E4, A3 C4 F4, A3 D4 F4, B3 D4 G4, C4 E4 G4), they move 2, 1, 2, 4 and 3 semitones: 12 in all.

The smooth version is in fact the best possible: no voice leading between these chords can do better at any step. This kind of measurement gives a precise sense of which chords are "close". From a C major triad, the smallest total motion (in pitch classes, so octaves don't count) to each major and minor triad is:

Some results are surprising. The dominant G and subdominant F are 3 semitones away, while the chromatic mediants A♭ and E are only 2 (C E G → C E♭ A♭ moves E down and G up, a half step each). The parallel minor Cm and the mediant Em are a single semitone away: these are the neo-Riemannian moves P and L of Chapter 10. Voice-leading distance and harmonic function are different things. Function says where a chord wants to go; distance says how cheaply the voices can get there. Much of the expressive power of Romantic harmony comes from chords that are functionally far but close in voice leading (Chapter 18).

Definition. For chords $X = (x_1, \dots, x_n)$ and $Y$ with the same number of notes, a voice leading is a bijection $\sigma$ from the notes of $X$ to the notes of $Y$, and its size is a norm of the displacement vector. Common choices:

$$\|d\|_1 = \sum_i |d_i| \ \text{(total motion)},\qquad \|d\|_2 = \Big(\sum_i d_i^2\Big)^{1/2},\qquad \|d\|_\infty = \max_i |d_i|\ \text{(largest move)}.$$

In pitch-class space, each $d_i$ is taken as the shortest signed distance mod 12, in $[-6, 6]$. The voice-leading distance $D(X, Y)$ is the minimum size over all bijections. For triads there are $3! = 6$ bijections, so $D$ is computed by checking them all.

Example. $D_1(\mathrm{C}, \mathrm{A}\flat) = 2$, realized by C → C, E → E♭, G → A♭, displacement $(0, -1, +1)$. $D_1(\mathrm{C}, \mathrm{F}) = 3$: C → C, E → F, G → A, displacement $(0, +1, +2)$. The table above lists $D_1$ from C major to all 24 major and minor triads (exhaustive computation).

Optimality of the smooth example. The minimal $D_1$ for C → Am → F → Dm → G → C is $(2, 1, 2, 4, 3)$, and the smooth voicing achieves exactly these values at every step, so its total, 12, is the minimum.

The norms disagree on some comparisons, which is why voice-leading theory usually states results for a whole family of norms at once. $L_1$ counts total effort; $L_\infty$ asks that no single voice jump far.

The geometry of voice leading

If chords can be close or far, they live in a space. One chord sits at the centre of the space of three-note chords: the augmented triad, C E G♯, made of three equal major thirds. Move any one of its notes by one semitone and you land on a major or a minor triad. Listen to the augmented triad going to six different chords, each time with one voice moving a half step.

The augmented triad sounds suspended, belonging to no key in particular. That ambiguity is the sound of being at the centre: it is equally close to six different destinations.

Here are the six resolutions written out. Each moves exactly one note of C E G♯ by one semitone.

Dmitri Tymoczko made this picture precise in "The Geometry of Musical Chords" (Science, 2006) and A Geometry of Music (2011), and with Clifton Callender and Ian Quinn in "Generalized voice-leading spaces" (Science, 2008). The idea: an unordered chord of $n$ notes, with octaves ignored, is a point in a space, and a voice leading is a path in that space. The spaces have a beautiful shape:

  • Two-note chords form a Möbius strip. Its edge is made of unisons (C–C, C♯–C♯, …), and running around its middle are the tritones (C–F♯, …), the most even dyads.
  • Three-note chords form a twisted triangular prism. The augmented triads, the perfectly even ones, run along its central axis. Major and minor triads lie close around them, which is why each augmented triad is one semitone from six of them. Chords with doubled notes lie on the boundary.

The consonant chords of tonal music are nearly even: they divide the octave almost equally (C E G divides it into 4 + 3 + 5 semitones). Nearly even chords lie near the centre of chord space, so they can be connected to many other chords by small motions. Tymoczko argues that this, together with acoustic consonance, is why they became the building blocks of Western harmony. Jack Douthett and Peter Steinbach drew the network of these single-semitone moves among major, minor and augmented triads as a graph they called "Cube Dance" (1998).

36-ET adds points between the 12-ET chords, and the golden triad sits in a special place among them. On a common root, Cg = (C + Cm + Caug)/3 voice by voice: it is the centroid of the triangle formed by major, minor and augmented in this space, and it is only 2 sixth-steps from C, 3 from Cm and 3 from Caug. The passing chords of the golden system lie on straight lines between Western chords: the half step is split into three sixth-steps, and the passing chords are the points one third and two thirds of the way along (Chapter 27, Chapter 28).

Listen to V → I twice: first directly, then with each half step in the upper voices split into three sixth-steps. The leading tone B rises B → ^B → vC → C and D rises D → vE♭ → ^E♭ → E, while the Gs are held (two upper voices and the bass).

Chord spaces (statement). Model pitch classes as points of the circle $\mathbb{R}/12\mathbb{Z}$, allowing any real value. An ordered $n$-note chord is a point of the torus $T^n = (\mathbb{R}/12\mathbb{Z})^n$; forgetting the order means dividing by the symmetric group $S_n$ that permutes coordinates. The space of unordered $n$-note chords is the quotient $T^n / S_n$, an orbifold. For $n = 2$ it is a Möbius strip whose boundary is the diagonal $x_1 = x_2$ (unisons); for $n = 3$ it is a triangular prism whose top and bottom faces are identified with a twist of one third of a turn. Proofs are in Tymoczko (2006) and Callender, Quinn and Tymoczko, "Generalized voice-leading spaces" (Science, 2008).

The dyad case, sketched. Write a dyad with $x_1 \le x_2 \le x_1 + 12$ and use the coordinates $s = x_1 + x_2$ and $d = x_2 - x_1 \in [0, 12]$. Transposing both notes by an octave changes $s$ by 24 and leaves $d$ alone, so $s$ is taken mod 24. The same dyad can also be written as $(x_2, x_1 + 12)$, with coordinates $(s + 12, 12 - d)$. So the space is the strip $0 \le s \le 12$, $0 \le d \le 12$, with the edge $s = 12$ glued to the edge $s = 0$ after the flip $d \mapsto 12 - d$: a Möbius strip. Its boundary, $d = 0$ glued to $d = 12$, is the circle of unisons, and the line $d = 6$, which the flip maps to itself, is the circle of tritones. ∎ (sketch)

The augmented hub (theorem, by enumeration). Each augmented triad is at $L_1$ distance 1 from exactly six major and minor triads, three major and three minor, as tabulated. Conversely each major or minor triad is at distance 1 from exactly one augmented triad (C E G → C E G♯ by G → G♯; A C E → G♯ C E by A → G♯). So the 4 augmented triads, 12 major and 12 minor triads form a graph in which each augmented triad has degree 6 and each major or minor triad has exactly one augmented neighbour.

Straight lines and averages. In the space of ordered voicings $\mathbb{R}^n$, the voice leading $X \to Y$ is the segment $X + t(Y - X)$, $0 \le t \le 1$. The points at $t = 1/3$ and $t = 2/3$ are $(2X + Y)/3$ and $(X + 2Y)/3$. When every voice moves by a whole number of semitones, these points have coordinates in multiples of 1/3 semitone, so they lie on the 36-ET grid. This is the uniform sub-chromatic path of Chapter 27: in the example above, $X = (\mathrm{G3}, \mathrm{B3}, \mathrm{D4}, \mathrm{G4})$, $Y = (\mathrm{G3}, \mathrm{C4}, \mathrm{E4}, \mathrm{G4})$, $Y - X = (0, +1, +2, 0)$ semitones, and the two passing chords move the inner voices by $(+1/3, +2/3)$ and $(+2/3, +4/3)$ semitones.

Exercise 14.1 (listening). In the "parallel and contrary" example, rank the four versions by how clearly you hear two separate voices. Compare your ranking with the coinciding-partials table.

Answer

Most listeners hear the contrary-motion version as most independent and the octaves as least: octaves (all partials coincide), fifths (half), thirds (about a fifth or a quarter), contrary motion (changing intervals, no co-modulation). The ranking is a perceptual measurement and varies somewhat with timbre and loudness.

Exercise 14.2. Find the parallel perfect intervals in this progression (bass, tenor, alto, soprano): C3 E3 G3 C4 → D3 F3 A3 D4.

Answer

Every voice rises a whole step, so every pair moves in parallel; the question is which intervals are perfect. Bass–tenor C3–E3 (M3) → D3–F3 (m3): fine. Bass–alto C3–G3 (P5) → D3–A3 (P5): parallel fifths. Bass–soprano C3–C4 (P8) → D3–D4 (P8): parallel octaves. Tenor–alto E3–G3 (m3) → F3–A3 (M3): fine. Tenor–soprano E3–C4 (m6) → F3–D4 (M6): fine. Alto–soprano G3–C4 (P4) → A3–D4 (P4): parallel fourths, allowed between upper voices in this style.

Exercise 14.3. Compute $D_1$ from C major to E major and give a voice leading that realizes it.

Answer

C E G → B E G♯: C → B (−1), E → E (0), G → G♯ (+1). Total 2. It is minimal because the two chords share only one note, E, so at least two voices must move, each by at least a semitone.

Exercise 14.4 (proof). Using the coinciding-partials theorem, show that the fraction of coinciding upper partials for a just interval $p/q$ depends only on $q$, and explain why the twelfth (3/1) fuses as strongly as the octave by this measure.

Answer

The $n$-th upper partial coincides iff $q \mid n$, a condition independent of $p$. For 3/1, $q = 1$, so every partial of the upper tone ($3f, 6f, 9f, \dots$, where $f$ is the lower fundamental) coincides with a lower partial. This is why organ builders can add a pipe at the twelfth to a fundamental stop and hear a single, brighter tone.

Exercise 14.5. Check that the golden triad Cg = C vE ^G is the voice-by-voice average of C, Cm and Caug, in sixth-steps above C.

Answer

C = (0, 12, 21), Cm = (0, 9, 21), Caug = (0, 12, 24). The average is $(0, (12 + 9 + 12)/3, (21 + 21 + 24)/3) = (0, 11, 22)$, which is C vE ^G.
