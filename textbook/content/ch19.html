The whole-tone scale

Play only whole tones, C, D, E, F♯, G♯, A♯, and you are back on C after six steps instead of seven. Listen to the scale and to the chords that live in it. Nothing in it pulls anywhere. There is no semitone to lean on, so there is no leading tone, and there is no perfect fifth, so no chord sounds like a home. Every note stands in exactly the same relation to the others, and the music seems to float.

Now try the whole-tone scale on C♯: a new set of six notes. Then try it on D. You may expect a third scale, but it has exactly the notes of the first one. There are only two whole-tone scales, and between them they use all twelve notes.

On the pitch-class circle the whole-tone scale is a regular hexagon. Rotate it by one position (transpose by a semitone) and you get the other hexagon; rotate it by two and it lands on itself. That is the whole story of its character:

  • Only even intervals. Every interval between two of its notes is 2, 4, 6, 8 or 10 semitones: whole tones, major thirds, tritones and their inversions. No semitone, no minor third, no perfect fourth or fifth.
  • One kind of triad. Stacking thirds inside the scale gives only augmented triads, and there are just two of them as sets of notes: C–E–G♯ and D–F♯–A♯.
  • No center. Any of the six notes could be called the "tonic" and the scale would look the same from it. Composers who want a center must supply it from outside, with a pedal, a register or a repeated note.

Some familiar tonal chords are whole-tone chords in disguise. The French augmented sixth A♭–C–D–F♯ uses only even pitch classes (8, 0, 2, 6), so it lies inside the whole-tone scale on C. The Tristan chord becomes a French sixth of the same kind, F–A–B–D♯ (5, 9, 11, 3), when its top voice reaches A (Chapter 18); that one lies inside the other whole-tone scale, as does the dominant seventh with a lowered fifth, G–B–D♭–F (7, 11, 1, 5). Late-Romantic harmony that leans on these chords is already halfway to the floating whole-tone sound, and composers around 1900 needed only to stay there instead of resolving.

The scale had been used for special effects before 1900. A famous early example is in Glinka's opera Ruslan and Lyudmila (1842), and Liszt also used it. Debussy made it a whole color of its own (Chapter 22).

Golden mirror. The whole-tone scale is the 12-ET picture of one subgroup, and 36-ET has many more subgroups to draw. Steps of 2 sixth-steps (an 18-note scale) or of 4 (a 9-note scale, 133.33 c per step) are "whole-tone scales" that 12-ET cannot play. You can hear the 9-note one in the last section; the full list is in Chapter 33.

Definition. The whole-tone collections are the two cosets of the subgroup $2\mathbb{Z}_{12} = \{0,2,4,6,8,10\}$:

$$WT_0 = \{0,2,4,6,8,10\}, \qquad WT_1 = \{1,3,5,7,9,11\} = WT_0 + 1.$$

Proposition. (i) $T_n(WT_0) = WT_0$ exactly when $n$ is even, so there are 2 distinct transpositions. (ii) Every interval within $WT_0$ is even. (iii) The interval vector of $WT_0$ is $\langle 0,6,0,6,0,3\rangle$.

Proof. (i) $T_n(WT_0) = WT_0 + n$, which is $WT_0$ if $n$ is even and $WT_1$ if $n$ is odd. (ii) The difference of two even numbers is even. (iii) Among the $\binom{6}{2} = 15$ pairs, interval classes 1, 3 and 5 (odd) cannot occur. Each note has two neighbours at interval class 2 and two at interval class 4, giving $6\cdot 2/2 = 6$ pairs of each, and one partner at the tritone, giving $6/2 = 3$ pairs. $\square$

The octatonic scale

Alternate a semitone and a whole tone: C, C♯, D♯, E, F♯, G, A, A♯, and then C. Eight notes. This scale has semitones, so it has edges, and it contains ordinary major and minor triads. But it contains them in a strange arrangement: four major triads whose roots are a minor third apart, C, E♭, F♯ and A, all inside the same eight notes.

The octatonic scale (from Greek okto, eight) is the 12-ET's other famous symmetric scale. In Russian music theory it is called the Rimsky-Korsakov scale, after the composer who used it widely. The name "octatonic" is usually credited to the theorist Arthur Berger, in an article on Stravinsky (1963). Its properties, all computed:

  • Three transpositions. Transposing by a minor third (3 semitones) maps it to itself. Only the transpositions by 0, 1 and 2 semitones give different collections.
  • Two modes. Starting on C it goes half–whole. Starting on C♯ it goes whole–half. Every other starting note gives one of these two patterns.
  • Its triads. 4 major (C, E♭, F♯, A), 4 minor (Cm, E♭m, F♯m, Am), 8 diminished, and no augmented triad. The major and minor triads are exactly the eight chords of the octatonic cycle in Chapter 18.
  • Interval vector $\langle 4,4,8,4,4,4\rangle$: every interval class occurs, minor thirds twice as often as the others.

Because the scale has both the semitone (which makes leading tones) and the symmetry (which keeps a center from emerging), composers found it ideal for music that is colorful, harmonic and yet not tonal. Rimsky-Korsakov, Scriabin, Ravel, Stravinsky, Bartók and Messiaen all used it. On Stravinsky see Chapter 22.

Definition. Let $D = \{0,3,6,9\}$, the diminished seventh chord, a subgroup of $\mathbb{Z}_{12}$ of order 4. Its cosets are $D$, $D+1$, $D+2$. The three octatonic collections are the unions of two of them:

$$O_{01} = D \cup (D+1),\qquad O_{12} = (D+1) \cup (D+2),\qquad O_{02} = D \cup (D+2).$$

Proposition. The stabilizer $\{n : T_n(O) = O\}$ of each octatonic collection is exactly $D$, so it has $12/4 = 3$ distinct transpositions. Proof. $D$ fixes each coset, so it fixes their union. Conversely, if $T_n$ fixes $O_{01}$, it permutes its two cosets. If $T_n$ swapped $D$ and $D+1$, then $n \equiv 1$ and also $n \equiv -1 \pmod 3$, which is impossible. So $T_n$ fixes $D$, and $n \in D$. $\square$

The same argument shows that the four major triads of $O_{01}$ form one orbit under $D$: $T_3$ carries C to E♭ to F♯ to A. That is why the roots in the octatonic cycle climb by minor thirds.

Messiaen's modes of limited transposition

Olivier Messiaen (1908–1992) built much of his harmony from a small family of scales he called modes of limited transposition. The whole-tone and octatonic scales are the first two. Five more follow, each made of a little pattern of steps that repeats inside the octave. Listen to all seven on C.

"Limited transposition" means that you run out of new versions quickly. Mode 3 has nine notes, built from the step pattern whole, half, half, three times over. Transpose it up a semitone, then two, then three: each time you hear new notes. Transpose it by four and you are back to the original set exactly.

Messiaen's harmony favored rich chords taken from inside one mode. Here is a chord (a major triad with an added sixth and augmented fourth, C–E–G–A–F♯) moved by minor thirds. Every chord stays inside the same octatonic collection.

Messiaen set out the modes in his treatise Technique de mon langage musical (1944). He wrote of the "charm of impossibilities" in them. They cannot be transposed more than a few times, just as his non-retrogradable (palindromic) rhythms cannot be reversed. The seven modes, on C, with numbers computed from the sets:

Each mode is a block of steps repeated 2, 3, 4 or 6 times to fill the octave. The number of repeats is the number of transpositions that map the mode onto itself (including $T_0$), and 12 divided by that number is the number of different transpositions it has. Some further facts, all computed:

  • All seven modes are also symmetric under inversion: each one reads the same, as a set, when turned upside down about some axis.
  • Modes 1 and 2 were in use before Messiaen (by Debussy, Rimsky-Korsakov, Stravinsky and others). Messiaen used the modes throughout his output, in works such as La Nativité du Seigneur (1935) and Quatuor pour la fin du temps (1941).
  • Messiaen regarded other symmetric scales as "truncations" of his seven. The next section proves him right in a precise sense: every pitch-class set with limited transposition is contained in some transposition of one of the seven modes.

Limited transposition changes what "modulation" can mean. A piece in mode 2 can move to another transposition of the mode, but there are only three, and any two of them share a diminished seventh chord (four notes), which can serve as a pivot. Mode 3's four transpositions overlap in more interesting ways (computed): $T_0$ and $T_1$ share the six notes of a hexatonic scale, $T_0$ and $T_2$ share a whole-tone scale, and $T_0$ and $T_3$ share another hexatonic scale. So within one mode, the composer has a small, closed world of related "keys", each pair linked by a smaller symmetric scale.

Not every symmetric set is a mode. The hexatonic scale C–E♭–E–G–A♭–B of Chapter 18 also has limited transposition (four forms). It is not among Messiaen's seven, because it is a subset ("truncation") of mode 3, which contains it.

Definition. A pitch-class set $S \subseteq \mathbb{Z}_{12}$ has limited transposition if $T_n(S) = S$ for some $n \not\equiv 0$. Its stabilizer is $\operatorname{Stab}(S) = \{n : T_n(S) = S\}$.

Lemma. $\operatorname{Stab}(S)$ is a subgroup of $\mathbb{Z}_{12}$, and $S$ has exactly $12/|\operatorname{Stab}(S)|$ distinct transpositions. Proof. If $T_m$ and $T_n$ fix $S$, so do $T_{m+n}$ and $T_{-n}$. The transpositions of $S$ correspond to the cosets of the stabilizer (orbit–stabilizer theorem, Appendix A). $\square$

For the seven modes the stabilizers are the subgroups of order 6, 4, 3, 2, 2, 2, 2, giving 2, 3, 4, 6, 6, 6, 6 transpositions, as in the table.

Subgroups and cosets of Z12

Every symmetric scale you have heard in this chapter is a pattern that repeats around the circle a whole number of times. You can hear the repeat: a whole-tone scale sounds the same from every one of its notes, an octatonic scale from notes a minor third apart, mode 3 from notes a major third apart. The repeat is always a regular polygon hidden in the twelve notes: the tritone, the augmented triad, the diminished seventh, the whole-tone hexagon. In 36-ET there are more polygons to hide. Here is one that 12-ET does not have, a scale of nine equal steps of 133.33 cents, which sounds like a whole-tone scale that has lost its way.

The pitch classes $\mathbb{Z}_{12} = \{0, 1, \dots, 11\}$ form a group under addition mod 12 (Chapter 8). A subgroup is a subset closed under addition. $\mathbb{Z}_{12}$ has exactly one subgroup for each divisor of 12:

A coset is a subgroup moved by a transposition: {1, 5, 9} is a coset of the augmented triad, the C♯ augmented triad. The cosets of a subgroup tile the circle without overlap. So the four augmented triads partition the twelve notes, and so do the three diminished sevenths and the two whole-tone scales.

The main theorem of this chapter says that a set is symmetric under a transposition exactly when it is built from whole cosets. The octatonic scale is two diminished sevenths, mode 3 is three augmented triads, and the hexatonic scale is two augmented triads. The Petrushka chord, C major plus F♯ major, is three tritones: {C, F♯}, {E, B♭}, {G, C♯}. You can make a symmetric scale by choosing a subgroup and picking any collection of its cosets. That is all there is to it.

Golden mirror. $\mathbb{Z}_{36}$ has a subgroup for each divisor of 36, of orders 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 9, 12, 18, 36. The new ones, of orders 9 and 18, generated by 4 and 2 sixth-steps, have no 12-ET counterpart, and the order-12 subgroup is 12-ET itself, sitting inside 36-ET. By the counting below, 36-ET has 266,176 sets with limited transposition (counting the empty and the full set), which form 14,938 transposition classes of nonempty proper sets, against 76 sets and 15 classes for 12-ET. Messiaen's "charm of impossibilities" multiplies (Chapter 33).

Theorem. Let $H = \langle k \rangle$ be the subgroup generated by $k$. Then $T_k(S) = S$ if and only if $S$ is a union of cosets of $H$.

Proof. If $S$ is a union of cosets $a + H$, then $T_k(a+H) = a + k + H = a + H$ because $k \in H$, so $T_k$ fixes $S$. Conversely, if $T_k(S) = S$ and $a \in S$, then $a + k, a + 2k, \dots \in S$, so the whole coset $a + H \subseteq S$. $\square$

Counting (theorem, with an exhaustive check). The subgroups of $\mathbb{Z}_{12}$ of prime order are $\{0,6\}$ and $\{0,4,8\}$, and every nontrivial subgroup contains one of them. So $S$ has limited transposition iff it is fixed by $T_6$ or by $T_4$. Sets fixed by $T_6$ are unions of the 6 tritone cosets: $2^6$ of them. Sets fixed by $T_4$: $2^4$. Sets fixed by both are fixed by $T_2$: $2^2$. By inclusion and exclusion,

$$2^6 + 2^4 - 2^2 = 76$$

sets have limited transposition, including the empty and the full set. Grouped by transposition, the 74 nonempty proper ones form 15 classes: one of size 2 (the tritone), one of size 3, three of size 4, five of size 6, three of size 8, one of size 9 and one of size 10. Two of the hexachord classes are inversions of each other (one is the Petrushka chord), so under transposition and inversion there are 14.

Theorem (computed exhaustively). Each of the 15 classes is contained in some transposition of at least one of Messiaen's seven modes. The 9-note mode 3 and the 10-note mode 7 are maximal, and every symmetric set lies inside a transposition of mode 3 or of mode 7.

In $\mathbb{Z}_{36}$ the prime-order subgroups are $\{0,18\}$ and $\{0,12,24\}$, so the same argument gives $2^{18} + 2^{12} - 2^{6} = 266{,}176$ sets with limited transposition. Burnside's lemma, confirmed by direct enumeration, gives 14,938 transposition classes of nonempty proper ones.

Exercise 19.1. How many transpositions does the set {C, C♯, F♯, G} have, and which subgroup is its stabilizer?

Answer

{0, 1, 6, 7} = {0, 6} ∪ {1, 7}, two tritone cosets. Its stabilizer is {0, 6}, of order 2, so it has 12/2 = 6 transpositions.

Exercise 19.2 (listening). In the mode 3 example, which transposition first repeats an earlier one? Why must it be that one?

Answer

T4. Mode 3 is a union of cosets of {0, 4, 8}, so its stabilizer contains 4, and no smaller positive transposition fixes it (its stabilizer is exactly {0, 4, 8}).

Exercise 19.3. Show that a set with an odd number of notes can have limited transposition only if its stabilizer is exactly {0, 4, 8}, and find all such sizes.

Answer

A set fixed by $T_n$ is a union of cosets of $\langle n\rangle$, so its size is a multiple of $|\langle n\rangle|$. The possible orders are 2, 3, 4, 6, 12. Odd sizes need order 3 (not 2, 4 or 6), so sizes 3 and 9: the augmented triad and mode 3.

Exercise 19.4. List the four major triads in the octatonic collection {C, C♯, D♯, E, F♯, G, A, A♯} and show that $T_3$ permutes them cyclically.

Answer

C, E♭, F♯, A. $T_3$: C → E♭ → F♯ → A → C.

Exercise 19.5. In $\mathbb{Z}_{36}$, which subgroup is generated by 8 sixth-steps, and how many notes per octave does a coset of it have?

Answer

$\gcd(8, 36) = 4$, so $\langle 8 \rangle = \langle 4 \rangle$, of order 36/4 = 9: nine notes, the scale heard above.
