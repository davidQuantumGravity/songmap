Contour

Hum a tune you know well and trace it in the air with your hand. Your hand goes up and down long before you could name a single interval. That outline, the pattern of rises and falls, is the melody's contour, and it is the first thing most listeners remember. Here are four melodies with exactly the same rhythm. They differ only in shape: an arch that climbs and comes home, a bowl that dips and climbs back, a line that rises to a high C, and one that falls to a low C.

Even without harmony the four shapes have different characters. The arch feels complete: it leaves home, peaks and returns. The rising line ends in the air, like a question. The falling one settles. Contour carries a large share of a melody's expression before any question of scales or chords comes up.

A melody is a succession of pitches in time, heard as one line. Two things describe it: its pitches and its rhythm. The contour keeps only the direction of each move: up, down or same. The arch above, C E G A G E C, has the contour up, up, up, down, down, down. The bowl, A G E D E G A, has the mirror image: down, down, down, up, up, up.

Contour matters because memory treats it as a separate layer. In a well-known line of experiments, W. Jay Dowling showed that listeners hearing an unfamiliar melody often accept a different melody with the same contour as "the same tune", especially when it is transposed. For familiar melodies the exact intervals matter much more: a well-known tune sung with the right shape but the wrong steps sounds wrong. Contour is the outline, and intervals fill it in.

Many melodies share a large-scale shape: a rise to a high point somewhere in the middle and a descent to the end. David Huron's study of thousands of phrases from a large collection of Western folk songs (1996) found that the average phrase has exactly this melodic arch, although individual phrases vary a great deal. Notice the word average: this is a measurement about a corpus, not a rule that good melodies must follow.

Definition. Write a melody as a sequence of pitches $p_0, p_1, \dots, p_{n-1}$ (in semitones, MIDI numbers for instance). Its intervals are $\Delta_i = p_{i} - p_{i-1}$ for $i = 1, \dots, n-1$, and its contour is the sign sequence $c_i = \operatorname{sgn}(\Delta_i) \in \{-1, 0, +1\}$.

Two melodies have the same contour when their sign sequences agree. Since each of the $n-1$ moves has three possible signs, an $n$-note melody has $3^{n-1}$ possible contours: 729 for seven notes. A finer description, used in contour theory, ranks the notes from lowest to highest. If all seven notes are different, the ranking is a permutation of $\{0, \dots, 6\}$, and there are $7! = 5040$ such contour segments. The arch has repeated pitches, so its ranking is $(0, 1, 2, 3, 2, 1, 0)$.

Invariance. Contour is unchanged by any order-preserving change of pitch: transposition $p \mapsto p + k$, but also stretching $p \mapsto a p + b$ with $a > 0$, or moving a melody from one scale to another degree by degree. That is why a melody in C major and its scale-degree copy in a quite different scale can share a contour, even when every interval changes (see the mirror below and Chapter 30).

Steps and leaps

A melody can move to a neighbouring note, a step, or jump over notes, a leap. Listen to three short lines. The first moves only by step and sounds smooth and singable. The second leaps everywhere, and it barely holds together as one line: your ear starts to hear it as notes scattered across registers. The third makes one big leap up and then walks back down by step, filling the gap it opened.

The third shape, a leap answered by steps in the opposite direction, is one of the most common gestures in melody. It sounds balanced, as if the line had borrowed height and then paid it back.

In a seven-note scale a step is a second: a semitone (1 semitone) or a whole tone (2). A move by a third (3 or 4 semitones) is often called a skip, and anything from a fourth up is a leap.

Most melodies are mostly steps. A simple count makes the point. The first two phrases of the "Ode to Joy" theme from Beethoven's Ninth Symphony (1824), heard later in this chapter, have 30 notes and 29 moves between them: 20 steps, 9 repeated notes and no leaps at all. Why should steps dominate? Three reasons work together.

  • Voices. Singing is the oldest melodic instrument, and small intervals are easier to sing in tune.
  • Grouping. The ear groups sounds that are close in pitch into one stream. Fast alternation between a high and a low register splits into two separate lines, a phenomenon studied in detail by Albert Bregman in Auditory Scene Analysis (1990). Steps keep a melody in one stream; wide leaps strain it. Composers exploit this on purpose: a solo violin line that alternates registers quickly can sound like two voices.
  • Expectation. Listeners expect small intervals, so steps are heard as continuation and leaps as events.

The gap-fill idea is that a leap creates a gap that the melody then fills, usually by steps in the other direction. Leonard Meyer described this in Explaining Music (1973), and Eugene Narmour's implication-realization model (1990) built a theory of melodic expectation on similar principles. An important correction came from Paul von Hippel and David Huron ("Why do skips precede reversals?", 2000). They argued that much of the tendency to reverse after a leap is regression to the mean: a big leap usually lands near the edge of the melody's range, and from the edge most available notes lie back toward the middle. The derive layer shows this effect in a model with no melodic rules at all.

In golden7 (C C♯ vE vF ^G ^A♭ B), the two scale steps are 3 and 8 sixth-steps: 100 c and 266.67 c in 36-ET (exact chain values 99.27 c and 267.6 c). The larger "step" is wider than a whole tone and close to a minor third, so a stepwise golden7 melody moves in a mixture of semitones and near-thirds. How listeners classify those moves, as steps or as skips, is an open listening question (Chapter 30).

A model with regression and nothing else (measurement). Let the pitches of a "melody" be independent draws from a normal distribution centred on middle C with a standard deviation of 3 semitones, rounded to whole semitones. Such a melody has no memory: each note ignores the previous one. Yet in two million simulated notes,

Size of the previous moveProbability the next move reverses direction
step (1 or 2 semitones)0.53
3 or 4 semitones0.63
5 or 6 semitones0.73
7 semitones or more0.84

(A repeated note counts as not reversing.) The larger the leap, the more likely a reversal, purely because a large leap tends to end far from the centre. With a wider distribution (standard deviation 4 semitones) the trend is the same but weaker: 0.52 after a step and 0.80 after a leap of 7 or more. This does not refute gap-fill as a stylistic practice; it shows that any test of it must compare real melodies with a baseline that already includes regression. That is the comparison von Hippel and Huron made.

Why the effect appears. If $X_1$ and $X_2$ are independent with the same symmetric distribution around $\mu$, then given a large upward leap $X_1 - X_0 = d > 0$, the note $X_1$ is likely to lie above $\mu$, and then $P(X_2 < X_1) > 1/2$. The bigger $d$, the larger the expected $X_1 - \mu$, and the larger this probability.

Motifs

A motif is a short musical idea that a piece keeps coming back to. The most famous example in Western music is four notes long: three quick repeated notes and a longer one a third lower. Beethoven opens his Fifth Symphony (first performed in 1808) with it, and then states it again one step lower.

The motif is recognizable from its rhythm and its shape alone. Beethoven builds much of the movement from it, repeated, moved to other pitches and passed between instruments. The next example shows the same principle with a gentler idea: one arpeggio figure, kept the same shape, moving through a chain of chords.

A motif has an identity made of a few features: a rhythm, a contour, often a characteristic interval. It can be changed in many ways and still be recognized. The basic operations are:

  • Repetition: the motif again, unchanged.
  • Sequence: the motif repeated at another pitch level, usually several times in a row, each time moved by the same amount. In the Beethoven example the second statement is a step lower.
  • Variation: the motif with something changed: an interval widened, a note added, the rhythm altered.
  • Fragmentation: only a piece of the motif, repeated or sequenced. Fragmentation is a classic way of building intensity before a cadence (see the sentence in Chapter 16).

A sequence can be real or tonal. A real sequence transposes every interval exactly. A tonal sequence moves the motif by scale degrees, so that it stays in the key and its intervals change size slightly: a major third may become a minor third. In the Beethoven example, G–E♭ is a major third and F–D a minor third: the second statement is a tonal (diatonic) step lower in C minor, not an exact transposition.

The second example uses a third kind of identity: a figure defined relative to the chord. Its notes are "root, third, fifth, octave, fifth, third" of whatever chord is sounding. The harmony underneath is the circle-of-fifths progression I–IV–vii°–iii–vi–ii–V–I (C F B° Em Am Dm G C), and the figure follows it like a shadow. This is how arpeggios and accompaniment patterns work, and it is also how this book's audio is written: a SongMap motif can be given in letters (c4 d e), in scale degrees (0/4 1 2), or relative to the chord (R8 3 5 R+), and the same text produces different notes over different harmonies.

Richard Wagner's leitmotifs ("leading motifs") attach a motif to a character, object or idea in a music drama, so that its return carries meaning. The term was popularized by writers on Wagner rather than by Wagner himself, but the practice runs through his Ring cycle and later through film music.

Definition. Represent a motif as a list of pairs $m = ((p_0, d_0), \dots, (p_{k-1}, d_{k-1}))$, where $p_i$ is a pitch and $d_i$ a duration. The real transposition by $t$ semitones is $T_t(m) = ((p_i + t, d_i))_i$.

A tonal transposition acts on scale degrees instead. Number the notes of a seven-note scale by degree $s \in \mathbb{Z}$ (degree 0 = tonic, degree 7 = tonic an octave up). Pitch is the function $\pi(s) = 12\lfloor s/7 \rfloor + \sigma(s \bmod 7)$, where $\sigma = (0, 2, 4, 5, 7, 9, 11)$ in C major. A tonal transposition by $j$ degrees maps $s \mapsto s + j$, and its pitch intervals are generally different from the original's, because $\pi(s + j) - \pi(s)$ depends on $s$. The C-minor degrees of the Beethoven motif are $(4, 4, 4, 2)$ and then $(3, 3, 3, 1)$: a tonal transposition by $j = -1$.

Chord-relative motifs. The arpeggio figure is a list of chord members $(R, 3, 5, R^{+}, 5, 3)$. Over a chord with root $r$ and member intervals $(0, a, b)$ it sounds $(r, r + a, r + b, r + 12, r + b, r + a)$. Over the falling-fifth chain the roots alternate up a fourth and down a fifth (C5, F5, B4, E5, A4, D5, G4, C5 in the example), so the line zig-zags while descending one step per two bars.

Inversion, retrograde, augmentation

Composers have long transformed motifs in ways that can be pictured as moving a shape on paper. Turn it upside down, so every rise becomes a fall: inversion. Play it backwards: retrograde. Do both: retrograde inversion. Slow it down, every note twice as long: augmentation. Listen to a four-note motif and its transformations.

The inversion is easy to hear as related: it has the same rhythm and a mirrored shape. The retrograde is much harder to recognize, because it reverses the rhythm as well. Augmentation keeps the shape but changes its pace, and in a fugue a subject in augmentation can sound like a slow chorale under faster music.

Write the motif as pitches and durations in beats (quarter notes): C (dotted quarter, 1.5), D (eighth, 0.5), F (quarter, 1), E (quarter, 1).

The inversion here is diatonic: it counts scale steps (up 1 step becomes down 1 step). A chromatic or real inversion counts semitones instead: C D F E is 0, +2, +5, +4 semitones from C, so its real inversion is 0, −2, −5, −4, that is C B♭ G A♭. The diatonic version stays in C major; the real version leaves it.

These devices are old. Inverted subjects are everywhere in Bach's fugues, and his Art of Fugue contains a canon by augmentation in contrary motion. His Musical Offering (1747) includes a crab canon (canon cancrizans), in which a line is played against its own retrograde. In the twentieth century, twelve-tone composers made O, I, R and RI the four basic forms of a tone row (Chapter 21).

How much of this does a listener actually hear? Listening experiments have generally found transposition far easier to recognize than inversion, and retrograde harder still. Transformations often work as a composer's construction method and as a source of unity that is felt more than identified. That is not a weakness: a well-made retrograde still shares intervals, rhythm values and harmony with its original.

The same four operations apply to melodies in golden7, where a diatonic inversion maps each golden7 step to the step below it, and the alternating 100 c and 266.67 c steps reverse their order. Golden motifs and their transformations are the subject of Chapter 30.

Picture a melody as a set of points in the plane, time across and pitch up. Each transformation is then a simple geometric map:

OperationMap on (time $t$, pitch $p$)Geometry
Transposition $T_k$$(t, p) \mapsto (t, p + k)$vertical shift
Inversion $I_a$$(t, p) \mapsto (t, 2a - p)$reflection in the line $p = a$
Retrograde $R$$(t, p) \mapsto (L - t, p)$reflection in the line $t = L/2$
RI$(t, p) \mapsto (L - t, 2a - p)$rotation by 180°
Augmentation$(t, p) \mapsto (2t, p)$horizontal stretch

(For notes with durations, the retrograde maps a note occupying $[t, t + d]$ to $[L - t - d, L - t]$, where $L$ is the motif's length.)

Theorem. For a fixed axis $a$, the four maps $\{1, I_a, R, RI_a\}$ form a group isomorphic to $\mathbb{Z}_2 \times \mathbb{Z}_2$ (the Klein four-group).

Proof. $I_a$ and $R$ are each their own inverse: $2a - (2a - p) = p$ and $L - (L - t) = t$. They act on different coordinates, so they commute: $I_a R = R I_a$, both equal to $(t, p) \mapsto (L - t, 2a - p)$. Hence $(RI_a)^2 = R^2 I_a^2 = 1$, and the four elements are closed under composition, each of order at most 2. A group of order 4 in which every element squares to the identity is $\mathbb{Z}_2 \times \mathbb{Z}_2$. ∎

The axis matters. If "inversion" means "about the motif's own first note", then $R$ changes the first note and the two operations no longer commute in general. Take C D F E with real (chromatic) inversion. Inverting about C and then reversing gives A♭ G B♭ C. Reversing first, to E F D C, and then inverting about its new first note E gives E E♭ F♯ G♯: the same shape, but transposed up 8 semitones. Fixing the axis, as row theory does, restores the group.

Adding transpositions: $T_k$ and $I_a$ generate the infinite dihedral group (on pitch classes mod 12, the dihedral group of order 24), and $R$ commutes with all of them. On a twelve-tone row this gives the $24 \times 2 = 48$ row forms of Chapter 21.

Phrases and cadences in melody

Melodies breathe. They are made of phrases, units of a few bars that end in a point of rest. Listen to the "Ode to Joy" theme from Beethoven's Ninth Symphony, transposed to C. It has two phrases that begin identically. The first stops on a note that leaves you waiting; the second, changing only its last bar, comes home.

The difference is small on paper and large to the ear. The first ending hangs on the second degree of the scale, over the dominant chord. The second walks down E, D, C to the tonic. Ending a phrase is what a cadence does, and a melody can make most of a cadence by itself.

A phrase is a complete musical thought ending with a cadence. In much tonal music phrases are four bars long, but two, three, five and eight are all common, and composers lengthen and shorten them freely. A melodic cadence has several ingredients, which usually arrive together:

  • The final degree. Ending on degree 1 (the tonic) sounds closed. Ending on 2, 7 or 5, usually over the dominant chord, sounds open: a half cadence.
  • The approach. The strongest closes reach the tonic by step: 2 → 1 from above, or 7 → 1 from below, where 7 is the leading tone, a semitone under the tonic (Chapter 10).
  • Rhythm and meter. Cadences tend to land on a strong beat and on a longer note. In the Ode, both phrases end on a half note at the start of beat 3 after a dotted rhythm.
  • Harmony. The chords underneath confirm the ending: V for a half cadence, V → I for an authentic one (Chapter 10).

Two phrases related like this, a question ending open and an answer ending closed, form a period: the first phrase is the antecedent, the second the consequent. Because the two phrases start alike, this one is a parallel period. Periods and their larger relatives are discussed in Chapter 16.

Look at the last three notes of each phrase. The antecedent goes E → D and stops: scale degrees 3 → 2. The consequent goes E → D → C: 3 → 2 → 1. In Heinrich Schenker's theory of tonal structure (Der freie Satz, 1935), a descent to degree 1 by step is the backbone of a tonal piece, and a period like this one is the classic case of an interruption: the line descends 3–2, breaks off, starts again, and completes 3–2–1.

Golden melodies need their own sense of ending. In golden7 the tonic C can be approached from B, a semitone below (the same leading-tone step as in C major), or from C♯, a semitone above. Which golden7 degrees sound like a question and which like an answer is one of the open problems of golden function (Chapter 30, Chapter 31).

A melodic cadence, formally. Let a phrase end with degrees $(s_{n-2}, s_{n-1})$ in a major key, with the last note on a metrically strong position. Call the ending

  • closed if $s_{n-1} \equiv 0 \pmod 7$ and $s_{n-2} \in \{1, -1\} \pmod 7$ (a step from 2 or from 7);
  • open if $s_{n-1} \bmod 7 \in \{1, 4, 6\}$ (degrees 2, 5, 7, the members of the dominant triad).

This is a definition, not a theorem: it describes the common practice of tonal melody, and real music adds exceptions (a closed ending on degree 3 over I is weaker but common; folk tunes often close by a leap from 5). Its use is to make the period's logic explicit: an antecedent ends open and a consequent ends closed.

Golden7 arithmetic. In 36-ET sixth-steps, golden7 is $\{0, 3, 11, 14, 22, 25, 33\}$, with steps $(3, 8, 3, 8, 3, 8, 3)$. The degrees $(0, 2, 4, 5, 4, 2, 0)$ are therefore $(0, 11, 22, 25, 22, 11, 0)$, so the moves are $+11, +11, +3, -3, -11, -11$ sixth-steps. In C major the same degrees give $+4, +3, +2, -2, -3, -4$ semitones. Both have the contour $(+, +, +, -, -, -)$, as the invariance statement of the first section predicts.

Exercise 13.1 (listening). Play the contours example and, without looking, sketch each melody's shape as a line. Then hum a familiar song and describe its first phrase as a contour.

Answer

The four examples are arch (+ + + − − −), bowl (− − − + + +), rising (all +) and falling (all −). Most familiar song phrases are some variant of the arch: a rise to a peak and a descent, often with small reversals along the way.

Exercise 13.2. How many contours (up, down, same) can a five-note melody have? How many if no two consecutive notes repeat?

Answer

Four moves with three choices each: $3^4 = 81$. Without repetitions each move is up or down: $2^4 = 16$.

Exercise 13.3. Write the diatonic inversion about C of the motif C E D G (in C major), and its real (chromatic) inversion. Then write the retrograde of the original.

Answer

In degrees the motif is 0, 2, 1, 4, so the diatonic inversion is 0, −2, −1, −4: C A B F (the A, B and F below the C). In semitones it is 0, 4, 2, 7, so the real inversion is 0, −4, −2, −7: C A♭ B♭ F. The retrograde is G D E C.

Exercise 13.4 (proof). Show that transposition and retrograde commute, but transposition and inversion do not: $I_a T_k = T_{-k} I_a$.

Answer

$T_k$ changes only pitch and $R$ only time, so they commute. For inversion: $I_a(T_k(p)) = 2a - (p + k) = (2a - p) - k = T_{-k}(I_a(p))$. So $I_a T_k = T_{-k} I_a$, which is the defining relation of a dihedral group.

Exercise 13.5. Count the steps, skips, leaps and repeated notes in the first phrase of the Ode (E E F G | G F E D | C C D E | E D D). Which degree does the phrase end on, and what kind of cadence is it?

Answer

15 notes, 14 moves: E–E repeat, E–F step, F–G step, G–G repeat, G–F, F–E, E–D, D–C steps, C–C repeat, C–D, D–E steps, E–E repeat, E–D step, D–D repeat. That is 9 steps, 5 repeats and no skips or leaps. It ends on D, degree 2, over V: a half cadence.
