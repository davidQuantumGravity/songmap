Chapter 5 cut the chain of fifths at seven notes and at twelve. Why those numbers? Why not eight, or nine? This chapter answers that question with three pieces of mathematics: a theorem about points on a circle, a family of fractions arranged in a tree, and the idea of a well-formed scale. Together they show that the pentatonic, diatonic and chromatic scales are not three separate inventions. They are three moments of one process, and the same process, run with a different generator, produces the golden scales of Part VI.

The three-gap theorem

Build a scale one fifth at a time, folding every new note into one octave, and play it after each addition. With three notes the scale has two kinds of step, a big leap and a smaller one. With four, an odd step appears that is neither. With five the scale settles down again: the pentatonic, with just two kinds of step. Six notes bring an odd one back, seven settle again into the major scale, and eight unsettle it once more.

However many fifths you stack, there are never more than three different step sizes. That is a theorem, and it is true for any generator at all, not just the fifth.

Think of the octave as a circle, one turn around being 1200 cents. Each fifth moves you 701.96 c around it. After $n$ notes the circle is cut into $n$ arcs, the steps of the scale. Here are the step sizes and how many of each there are, for the first few values of $n$:

Two features stand out. There are never more than three sizes. And whenever there are three, the largest is the sum of the other two: at $n = 4$, $203.91 + 294.13 = 498.04$; at $n = 6$, $90.22 + 203.91 = 294.13$; at $n = 9$, $90.22 + 113.69 = 203.91$. Between $n = 7$ and $n = 12$ you can watch the whole tones (203.91 c) being split, one per new note, into an apotome (113.69 c) and a limma (90.22 c) until none is left.

The step sizes do not depend on where the chain starts. Starting from C instead of F rotates the scale but leaves the list of steps the same.

Theorem (three-gap theorem). Let $\alpha$ be a real number and $n \ge 1$. The $n$ points $\{k\alpha\}$, $k = 0, 1, \dots, n-1$, on a circle of circumference 1 (here $\{\cdot\}$ is the fractional part) divide it into $n$ arcs of at most three different lengths. If there are three, the largest is the sum of the other two.

The theorem was conjectured by Hugo Steinhaus and proved in the late 1950s by Vera Sós, Stanisław Świerczkowski and János Surányi.

Proof sketch. Write $x_k = \{k\alpha\}$ and assume $\alpha$ is irrational (so the points are distinct). Among $x_1, \dots, x_{n-1}$, let $x_i$ be the point closest to 0 going counterclockwise, at distance $a = x_i$, and $x_j$ the point closest to 0 going clockwise, at distance $b = 1 - x_j$. Now look at the arc that starts at $x_k$ and goes counterclockwise.

  • If $k + i \le n - 1$, the point $x_{k+i} = x_k + a$ exists, and no point lies strictly between: such a point $x_m$ would give an index difference $|m - k|$ or $|k + i - m|$ below $n$ whose point lies closer to 0 than $a$. The arc has length $a$.
  • Otherwise, if $k - j \ge 0$, the point $x_{k-j} = x_k + b$ exists, and by the same argument the arc has length $b$.
  • Otherwise ($n - i \le k \le j - 1$) the next point is $x_{k+i-j} = x_k + a + b$, and the arc has length $a + b$.

So every arc is $a$, $b$ or $a + b$. ∎ (Full proof in Appendix D.)

Counting. The proof also counts the arcs: $n - i$ of length $a$, $n - j$ of length $b$, and $i + j - n$ of length $a + b$. (One can show $i + j \ge n$.) Check with fifths at $n = 9$: the closest returns are $x_7$ (an apotome above 0, $a$ = 113.69 c) and $x_5$ (a limma below, $b$ = 90.22 c). The formula gives $9 - 7 = 2$ apotomes, $9 - 5 = 4$ limmas and $7 + 5 - 9 = 3$ whole tones, as in the table.

Corollary. The scale has exactly two step sizes if and only if $n = i + j$.

Moments of symmetry

The sizes 5, 7 and 12 are special moments in the growth of the chain: the moments when the steps come in only two sizes. Such a scale has a remarkable evenness. Not only its steps but every interval comes in just two sizes. In the major scale, for example, every third you can form from the scale's own notes is either a major third or a minor third, never anything else.

The tuning theorist Erv Wilson called these special sizes moments of symmetry (in the 1970s). Music theorists also call them well-formed scales, the term of Norman Carey and David Clampitt (1989).

Definition. A moment-of-symmetry scale (MOS), or well-formed scale, is a scale made of $n$ consecutive notes of a chain of one generator, folded into the octave, that has exactly two step sizes. Call the larger step $L$ and the smaller $s$. If there are $a$ large and $b$ small steps, the scale is described as $a$L $b$s.

This is called Myhill's property. For chains of one generator it goes hand in hand with having two step sizes: a scale has one exactly when it has the other. Norman Carey and David Clampitt proved this equivalence in general (1989), and we checked it by computation for the chain of fifths and for the golden chain, for every size below 320.

The generic fourth and fifth are special: they are the generator itself (a fifth up is a fourth down). Six of the seven fifths are pure, and one, B–F, is smaller by an apotome: 701.96 − 113.69 = 588.27 c, the diminished fifth. That single odd fifth is the scale's tritone, and it is a miniature version of the wolf of Chapter 5.

Proposition (the odd generator). In the scale of $n$ consecutive chain notes $x_0, \dots, x_{n-1}$ with generator $g$ (in cents), the $n - 1$ pairs $x_k \to x_{k+1}$ are exactly $g$ apart (folded). The remaining pair, $x_{n-1} \to x_0$, differs from $g$ by the leftover $\varepsilon_n = n g - 1200\,m$, where $m$ is the nearest whole number of octaves: it is $g - \varepsilon_n$.

Proof. Going from $x_{n-1}$ up by $g$ lands on chain position $n$, which is $\varepsilon_n$ away from position 0. ∎

For the diatonic scale $\varepsilon_7 = 7 \times 701.955 - 4800 = 113.69$ c (the apotome), so the odd fifth is 588.27 c. For the chromatic scale $\varepsilon_{12} = 23.46$ c (the comma), and the odd fifth is the wolf, 678.49 c. For the pentatonic $\varepsilon_5 = -90.22$ c, so the odd interval is $701.96 + 90.22 = 792.18$ c, the minor sixth E–C standing in for a fifth.

MOS and Myhill (computed). For $g$ = the fifth and for $g$ = the golden sixth, and every $n$ from 2 to 319, the following were computed directly: (i) the step sizes; (ii) the number of specific sizes of each generic interval. In every case the scale has two step sizes if and only if every generic interval (other than the octave) has exactly two specific sizes.

The Stern–Brocot tree

After twelve, the next well-formed size of the chain of fifths is seventeen. In the 17-note Pythagorean scale each black key of the piano splits into two notes, a sharp and a flat, a comma apart. The steps are now limmas and commas: a scale of tiny and very tiny steps.

Where do the sizes 5, 7, 12, 17 come from? There is a single structure, a tree of fractions, that lists them all, and that also contains the near-misses of the last chapter.

The mediant of two fractions $a/b$ and $c/d$ is $(a + c)/(b + d)$: add the tops and add the bottoms. It always lies between the two. The Stern–Brocot tree is built from mediants. Start with $0/1$ and $1/1$. Their mediant $1/2$ is the root. Each new fraction has two children: the mediant with its nearest neighbor on the left, and the mediant with its nearest neighbor on the right. Every fraction between 0 and 1 appears exactly once, in lowest terms.

To find a number in the tree, start at the root and at each fraction go left if the number is smaller, right if it is larger. For the fifth, $x = 0.58496\ldots$:

The denominators along the path are 2, 3, 5, 7, 12, 17, 29, 41, 53, and those are exactly the well-formed sizes of the chain of fifths. Each fraction $m/n$ on the path has a musical meaning: in the $n$-note well-formed scale, the fifth spans $m$ of the $n$ steps. In the pentatonic a fifth is 3 of 5 steps (C to G is C–D–E–G); in the diatonic, 4 of 7 (C–D–E–F–G); in the chromatic, 7 of 12.

The path also contains every convergent of Chapter 5's continued fraction (1/2, 3/5, 7/12, 24/41, 31/53) plus the fractions in between (2/3, 4/7, 10/17, 17/29). The runs of lefts and rights follow the continued fraction: one right, two lefts, two rights, three lefts, and so on.

Two different sevens. "Twelve fifths make about seven octaves" and "the major scale has seven notes" use the number 7 in two different ways.

  • The 7 in "twelve fifths ≈ seven octaves" is the numerator of 7/12: a fifth is 7 of the 12 chromatic steps.
  • The 7 notes of the diatonic scale are a denominator: 4/7, the step before 7/12 on the path. A fifth is 4 of the 7 diatonic steps.

The two coincide because 7/12 is the mediant of 3/5 and 4/7, so its numerator is $3 + 4 = 7$. They are arithmetically linked, but neither causes the other. The coincidence does not generalize: 24/41 is on the path, but 24 notes do not make a well-formed scale (they have three step sizes).

Facts about the tree (Stern 1858, Brocot 1861). If $a/b < c/d$ are neighbors at some stage of the construction, then $bc - ad = 1$, and their mediant is the fraction with the smallest denominator strictly between them. Every reduced fraction in $(0, 1)$ appears exactly once.

Theorem (well-formed sizes). Let $x \in (0,1)$ be irrational. The scale of $n$ consecutive points of the chain $\{kx\}$ has exactly two step sizes if and only if $n$ is the denominator of a fraction on the Stern–Brocot path of $x$ (the root $1/2$ included).

Proof sketch. Use the notation of the three-gap proof. The closest return from above, $x_i = \{ix\}$, means $ix$ is just above some integer $p$: the fraction $p/i$ is the best lower approximation of $x$ with denominator below $n$. Likewise the closest return from below gives the best upper approximation $q/j$. Best one-sided approximations with bounded denominators are neighbors in the tree: $p/i < x < q/j$ with $qi - pj = 1$. By the corollary of the three-gap theorem the scale has two sizes exactly when $n = i + j$, the denominator of their mediant $(p + q)/(i + j)$, which is the next fraction on the path. ∎

For example, at $n = 7$ the closest returns are $x_2$ ($2x = 1.170$, just above 1, so the lower fraction is 1/2) and $x_5$ ($5x = 2.925$, just below 3, so the upper fraction is 3/5). Indeed $3 \cdot 2 - 1 \cdot 5 = 1$ and $2 + 5 = 7$. At $n = 12$ the returns are $x_7$ (lower 4/7) and $x_5$ (upper 3/5), and $7 + 5 = 12$.

Computed. For the fifth, the sizes $n < 320$ with two step sizes are 2, 3, 5, 7, 12, 17, 29, 41, 53, 94, 147, 200, 253, 306. The five after 53 are the denominators of $(24 + 31t)/(41 + 53t)$ for $t = 1, \dots, 5$: the intermediate fractions 55/94, 86/147, 117/200 and 148/253, and then ($t = 5$) the convergent 179/306 itself. They come from the partial quotient 5 in the continued fraction.

The golden sixth's path through the tree is 1/2, 2/3, 3/4, 5/7, 7/10, 9/13, 16/23, 25/36, so the well-formed golden scales have 2, 3, 4, 7, 10, 13, 23 and 36 notes. The golden "two sevens" puzzle has the same answer: 13 golden sixths make about 9 octaves, but 9 is not a well-formed size. Nine golden sixths give three step sizes (99.3, 168.4 and 267.6 c). The parents of 9/13 are 2/3 and 7/10, so golden13's well-formed sub-scales are golden10, golden7 and golden4 (Chapter 23).

Pentatonic, diatonic, chromatic

Now listen to the three famous moments one after another: five notes, seven notes, twelve notes. The pentatonic has no half steps at all; pentatonic scales are found in many of the world's musical traditions. The diatonic adds two half steps, and with them the pull of E toward F and B toward C. The chromatic fills in every gap.

Each scale in the family comes from the previous one by splitting every large step in two:

  • Pentatonic → diatonic. The pentatonic's large step, the minor third 294.13 c (E–G and A–C), splits into a whole tone and a limma: $294.13 = 203.91 + 90.22$. The new notes are F (in E–G) and B (in A–C).
  • Diatonic → chromatic. Each whole tone splits into an apotome and a limma: $203.91 = 113.69 + 90.22$. The new notes are the five black keys.
  • Chromatic → 17. Each apotome splits into a limma and a comma: $113.69 = 90.22 + 23.46$.

The figures show the two scales on the twelve-position circle, with the chain of fifths drawn through their notes. A pleasing complement: the seven white keys are the chain segment F to B, and the five black keys are the next five links, F♯ C♯ G♯ D♯ A♯. So the black keys form a pentatonic scale, and every 12-note chain splits into a 7-note and a 5-note chain segment.

The golden chain has its own family: golden4, golden7, golden10, golden13. golden7, C C♯ vE vF ^G ^A♭ B, is the golden diatonic. Its steps are 3, 8, 3, 8, 3, 8, 3 sixth-steps in 36-ET: the golden apotome (99.27 c) and a large step of 267.6 c, which is two golden apotomes plus a golden limma. In golden13 that large step has been split into golden apotomes and limmas (Chapter 23).

Proposition (splitting). Let an MOS of $n$ notes have $a$ large steps $L$ and $b$ small steps $s$. The next well-formed size on the path is $n + a$: each large step is split into $s$ and $L - s$, giving $a + b$ steps of size $s$ and $a$ steps of size $L - s$. Which of the two is now the larger depends on whether $L - s$ is more or less than $s$.

Complements. In any closed 12-note cycle of fifths, the complement of 7 consecutive positions is 5 consecutive positions. So the complement of a diatonic set in 12-ET is a pentatonic set, and vice versa.

Modes

A scale is a set of notes; a mode is that set heard from a particular home note. Play the white keys from C to C and you hear major. Play the same white keys from D to D, or from E to E, and the mood changes completely, because the pattern of large and small steps now starts in a different place. Here are all seven modes, each moved to start on C so that only the pattern changes. Each one lowers exactly one note of the one before.

The effect is easiest to hear with a melody. Here one tune, written only as scale degrees, is played in four modes on C.

The diatonic step pattern is 5L 2s arranged as L L s L L L s (from C). Starting it on each of its seven notes gives the seven modes. Ordered by the home note's place on the chain of fifths, F C G D A E B:

Moving down the table, the home note moves one step up the chain of fifths, and exactly one note drops by a semitone. The first step cancels Lydian's F♯; after that the notes drop in the order B, E, A, D, G, which is the order of flats in key signatures (Chapter 8). In Lydian every degree takes the higher of its two diatonic forms; in Locrian every degree takes the lower one. Musicians often describe this ordering as bright to dark. Locrian is the only mode without a perfect fifth above its tonic (C–G♭ is the odd generator of the last section), and it is rarely used as a home mode.

The pentatonic has five modes in the same way. Two of them have everyday names: C D E G A is the major pentatonic, and its rotation A C D E G the minor pentatonic.

Names. The mode names are borrowed from ancient Greek, but the way they are attached to the white-key scales today comes from medieval and Renaissance theory, not from ancient Greek practice. Medieval theory recognized eight church modes on D, E, F and G. Heinrich Glarean's Dodecachordon (1547) added modes on A and C, named Aeolian and Ionian.

golden7 has seven modes too, one on each of its notes. Since golden7 is built the same way (seven consecutive links of a chain), its modes are ordered along the golden chain in exactly the same manner. Consecutive modes differ in one note, moved by seven golden sixths folded into the octave: 168.37 c, the difference of golden7's two steps (267.64 − 99.27 c), just as the diatonic modes differ by the apotome, 203.91 − 90.22 c (Chapter 23).

Proposition (modes along the chain). Let a scale be the chain positions $c, c+1, \dots, c+6$ (relative to the tonic at position 0), so the Lydian mode is $c = 0$ and Locrian is $c = -6$. Passing from $c$ to $c - 1$ removes position $c + 6$ and adds position $c - 1$. These are seven links apart: the same letter, lowered by an apotome (a semitone in 12-ET). So consecutive modes differ in exactly one note, lowered by a chromatic semitone.

Proposition (seven distinct modes). The seven rotations of L L s L L L s are all different. A rotation that equaled the original would make the pattern periodic with a period dividing 7, hence all steps equal, which is false. The same argument shows that an $a$L $b$s pattern with $a + b$ prime has $a + b$ distinct modes.

Fifths above the tonic. Mode $c$ contains the fifth above its tonic (position 1) exactly when $c + 6 \ge 1$, that is, for every mode except Locrian ($c = -6$).

Exercise 6.1. Using the counting formula of the three-gap theorem, find the steps of the 10-note scale of the chain of fifths.

Answer

For $n = 10$ the closest returns are still $x_7$ (apotome, 113.69 c) and $x_5$ (limma, 90.22 c), so $i = 7$, $j = 5$. There are $10 - 7 = 3$ apotomes, $10 - 5 = 5$ limmas and $7 + 5 - 10 = 2$ whole tones, matching the table.

Exercise 6.2. Compute the two children of 7/12 in the Stern–Brocot tree, and say which is on the path of the fifth.

Answer

The neighbors of 7/12 when it is created are 4/7 and 3/5. Its children are $(4 + 7)/(7 + 12) = 11/19$ and $(7 + 3)/(12 + 5) = 10/17$. Since $7/12 = 0.5833 < x$, the path goes right, to 10/17. (11/19 is on the path of the meantone fifth: Chapter 7.)

Exercise 6.3. Name the two sizes of diatonic sixth in the Pythagorean C major scale and say how many of each occur.

Answer

Minor sixths of 792.18 c (E–C, A–F, B–G: three) and major sixths of 905.87 c (C–A, D–B, F–D, G–E: four). They are the inversions of the four minor and three major thirds.

Exercise 6.4. (Listening.) In the "one tune, four modes" example, which scale degrees change between the Ionian and the Mixolydian versions? Between Ionian and Dorian?

Answer

The tune uses degrees 1, 2, 3, 5 and 6 and the seventh below the tonic, but never the fourth. Ionian to Mixolydian: only the seventh changes (B to B♭), heard once, near the end. Ionian to Dorian: the third (E to E♭) and the seventh (B to B♭); the sixth, A, is the same in both.

Exercise 6.5. Show that the diatonic scale contains exactly one tritone.

Answer

A tritone in the diatonic is a generic fourth or fifth of the odd size. By the proposition on the odd generator, exactly one of the seven chain pairs (B → F, folded) is not a pure fifth: it is 588.27 c, the diminished fifth B–F, whose inversion F–B is the augmented fourth. So there is one tritone pair, {F, B}.
