Hear it, Understand it, Derive it

This book is written for two kinds of reader at once: musicians who may not have studied much mathematics, and mathematically minded readers who may not have studied much music. Neither is assumed. Every term is defined when it first appears, every symbol is explained, and nothing is skipped in the proofs. To make that possible without burying anyone, almost every section is written in three layers, always in the same order and always marked by a colored bar and a label.

  • Hear it is about intuition and listening. It uses plain language, numbers in words, and an audio example whenever there is something to hear. It has no formulas.
  • Understand it gives the concepts, the notation, worked examples and tables, with light mathematics: fractions, and logarithms with an explanation.
  • Derive it gives the definitions, theorems, proofs or proof sketches, exact values and small algorithms. Proofs too long for the page are completed in Appendix D.

Here are the three layers in miniature, on one idea from later in the book.

Between the bright major third and the darker minor third there is room for another third. The golden third sits about two thirds of the way from minor to major. Listen to a major third, a minor third, and then the golden third, each played as two notes and then together.

The golden third is the frequency ratio $2/\varphi \approx 1.236$, where $\varphi \approx 1.618$ is the golden ratio. Measured in cents (hundredths of an equal-tempered semitone), it is 366.91 c, between the minor third (300 c) and the major third (400 c). Two thirds major plus one third minor is $\tfrac23 \cdot 400 + \tfrac13 \cdot 300 \approx 366.67$ c, only 0.24 c away.

Computation. The size in cents of a ratio $r$ is $1200\log_2 r$. For $r = 2/\varphi$ this is $1200\,(1 - \log_2\varphi) \approx 1200\,(1 - 0.694242) \approx 366.91$. Since $\tfrac23\cdot400+\tfrac13\cdot300 = 1100/3 \approx 366.67$, the difference is about 0.24 c.

Theorems, measurements and hypotheses

The book is careful about what kind of claim it is making, and labels it.

  • A theorem is proved, or checked exhaustively by computer over every case. Example: in 36-ET a golden triad has exactly one note on each of the three layers.
  • A measurement depends on a model: of roughness, of harmonic entropy, of the tone color or the register. Example: with harmonic tones the golden triad measures rougher than major, minor and augmented triads but smoother than diminished.
  • A conjecture or hypothesis is an idea that has not been established. Example: that golden harmony can encourage a blissful state (Chapter 34). It is never presented as fact.

Which kind of claim is each of these? (a) Twelve perfect fifths overshoot seven octaves by 23.46 c. (b) With harmonic tones, the diminished triad is rougher than the golden triad. (c) Golden beats can calm a listener.

Answer

(a) A theorem: it is exact arithmetic, $1200\log_2\big((3/2)^{12}/2^7\big) \approx 23.46$. (b) A measurement: it depends on the roughness model and on the tone. (c) A hypothesis: it would need listening experiments to test.

Reading paths

The book is built in nine parts plus appendices. Parts I–V develop Western theory from first principles, from the physics of sound through tuning, harmony, voice leading and form, to twelve-tone and modern music. Parts VI–VII build the golden tonal system as a mirror of that development, Part VIII turns to the mind and to history, and Part IX shows how the music in the book was made. The chapters are in an order that works straight through, but you do not have to read them that way.

The musician's path

Read with Hear it and Understand it switched on, and Derive it off.

  1. Part I quickly, for the vocabulary of sound: cents, the harmonic series, roughness.
  2. Part II for tuning: Pythagorean tuning, temperament, the circle of fifths.
  3. Parts III–IV as a theory course, then dip into Part V where you like.
  4. Then the golden system: Chapter 23 to Chapter 25, followed by System 1, golden cadences and the golden home.
  5. If you want to write golden music yourself, finish with Chapter 36.

The mathematician's path

Leave all three layers on and lean on Derive it. Appendix A reviews logarithms, modular arithmetic, groups, continued fractions and Fourier series.

  1. Hearing in ratios and Fourier's idea.
  2. Continued fractions and why twelve, the three-gap theorem and the Stern–Brocot tree.
  3. Pitch classes and $\mathbb{Z}_{12}$, the geometry of voice leading, subgroups and cosets, pitch-class set theory.
  4. The golden side: the golden chain, why 36, the layer theorem, the four-path theorem, the half-step theorem, $\mathbb{Z}_{36}$.
  5. The complete proofs in Appendix D.

Notation at a glance

This is a one-page summary. Every item is introduced properly in the chapter where it first matters, and Appendix B is the complete reference.

Ordinary notes are named with letters, sharps and flats. The golden system needs notes between the keys of the piano, so it adds two small marks: ^ raises a note by a third of a semitone and v lowers it by the same amount. Listen to a C major chord, and then to the same three letters with the E bent down and the G bent up. That second chord is the golden triad.

Three of these small steps make one semitone. Here is the ladder from C up to C♯ and back down, and then the plain semitone in a single jump.

Notes, frequencies and cents

  • Note names carry an octave number: C4 is middle C, which is MIDI note 60, and A4 = 440 Hz. C3 is an octave lower, C5 an octave higher. In prose the book writes ♯ and ♭; in code and SongMap files, # and b.
  • Ratios. An interval is a frequency ratio: the octave 2/1, the perfect fifth 3/2, the major third 5/4 in just intonation, the golden sixth φ.
  • Cents. Intervals are also measured in cents: 1200 to the octave, 100 to the equal-tempered semitone. The pure fifth 3/2 is 701.96 c; the just major third 5/4 is 386.31 c. Values are given to two decimals.

36-ET: sixth-steps, ups and downs

36-ET divides the octave into 36 equal sixth-steps of 33.33 c, a third of a semitone (a sixth of a whole tone, hence the name). A prefix ^ ("up") raises a note one sixth-step and v ("down") lowers it one. In the golden chapters pitch classes are numbered in sixth-steps from C = 0:

Step0123456…11…22…33

Every third step is an ordinary 12-ET note, so 36-ET contains 12-ET exactly. Steps 0, 3, 6, … form the ordinary layer; steps 1, 4, 7, … the ^ layer; steps 2, 5, 8, … the v layer. A note just below a 12-ET note is written with v on that note (step 2 is vC♯, not ^^C). In Western chapters pitch classes are numbered in semitones, C = 0 to B = 11.

In the piano rolls, rows are sixth-steps and notes are shaded by layer, so a golden chord shows one note on each of the three layers.

Step notation. A chord's shape is written as the list of its notes in steps above the root: semitones in the Western chapters, so a major triad is (0, 4, 7), and sixth-steps in the golden chapters, where the same triad is (0, 12, 21) and the golden triad is (0, 11, 22).

Roman numerals

Chords in a key are numbered by scale degree, with upper case for major and lower case for minor: I ii iii IV V vi vii° in a major key, and ♭VI, ♭III and so on for chromatic chords. The golden chapters add the sixth-step marks and the g suffix. vIg is the golden triad a sixth-step below the tonic (vCg in C major), vIVg is vFg, v♭VIg is vA♭g, and vIg/V is vCg over the dominant G in the bass.

Formulas. MIDI note $m$ has frequency $f = 440\cdot 2^{(m-69)/12}$ Hz, so C4 ($m = 60$) is 261.63 Hz. A ratio $r$ measures $1200\log_2 r$ cents. In 36-ET, step $k$ above a reference has ratio $2^{k/36}$; its layer is $k \bmod 3$ (0 ordinary, 1 up, 2 down), and its 12-ET neighbour is $\lfloor (k+1)/3 \rfloor$ semitones up, so the 36 pitch classes form the cyclic group $\mathbb{Z}_{36}$ with $\mathbb{Z}_{12}$ inside it as the multiples of 3. The golden intervals sit within a cent of the grid: the golden third $2/\varphi$ = 366.91 c against 11 steps = 366.67 c, the golden fifth $4/\varphi^2$ = 733.82 c against 22 steps = 733.33 c, and the golden seventh $8/\varphi^3$ = 1100.73 c against 33 steps = 1100 c, exactly the 12-ET major seventh.

About the audio examples

Every example in this book was synthesized from scratch, note by note, and none is a recording. The examples are short, usually five to forty seconds, and each makes one point. The caption under each player tells you what to listen for. The instruments are deliberately plain and steady, so what you hear is the tuning itself and not an effect. Listen at a moderate volume, ideally on headphones or good speakers: the differences between sixth-steps are small, and small laptop speakers lose the bass.

Most examples are tuned on the 12-ET or 36-ET grid. A few, where exact ratios are the point, are tuned exactly. For golden chords the two are very close. Here is the golden triad in 36-ET and then in exact golden ratios.

SongMap

The musical examples are written as short text files in SongMap, a format and synthesis engine made for this project (Chapter 36). A song file names the tempo, key, tuning (12edo or 36edo), instruments and a harmony string such as C:1 ~:0.5 ~:0.5 F:1. The engine voice-leads the chords in four parts in sixth-steps, and a placeholder ~ (System 1, the strict path) or ~g (System 2, the natural path) asks it to insert the passing chords the voice leading picks out. Each example's source sits in the book's repository under textbook/audio/<chapter>/, so any example can be read, changed and rendered again.

Clean, stable instruments

The instruments are additive: each tone is a sum of sine waves (partials) at fixed, exact frequencies, with no vibrato, drift, detuning, noise, filtering or distortion, and with smooth attack and release envelopes (Chapter 37). So any beating you hear comes from the intervals, not from the instrument. There are two families of tone color:

  • harmonic tones, with partials at 1, 2, 3, … times the fundamental, like most acoustic instruments; for example the clean organ, 8 harmonics with amplitudes falling as $1/k^{1.2}$;
  • golden-spectrum tones, with partials at $2^m\varphi^n$ times the fundamental (the "glass" bell and the golden pad), on which golden chords measure as the smoothest chords (Chapter 26).

36-ET and exact references

Anything on the 12-ET or 36-ET grid is rendered by SongMap. When the exact ratio is itself the point, as with the harmonic series, beats, just intonation, exact powers of φ or combination tones, the example is a short Python program that computes the frequencies directly. On middle C the 36-ET golden triad differs from the exact one by about 0.05 Hz on the third and 0.11 Hz on the fifth.

Mastering without compression

Every song uses the clean master: the mix is only scaled so that its loudest peak sits at −1 dBFS. There is no equalization, compression, limiting or saturation, all of which would add or remove partials and blur the very differences the examples are about. The site then encodes each example as an MP3. One consequence is that examples are not loudness-matched; a quiet pure sine and a full chord both peak at the same level. Longer finished pieces from the golden audio suite (for example the Well-Tempered Golden Clavier) are embedded in the same way, and Appendix G indexes everything.

Precision of the grid. With $C_4 = 440\cdot 2^{-9/12} \approx 261.63$ Hz, the exact golden third is $C_4\cdot 2/\varphi \approx 323.387$ Hz and the 36-ET one is $C_4\cdot 2^{11/36} \approx 323.342$ Hz, a difference of 0.045 Hz (0.24 c). The exact golden fifth is $C_4\cdot 4/\varphi^2 \approx 399.728$ Hz against $C_4 \cdot 2^{22/36} \approx 399.616$ Hz, a difference of 0.112 Hz (0.49 c). The golden seventh $8/\varphi^3$ is 1100.73 c, which 36-ET renders as 33 steps, exactly the 12-ET major seventh, 0.73 c flat of the exact value.

Listen to the 36-ET versus exact example again. If both golden triads were sounded together, the two thirds would beat at about 0.045 Hz. How many seconds is one beat cycle, and could you hear it in a four-second chord?

Answer

One cycle takes $1/0.045 \approx 22$ seconds. A four-second chord covers less than a fifth of a cycle, so no beat is audible: for listening purposes the two tunings are the same.
