Species counterpoint

Counterpoint is the art of combining independent melodies. Where harmony asks "what chord is this?", counterpoint asks "how do these lines move against each other?" The traditional way to learn it starts very small: one given melody, moving in long equal notes, and one new line written against it, note against note. Listen to a slow, plain melody, and then to the same melody with a second line above it.

The new line is a melody in its own right: it rises to a single high point, F, descends by step to B, and climbs back through the leading tone C♯ to the final D. Against the lower line it alternates open sounds (fifths and octaves) with warmer ones (thirds and sixths), and it mostly moves in the opposite direction. At the end the two lines move apart in contrary motion: the sixth E–C♯ opens to the octave D–D.

The method is named for Johann Joseph Fux's Gradus ad Parnassum ("Steps to Parnassus", 1725), a Latin treatise written as a dialogue between a master, Aloysius, and a pupil, Josephus. Fux presented the master as standing for Palestrina, whose sixteenth-century style was the model. The book was used for generations: Haydn studied from it, and Beethoven's counterpoint lessons followed its method. It teaches counterpoint in five species, each adding one kind of rhythm against a cantus firmus ("fixed song"), a plain melody in whole notes:

First species uses only consonances: unisons, fifths, octaves, thirds and sixths (and their compounds). The fourth counts as a dissonance in two-voice writing. Fux classifies the consonances as perfect (unison, fifth, octave) and imperfect (thirds, sixths), and gives four rules of motion:

  1. From a perfect consonance to a perfect consonance, move by contrary or oblique motion.
  2. From a perfect consonance to an imperfect one, any motion is allowed.
  3. From an imperfect consonance to a perfect one, move by contrary or oblique motion.
  4. From an imperfect consonance to an imperfect one, any motion is allowed.

Rules 1 and 3 together forbid both parallel and hidden fifths and octaves in two voices, for the perceptual reasons of Chapter 14. Other conventions: begin and end on a perfect consonance; avoid unisons in the middle; prefer steps, and follow a leap by a step in the other direction; let the line have one climax; do not stay in parallel thirds or sixths too long. At the close, the cantus descends 2 → 1 and the counterpoint rises 7 → 8, a major sixth opening to an octave. In the D mode the seventh degree is C, so it is raised to C♯ to make a leading tone, a practice called musica ficta.

Fourth species is where counterpoint begins to sound like harmony with tension in it. The upper voice enters on the weak half of the bar and is held across the bar line while the lower voice moves. On the downbeat the held note becomes a dissonance, the suspension, and it then resolves down by step to a consonance. Each resolution is itself the preparation of the next suspension, so the pattern chains.

This is the same suspension that Chapter 12 describes in harmony: preparation (consonant), suspension (dissonant, on the strong beat), resolution (down by step).

Counterpoint as constraint satisfaction. First species can be stated as a finite search. Given a cantus $c_0, \dots, c_{n-1}$, find $p_0, \dots, p_{n-1}$ with

  • $(p_i - c_i) \bmod 12 \in \{0, 3, 4, 7, 8, 9\}$ (consonance), $p_i \ge c_i$ (counterpoint above);
  • $(p_0 - c_0) \bmod 12 \in \{0, 7\}$, and $p_{n-2} - c_{n-2} = 9$, $p_{n-1} - c_{n-1} = 12$ (the cadence);
  • if $(p_i - c_i) \bmod 12 \in \{0, 7\}$, then $p_i - p_{i-1}$ and $c_i - c_{i-1}$ are not both nonzero with the same sign (Fux's rules 1 and 3).

plus melodic conditions. With the conditions used for the example (white notes from D4 to A5 plus the cadential C♯, intervals up to a tenth, no unisons except at the ends, no repeated notes, melodic leaps no larger than a fifth except the octave, no tritone or seventh leaps, no two successive leaps in the same direction, and no more than three parallel thirds or sixths in a row), a computer search finds 1208 first-species counterpoints above this cantus. The count depends entirely on the rule set chosen; the point is that the rules leave a large but finite space of solutions, and the art lies in choosing among them. The example was chosen from the search for having a single climax, one leap, and the most contrary motion.

The suspension, exactly. Over a bass descending by step, $b_k = b_0 - (\text{one scale step}) \cdot k$, the upper voice sounds $u_k$ from the middle of bar $k$ to the middle of bar $k+1$, with $u_k$ a sixth above $b_k$. On the downbeat of bar $k + 1$, the bass has dropped a step but $u_k$ is held, so the interval becomes a seventh; then $u_{k+1}$, a step lower, restores the sixth above $b_{k+1}$. In the example the sevenths are m7, m7, m7, M7, m7, m7 and the sixths m6, m6, M6, M6, m6, M6, as the diatonic scale dictates.

Imitation and canon

One voice sings a phrase, and another voice sings the same phrase a moment later. That is imitation, the most natural way to make several voices sound related and yet independent. If the second voice imitates the whole of the first, all the way through, the result is a canon. The simplest canon is the round, a tune designed so that copies of it fit against themselves. Listen to a familiar one in four voices.

Each new entry seems to fill in the texture rather than clash with it. When all four voices sound, you hear one harmony, decorated four ways at once.

Terms: the voice that starts is the leader (Latin dux); the imitating voice is the follower (comes). A canon is described by two distances: the time interval (how long after the leader the follower enters) and the pitch interval (at the unison, the octave, the fifth, …). A canon may also transform the leader: a canon by inversion answers rises with falls, a canon by augmentation answers in longer notes, and a crab canon answers with the retrograde (Chapter 13).

Canon has a long history. "Sumer is icumen in", an English round from the thirteenth century, is one of the oldest surviving pieces of polyphony for many voices. J. S. Bach's Goldberg Variations (published 1741) place a canon at every third variation, at successively wider intervals from the unison to the ninth.

Why a round works. Cut "Frère Jacques" into its four two-bar segments: C D E C (twice); E F G (twice); G A G F E C (twice); C G C (twice). In a four-voice round, after the last entry, the four segments always sound together, in rotation. Each segment outlines the same chord, C major: every note that falls on beat 1 or beat 3 of a bar is C, E or G, and the other notes (D, F, A) are passing or neighbour tones between them. So any stacking of segments is a decorated C major chord.

Imitation does not need its voices to live in the same world. In Chapter 30 a phrase in C major is imitated in golden7: the follower keeps the scale degrees and the contour, but every interval is translated into golden ones.

Definition. A two-voice canon with time interval $\delta$ and transformation $g$ (a transposition $T_k$, an inversion, a time stretch) is a pair of voices with $B(t) = g(A)(t - \delta)$ for all $t \ge \delta$.

Round condition. Let a tune of length $L = n\delta$ be cut into segments $s_0, \dots, s_{n-1}$ of length $\delta$. In an $n$-voice round at the unison (or octave) with entries $\delta$ apart, once all voices have entered, the sounding segments at any time are $\{s_{j}, s_{j-1}, \dots, s_{j-n+1}\}$ (indices mod $n$), that is, all $n$ segments at once. So a tune works as an $n$-voice round if and only if all of its segments sound acceptable when superimposed, which in tonal practice means they all fit one harmony (or one chord progression of length $\delta$).

Check (exhaustive, by computer). For "Frère Jacques" with $\delta = 2$ bars and $n = 4$, at every beat 1 and beat 3 where all four voices sound, the set of sounding pitch classes is contained in $\{C, E, G\}$. Over the whole tune the only pitch classes used are C, D, E, F, G and A.

Fugue

A fugue is a piece built from imitation of one main theme, the subject. It begins with an exposition: the voices enter one by one with the subject, alternating between the tonic and the dominant, and each voice, once it has stated the subject, continues with counterpoint against the next entry. Listen to a short three-voice exposition written for this book: the middle voice states the subject alone, the top voice answers at the dominant, and finally the bass enters with the subject.

Listen for the second entry. It sounds like the subject, but its opening leap is a fourth (G up to C), not a fifth. Then listen for the alto during that entry: it is no longer the subject but a new line, the countersubject, mostly moving in eighth notes where the answer moves in quarters, and in quarters where it moves in eighths.

The parts of a fugue:

ElementMeaning

In the example the subject begins C → G, tonic up to dominant. A real answer would begin G → D, dominant up to the second degree, and pull the music away from C too abruptly. The tonal answer begins G → C instead, dominant up to tonic, and only then continues as an exact transposition: E D C B | C B A G F♯ G. One note is changed (the D of the real answer becomes C), and the answer still lands in G, with F♯ as its leading tone.

A fugue is less a fixed form than a procedure. After the exposition, composers alternate episodes and entries in different keys, and often finish with stretto, a pedal point and a final entry in the tonic. The supreme examples are J. S. Bach's: the 48 fugues of the Well-Tempered Clavier (Book I 1722, Book II 1742; see Chapter 7) and The Art of Fugue, left unfinished at his death in 1750.

Every vertical sonority in the example exposition was checked: on every beat the voices form consonances (thirds, sixths, fifths, octaves, and fourths only between upper voices), dissonances occur only on off-beat eighth notes moving by step, and no two voices move in parallel fifths or octaves.

Real and tonal answers in scale degrees. Write the subject in C-major degrees (0 = C):

$$S = (0, 4, 5, 4, 3, 2 \mid 3, 2, 1, 0, -1, 0).$$

The real answer adds 4 degrees, using the scale of G major, so the degree written 3 below (F in C major) is sung as F♯:

$$A_{\text{real}} = (4, 8, 9, 8, 7, 6 \mid 7, 6, 5, 4, 3^{\sharp}, 4) = \text{G D E D C B} \mid \text{C B A G F}\sharp\ \text{G}.$$

The tonal answer applies a mutation to the head: the subject's motion tonic → dominant ($0 \to 4$) is mapped to dominant → tonic ($4 \to 7$) instead of $4 \to 8$:

$$A_{\text{tonal}} = (4, 7, 9, 8, 7, 6 \mid 7, 6, 5, 4, 3^{\sharp}, 4).$$

In general the mutation exchanges the roles of degrees 0 and 4 near the start of the subject, so that the "tonic–dominant frame" of the subject becomes a "dominant–tonic frame" in the answer. The rest is a real transposition.

Invertible counterpoint. A countersubject is most useful if it works both above and below the subject. Inverting two voices at the octave turns an interval $x$ into $12 - x$ (mod 12): thirds become sixths and sixths thirds (fine), the octave becomes a unison (fine), but the fifth becomes a fourth, which is a dissonance against a bass. So a countersubject meant to be invertible at the octave treats fifths like dissonances. This is why Bach's countersubjects so often move in thirds and sixths against their subjects.

Call and response

Counterpoint combines voices at the same time. Call and response combines them in alternation: one voice or group sings or plays a phrase, and another answers. It is one of the most widespread ways of making music together, found in worship, work, dance and play on every inhabited continent. Chant: responsorial and antiphonal singing

In the Latin liturgical chant of the Western church (Gregorian chant), psalms were sung in two main ways. In responsorial singing a soloist (cantor) sings the verses and the choir or congregation answers with a refrain. In antiphonal singing two half-choirs alternate verses, and an antiphon, a short chant, frames the psalm. Each verse is sung to a psalm tone, a formula with an opening rise, a reciting note on which most of the words are chanted, a cadence at the half-verse and a cadence at the end. Chant is monophonic: everyone sings the same line. The example above imitates the shape of such a formula; it is not a historical psalm tone.

Alternation of groups grew into a polyphonic style in sixteenth-century Venice, where the basilica of St Mark's encouraged music for divided choirs placed apart (cori spezzati). Adrian Willaert worked there in the middle of the century, and Giovanni Gabrieli (c. 1555–1612) wrote large works in which choirs of voices and instruments answer each other across the space.

African and African American traditions

Call and response is a central organizing principle in many musical traditions of sub-Saharan Africa: a leader sings a line and a chorus answers, often with a fixed refrain while the leader varies and improvises. The form is participatory. The response is not an echo but the community's part in the music, and calls and responses frequently overlap. Through the African diaspora this practice shaped a great deal of music in the Americas: work songs, in which a leader's calls coordinated group labour; spirituals; the call and response between preacher and congregation and between lead singer and choir in African American churches and gospel music; the pregón (lead) and coro (chorus) of Cuban son; and the blues and jazz.

A related practice is lining out, in which a leader sings or speaks each line of a hymn and the congregation sings it back, slowly and with rich ornamentation. It was common in British and colonial American Protestant psalm singing, survives in Gaelic psalm singing in the Scottish Hebrides, and became a distinctive tradition in African American churches, where lined hymns are often called "Dr. Watts" hymns after the hymn writer Isaac Watts.

Blues and jazz

In the twelve-bar blues each four-bar line typically divides into a sung phrase of about two bars and an answer of about two bars, played by an instrument or by the singer's own guitar. Blues lyrics often follow an AAB pattern: a line, the line repeated (over the subdominant), and a different, answering line. Swing-era big bands built whole arrangements from call and response between sections, with brass riffs answered by saxophones. In small-group jazz, trading fours (or eights, or twos) is a common practice in which soloists, often a horn player and the drummer, alternate short solos within the chorus. Each player responds to what the other just played, so the exchange is a musical conversation in real time.

Alternation arithmetic. Let a chorus (one pass through the form) have $L$ bars and let two players trade phrases of $k$ bars, with $k$ dividing $L$. Each chorus then contains $m = L/k$ phrases. If $m$ is even, every chorus starts with the same player. If $m$ is odd, the starting player alternates from chorus to chorus, and the pattern repeats only every two choruses.

Proof. Number the phrases $0, 1, 2, \dots$ across choruses; player A plays the even-numbered phrases. Chorus $j$ begins with phrase $jm$, which is even for all $j$ if $m$ is even, and has the parity of $j$ if $m$ is odd. ∎

The same parity argument governs antiphonal psalmody: with two half-choirs alternating verses, a psalm with an odd number of verses ends with the choir that began.

Exercise 15.2. Check Fux's rules for the move from the third note to the fourth of the example (cantus E → D, counterpoint E → F). Which rule applies?

Answer

E–E is an octave (perfect) and D–F a minor tenth (imperfect), so rule 2 applies: perfect to imperfect, any motion allowed. The motion is in fact contrary (the cantus falls, the counterpoint rises).

Exercise 15.3. Write the real answer and the tonal answer for a subject that begins G C (5 up to 1) in C major.

Answer

Real answer: transposed up a fifth, D G. Tonal answer: the dominant–tonic frame of the head is mirrored, so 5 → 1 is answered by 1 → 5 (in the dominant key): C G. The rest of the subject would follow as a real transposition.

Exercise 15.4. Using the round condition, explain why a melody that outlines C major in its first two bars and G major in its next two bars cannot be sung as a round with entries two bars apart in two voices without clashes.

Answer

With $n = 2$ segments, once both voices have entered, segment 1 (C major) always sounds against segment 2 (G major). The strong beats then combine C-major and G-major chord tones, for example C against B or E against D, which are dissonances on strong beats. A two-voice round needs both segments to fit one harmony, or a harmony that works with both.

Exercise 15.5. Two players trade threes over a 12-bar blues. How often does the starting player alternate?

Answer

$m = 12/3 = 4$ phrases per chorus, which is even, so the same player starts every chorus.
