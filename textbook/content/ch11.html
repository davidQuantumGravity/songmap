Circle progressions

Some progressions seem to roll downhill by themselves. Start on E minor and let each chord's root fall a fifth: E to A, A to D, D to G, G to C. Every chord prepares the next, the way V prepares I, and the whole chain feels inevitable. With seventh chords the effect is even stronger, and it is one of the main engines of jazz standards and Baroque sequences alike.

Keep falling by fifths inside the key and you pass through all seven diatonic chords before you come home, with one slightly odd step on the way: F to B diminished.

A circle progression is a progression whose roots move by falling fifths (equivalently, rising fourths). The model is V → I: the root of each chord is a fifth above the root of the next, so each chord can act like a dominant of its successor.

The diatonic circle. Inside C major, falling fifths from C visit

C → F → B → E → A → D → G → C   (I → IV → vii° → iii → vi → ii → V → I)

Six of the seven steps are perfect fifths; F → B is a diminished fifth (6 semitones), because the key has only one tritone. The second half of the chain, vi – ii – V – I (or iii – vi – ii – V – I), is the backbone of countless endings.

Why it flows. Two triads a fifth apart share one note (the root of the first is the fifth of the second), and the other voices move by step. With seventh chords there is an extra pull: the seventh of each chord falls by step to the third of the next. In Dm7 → G7 the seventh C falls to B; in G7 → Cmaj7 the seventh F falls to E. A chain of sevenths is therefore a chain of small resolutions, each of which creates the next dissonance.

Sequences. When a short pattern repeats at successively lower pitch levels, the result is a sequence. The circle of fifths is a favorite harmonic plan for sequences in Baroque music, and the same chain of ii7 – V7 – I motions, often moving through several keys, is a staple of jazz.

The golden system has its own circle. Stepping the golden triad's root by a golden third (11 sixth-steps) walks through all 36 golden triads, Cg → vEg → ^Gg → Bg → …, keeping two notes each time and moving one voice by a semitone, exactly as falling fifths keep one note and move the others by step. See the golden circle.

Proposition. Falling fifths within a diatonic scale visit all seven degrees before returning, and exactly one of the seven steps is a diminished fifth.

Proof. In scale-degree numbers ($\mathbb{Z}_7$), a falling fifth is $-4 \equiv +3$. Since $\gcd(3, 7) = 1$, repeated steps of 3 visit every degree. In semitones the seven fifths of the scale (C–G, D–A, E–B, F–C, G–D, A–E, B–F) are perfect (7) except B–F (6); in any listing of the chain, each appears once, so the roots travel $6 \cdot 7 + 6 = 48$ semitones, exactly four octaves. $\square$

Sevenths resolve by step. Count in scale degrees mod 7. A chord on degree $j$ has its seventh on $j + 6 \equiv j - 1$. The next chord of the chain is on $j + 3$, and its third is on $j + 5 \equiv j - 2$. So the seventh of each chord lies one scale step above the third of the next, and falls to it by step (a semitone or a whole tone). $\square$

Exercise 11.1. Write the root sequence of a falling-fifth chain of dominant seventh chords starting on E7, and say after how many chords it returns to E7. Why does this chain leave the key, unlike the diatonic circle?

Answer

E7 A7 D7 G7 C7 F7 B♭7 E♭7 A♭7 D♭7 G♭7 B7, then E7: twelve chords, because 5 (a rising fourth) generates $\mathbb{Z}_{12}$. Dominant sevenths keep their quality at every step, so they need chromatic notes; the diatonic circle instead uses each chord of the key, changing quality as it goes, and closes after seven.

The common progressions

A handful of chord patterns carry an enormous amount of music. You may recognize these three within a bar or two: the four-chord pattern of modern pop, the "doo-wop" loop of 1950s ballads, and the descending Andalusian line of flamenco.

Two more are the grammar of whole genres: the twelve-bar blues, built from three dominant seventh chords, and the ii–V–I of jazz, the falling-fifth chain in its shortest form.

The blues shows how flexible function is: its I7 is a dominant seventh chord that is nevertheless heard as home, and its V7 often moves to IV7, a "backward" motion that classical harmony avoids.

Smoothness. Measured as the smallest total motion of three voices (semitones moved per change, every voice choosing its nearest note), one full cycle of I–V–vi–IV costs 3 + 5 + 1 + 3 = 12 semitones, I–vi–IV–V 2 + 1 + 6 + 3 = 12, and I–IV–V–I 3 + 6 + 3 = 12. The step IV → V (6) is the largest: the roots are a step apart, the chords share no note, and all three voices move a whole tone. ii → V costs only 4, one reason ii so often takes IV's place before V.

Every progression here has golden versions in 36-ET, in which golden passing chords are inserted where the passing-chord theorems allow. In the pop axis, for example, the step G → Am can pass through the two golden chords of the golden deceptive cadence, C – G – vA♭g – A♭g – Am – F (the generated dictionary of Chapter 28 gives a fuller version with passing chords in every change); the Andalusian becomes the golden Andalusian, Am – A♭g – vA♭g – G – ^F♯g – vF♯g – F – vF – ^E – E. See the golden staircase and the Andalusian.

Rotations. A looping progression has no first chord in itself; the starting point is set by phrasing and by where the tonic falls. I–V–vi–IV and vi–IV–I–V are rotations of the same cycle, and so are I–vi–IV–V and IV–V–I–vi. The two cycles are not rotations of each other: their root motions (in semitones, mod 12) are $(+7, +2, +8, +7)$ and $(+9, +8, +2, +5)$.

Voice-leading cost. For triads $X$ and $Y$ in three voices, the cost is $\min_\sigma \sum_i |x_i - y_{\sigma(i)}|$, each difference taken as the shortest distance mod 12. The values quoted above were computed this way over all six assignments $\sigma$. For example V → vi (G B D → A C E) costs 5: G → A, B → C, D → E.

Exercise 11.2 (listening). In the first example, the Andalusian section ends on E major in A minor. Is that ending a full stop, a comma or a surprise, in the terms of Chapter 10?

Answer

E is V of A minor, so the phrase ends with a half cadence: a comma. The E major chord contains G♯, the leading tone of A minor, which is why the loop wants to start over on A minor.

Secondary dominants

Any major or minor chord of a key can be made to sound, for a moment, like a little home of its own. The trick is to put its dominant in front of it. In C major, E7 before A minor makes A minor sound like a brief arrival; A7 before D minor does the same for D minor; D7 before G makes G sound like a tonic. Chain them and the music gains momentum without ever leaving C.

A secondary dominant is the dominant (V or V7) of a chord other than the tonic, written V/x ("five of x"). Playing it before x and resolving into x is called tonicization: x is treated briefly as a tonic, without a real change of key.

V/V is so common that it is often treated as a normal part of the cadence: ii – V/V – V – I, or, in the classical cadence, IV – V/V – I⁶₄ – V – I. Chains of secondary dominants, each resolving into the next (E7 – A7 – D7 – G7 – C), are circle progressions made entirely of dominant sevenths.

The golden ii–V–I adds golden passing chords on the way: Dm – Dg – vGg/D – G – vE♭T13/G – vCg/G – C. The first pair, two golden chords in a row over a held D, needs the natural paths of System 2, because ii and V have opposite qualities and System 1 offers no golden chord between them; the second pair is the golden six-four. See the golden authentic cadence and uneven splits.

Construction. For a target triad with root $t$, V7/t is the dominant seventh on $t + 7$: $\{t+7,\ t+11,\ t+2,\ t+5\}$. Its third $t + 11 = t - 1$ is the leading tone of $t$, and its seventh $t + 5$ falls to the target's third ($t + 4$ or $t + 3$). Comparing with the C major scale $\{0, 2, 4, 5, 7, 9, 11\}$:

  • for $t = 2, 7, 9$ (ii, V, vi) the only foreign note is the leading tone $t - 1$ (C♯, F♯, G♯);
  • for $t = 4$ (iii) the chord B D♯ F♯ A has two foreign notes;
  • for $t = 5$ (IV) the leading tone is E, already in the key, and the foreign note is the seventh, B♭;
  • for $t = 11$ the target is diminished and V/vii° is not used.

Exercise 11.3. In G major, spell V7/ii, V7/V and V7/vi.

Answer

ii = Am, so V7/ii = E7 (E G♯ B D). V = D, so V7/V = A7 (A C♯ E G). vi = Em, so V7/vi = B7 (B D♯ F♯ A).

Borrowed chords and modal mixture

Major and minor keys on the same tonic, C major and C minor, can lend each other chords. A major-key song that suddenly uses F minor instead of F major gets a shadow of sadness; one that goes C – A♭ – B♭ – C gets a heroic, cinematic lift. And a minor-key piece that ends on a major chord sounds like a door opening onto light.

Modal mixture means using in one mode the chords of its parallel mode (the mode with the same tonic). In C major the most common borrowings come from C minor:

The reverse borrowing, a major tonic at the end of a minor piece, is the Picardy third (French tierce de Picardie; the origin of the name is not known for certain). It was a common way to end minor-key pieces in the Baroque.

Another chromatic chord often grouped with mixture is the Neapolitan, the major triad on the lowered second degree, ♭II (D♭ F A♭ in C). It normally appears in first inversion (with F in the bass) as a predominant: ♭II⁶ – V – i.

Golden harmony has a mixture chord that is literally between the modes. The golden triad Cg is the voice-by-voice average of C, Cm and Caug, and C – Cg – Cm moves from major to minor through it. The golden Picardy raises the third of C minor through every sixth-step, Cm – C(^m3) – Cg – C, the third climbing 9 → 10 → 11 → 12 sixth-steps. See modal mixture and the golden Picardy.

The parallel move is P. In neo-Riemannian terms (Chapter 10), the parallel relation C ↔ Cm, F ↔ Fm, G ↔ Gm is exactly the move $P$, which keeps the root and fifth and moves the third by a semitone. Borrowing iv for IV is $P$ applied to one chord of the key.

Which chords are new? C natural minor differs from C major in three notes, E♭, A♭ and B♭. Every one of its seven triads (Cm, Ddim, E♭, Fm, Gm, A♭, B♭) contains at least one of them, so none of the seven is a diatonic triad of C major (checked by listing). Adding harmonic minor's raised seventh B brings back G and Bdim, which are already in C major; that is why the dominant is shared by C major and C minor, while mixture concerns mainly the other degrees.

Exercise 11.4. In C major, which borrowed chord gives the deceptive cadence V – ♭VI? Which note does the leading tone go to, and what common tone does ♭VI share with I?

Answer

♭VI = A♭ (A♭ C E♭). The leading tone B rises to C, as in an authentic cadence, while the bass rises G → A♭ by a semitone. ♭VI shares exactly one note with I: C.

Chromatic mediants

Play C major, then E major. Nothing about the change is functional, and yet it is one of the most striking moves in harmony: one note stays put, the others slide, and the light changes, as if a cloud moved. Nineteenth-century composers, Schubert among them, used these mediant shifts for color, and film scores are full of them.

Two chords are in a mediant relation when their roots are a third apart. The diatonic mediants of C, E minor and A minor, share two notes with C and sound smooth and closely related. A chromatic mediant keeps the quality instead: a major chord whose root is a major or minor third away. From C major there are four:

Each shares exactly one note with C, and each is two neo-Riemannian moves away. The doubly chromatic mediants change quality as well, E♭ minor and A♭ minor from C major, and share no note at all.

Because they keep a common tone and move the other voices by small steps, chromatic mediants can connect keys that are far apart on the circle of fifths: C and E major differ in four sharps, but the chords share E.

Every chromatic mediant pair has exactly one strict golden passing chord, and its partner on the path is a bronze triad:
  • C → E passes Eg (E vA♭ ^B) at the second position;
  • C → A♭ passes Cg (C vE ^G) at the first position;
  • C → A passes ^Cg (^C E vA♭) at the first position;
  • C → E♭ passes ^E♭g (^E♭ G vB) at the second position.
In this sense golden harmony is native to Romantic mediant harmony. See mediant gold.

Modulation

A modulation is a change of home. Sometimes it is so smooth that you only notice afterwards that the music has settled somewhere new; sometimes it is a sudden lift. Listen to both kinds.

Closely related keys. Keys whose scales differ by at most one note are closely related. For C major they are G major and F major (one sharp, one flat), and the relative minors of all three: A minor, E minor and D minor. Keys a step further round the circle of fifths share fewer notes and fewer chords:

Kinds of modulation:

  • Pivot chord. A chord diatonic in both keys is heard first in the old key and then reinterpreted in the new one. A minor is vi in C and ii in G, so C – F – G – Am – D7 – G moves to G major with Am as the pivot.
  • Direct (phrase) modulation. The new key simply starts, often at a phrase boundary. The lift of a whole step near the end of a pop song is the familiar example.
  • Chromatic modulation. A chromatic step in one voice leads into the new key, often through a secondary dominant that turns out to be a real dominant.
  • Common-tone modulation. A held note links two keys, as in the chromatic mediants above.
  • Enharmonic modulation. A chord is respelled. The diminished seventh can resolve to four tonics (Chapter 9), and a dominant seventh A♭ C E♭ G♭ can be respelled as the German augmented sixth A♭ C E♭ F♯, which resolves to G (V of C).

Whether a passage has modulated or only tonicized is a matter of degree: a new key needs time and a cadence to feel like home.

In 36-ET there are two new kinds of modulation: moving a whole key onto another 12-ET layer (a sixth-step up or down), and pivoting through a golden chord that belongs to two worlds at once. For example, Cg lies on the strict path C ↔ A♭ and also on the path Cm ↔ Fm. See changing layers and golden pivot chords.
Pivot-chord modulation itself comes back between layers. C major and ^E♭ major share no note, but F (IV of C) and ^F minor (ii of ^E♭) have the same nearest golden triad, Fg; so do C and ^C minor (Cg), G and ^G minor (Gg). Keys a silver third (333 c) apart therefore share three golden pivot chords, and Fg is "golden IV" in one key and "golden ii" in the other (Chapter 35).

Proposition. Two major keys $k$ steps apart on the circle of fifths share $7 - k$ scale notes for $0 \le k \le 5$, and 2 notes for $k = 6$.

Proof sketch. A major scale is seven consecutive notes on the circle of fifths. Moving the tonic up a fifth shifts that window by one position: one note leaves and one enters. After $k \le 5$ shifts the windows overlap in $7 - k$ positions; at $k = 6$ the new window wraps around the twelve-note circle, and the overlap is again 2 (C major and F♯ major share B and E♯ = F). The triad counts in the table were found by listing. $\square$

Shared triads at distance 1. A triad is diatonic in both C and G exactly when it avoids F and F♯; of C's seven triads, Dm, F and Bdim contain F, leaving C, Em, G and Am.
