Golden Worlds is a set of five instrumental songs that keep golden harmony as their pitch language throughout. Their styles are broken beat, jazz/funk, progressive electronic music, chamber counterpoint and atmospheric music. Each lasts between three and four minutes. Familiar instrumental sounds and synthetic golden spectra share the same scales, and each piece ends within the golden system.

This chapter explains decisions visible in the scores. The listening descriptions state the intended effect; the tables and pitch comparisons report computed score facts. The videos follow the composed note events, with a separate window for each pitched part. Listen to a whole piece first, then replay a passage while following one instrument. Its rests and replies often explain the arrangement more clearly than the combined chord.

Melody revision 2. The current players contain newly written melodies and sampled melodic instruments. The first listening review liked the bass lines, chords and comping, but found the leads too loud, shrill and melodically unconvincing. The revision retains the harmonic paths, bass figures, grooves and accompaniment patterns. It replaces the high chord-outline melodies with smaller motifs, repeated notes, quieter answers and longer rests. The originals remain available on the comparison listening page.

A complete song inside the golden system

The earlier songs of Chapter 47 placed golden chords inside Western harmony. Here a golden chord can last, begin a phrase, support a tune and close the piece. A bass line, a drum pattern and a recognizable melody establish the character of each song. The tuning supplies its pitch relationships.

Listen for recurring ideas that change their function. A short question becomes a longer answer; a bass reply fills a gap in the lead; the return keeps a recognizable rhythm while changing its register or key. Those relationships carry the longer forms. A new scale or a new sound alone would provide much less development.

A pitch class groups notes that differ by whole octaves. In 36-tone equal temperament, abbreviated 36-ET, the octave contains thirty-six equal steps. One step, called a sixth-step here, is one third of a semitone. The prefix ^ raises a written note one sixth-step, and v lowers it one. Cg is the golden triad C vE ^G. Cg7 adds B. Both chord symbols appear throughout the videos.

The seven-note scale C golden7 is C C♯ vE vF ^G ^A♭ B. Its centered mode is called 3|3 because its generating chain extends three notes below the tonic and three above it. Its seven modes are different placements of the same chain window. The ten-note decatonic, golden10, supplies smaller melodic steps and more golden chords. Its 35 and 53 readings split golden7's wide gaps in opposite orders. They are two modes of one scale family (Chapter 36).

Pitch language and timbre are separate choices. An electric piano playing a golden triad retains its electric-piano spectrum. A golden-spectrum pad instead places partials at powers of two times powers of the golden ratio. Both choices occur here. The songs use tempered fundamentals; they do not claim exact golden-ratio tuning of every interval. Nor does the choice of tuning establish a psychological effect or universal consonance. The timbre experiments and their limits are discussed in Chapter 44.

The source scores are the five .sm files in songs/golden_worlds/. The chapter's audio sources preserve these revised scores exactly, including their notes, instruments, balance, form and timing. Their expanded note-event digests were checked for equality. The earlier instrument-only editions remain separate historical versions. The edition notes identify the recorded instruments.

A mode supplies a collection, but a tune also needs recognizable rhythm, a limited working register and places to rest. The revised leads stay within an octave, and none reaches C6. Most of the detail remains in the bass, comping and relationships between parts. Fader numbers cannot be compared directly between sampled and synthetic instruments, so the balance was checked from the rendered stems. The lead stems measure about seven to nine and a half decibels below their original versions before mastering; that measurement is not a listening verdict.

Definitions. Let one octave be the pitch-class group $\mathbb{Z}_{36}$, with C represented by zero. The tempered golden generator is twenty-five steps. A mode with chain window $[-b,a]$ has the collection $$S_{b,a}=\{25j\pmod{36}:-b\leq j\leq a\}.$$ For golden7, $a+b=6$; for golden10, $a+b=9$. A golden triad on root $r$ has classes $\{r,r+11,r+22\}$, and its golden seventh adds $r+33$, all modulo thirty-six.

Computed score checks. Each composed pitched note and each chord tone belongs to its section's declared golden collection. Drum-kit voices are treated as percussion. The five songs contain no Western chord visits. This membership result describes the score; it does not assess how convincing a cadence sounds. The latter is a question for listening, as in the proposed golden functions of Chapter 31.

Sideways Sun: a broken-beat conversation

The first piece builds its movement from gaps. The sampled flute begins after the beat, the electric piano answers with short chord strikes, and the bass alternates weight with silence. Small changes in the drum pattern keep the backbeat recognizable while moving the surrounding accents. The intended character is a loose, forward-moving conversation, with a brief pause before its strongest return.

At 1:02 the ten-note section opens the melodic vocabulary. At 1:20 the same instrumental group visits a neighboring golden key. The quiet passage at 2:13 removes the drum and bass parts. The lead's return at 2:31 consequently has a different weight even though its rhythmic identity remains familiar.

The opening key is C golden7. The head uses Cg7, ^A♭g7, vFg7 and vEg, with C♯g7 entering as another approach chord. Chord-relative bass and keyboard figures follow the harmony. The lead is written in scale degrees, with short neighboring notes between its longer chord tones. Its eight-bar phrases include different endings and rests, rather than repeating one bar of arpeggio.

The middle uses the 35 decatonic on C. It retains all seven notes of centered C golden7 and adds D, vF♯ and ^A. These pitches also support new golden roots, so the expansion changes both the line and the chord sequence. The following section moves to vE golden7. This is a neighboring chain window, with six pitch classes shared with the opening key. Common material can carry the phrase while a changed note identifies the destination.

The part windows make the bass rhythm particularly useful to study. Its octave jumps and rests have a role in the groove; they are not simply a low copy of the lead. The sampled harp carries remembered phrases during the instrumental replies, while golden glass retains the short accompaniment figures. The flute's theme is anchored by repeated vE4 and C4, rather than by a fresh arpeggio at every chord change.

Computed collection comparison. In sixth-steps from C, centered golden7 is $\{0,3,11,14,22,25,33\}$, and the 35 decatonic is $\{0,3,6,11,14,17,22,25,28,33\}$. Their difference is $\{6,17,28\}$, the three added notes. The neighboring vE golden7 replaces C♯ by vE♭. That is a different operation from adding notes to the same key.

The principal head progression lasts eight bars. Its written question and answer use different note patterns over related harmonies. This separates three kinds of repetition: recurring harmony, recurring rhythmic identity and a literal recurrence of the complete phrase. Only the last repeats all the musical information.

Pocket Algebra: the decatonic in a pocket

The second piece uses a steadier conversation. Soft sampled vibraphone and marimba take turns with the head, while electric-piano chords punctuate the gaps. The bass uses more frequent attacks than in the first song. The solo continues that vocabulary with shorter runs, pauses and repeated rhythmic shapes.

The bridge begins at 1:00. Its clearest immediate change is the removal of the drums. The written solo starts at 1:17, the electric piano takes a featured turn at 1:51, and the fullest statement arrives at 2:34. The ending reduces the activity instead of adding a final flourish.

The main collection is F golden10 6|3, the 53 reading. A golden triad in the decatonic generally skips two scale notes between chord tones. In one-based numbering its shape is 1–4–7; in SongMap's zero-based degrees it is 0–3–6. A 1–3–5 triad built mechanically from decatonic degrees would describe a different set of intervals.

The comping uses selected chord tones and rests. The lead, plucked replies and bass supply other members of the harmony at different moments. The written solo spans sixteen bars and follows a sixteen-bar progression, with a varied second half. Its pitches were checked against the chords under those particular bars.

A distinction now sounded in the score. The bridge declares F golden10 5|4. Relative to 6|3, that collection replaces E♭ by ^D. The original draft left both defining pitches out of the compared passages. The revised head sounds E♭ on its way to E, and the second bridge bar briefly passes through ^D between ^C♯ and E. The bridge is still mostly shared material: a single prepared inflection identifies the new collection without turning the phrase into a demonstration of every scale note. Removing the drums supplies its larger textural contrast.

The short comparison below isolates the same distinction. It alternates E♭ and ^D above the same F, using stable tones. Its exposed tuning comparison is separate from the complete song's soft vibraphone phrasing.

Computed mode difference. The windows for 6|3 and 5|4 are $[-6,3]$ and $[-5,4]$. One chain note leaves and another enters, ten generator links away. Since $25\cdot10\equiv-2\pmod{36}$, the replacement lowers one pitch by two sixth-steps, or $66.67$ cents in this temperament. The exact golden limma is $69.10$ cents (Chapter 23).

Score observation. Testing the revised bridge note set against the set difference finds ^D, while testing the revised first head finds E♭. Both are degree eight in their respective ten-note collections. The bridge inflection is short and resolves to a chord tone; it is not sustained as a new harmony. A key declaration alone would not establish this distinction, so the chapter checks sounded notes as well as labels.

Try it. Pause the comparison between its two notes and sing the continuation you expect. Then compare the quiet bridge with the opening head. Identify a change you can hear without consulting the mode label.

Parallax Arcade: a return one sixth-step higher

The electronic piece makes its large-scale change through a small pitch displacement. A regular kick supports short organ chords, a plucked arpeggio and a recurring lead figure. The middle opens into a larger collection, then removes most of the rhythm. When the beat returns, every pitched player belongs to a key one sixth-step higher. The strongest statement comes after the new level has had time to settle.

The decatonic begins at 1:02. The empty-lane passage at 1:33 prepares the change, and the comma-crossing section at 1:48 establishes ^D. The later summit at 2:35 combines the fuller arrangement with a developed lead. Its arrival is separate from the first statement in the higher key.

The starting mode is D golden7 4|2. The following collection is D golden10 3|6, the 35 decatonic. This is a change of collection, not a strict nesting. Centered golden7 would fit inside the 35 decatonic, but the 4|2 mode has a different note. Here vF leaves, while E♭, E, vA♭ and ^B enter. Six pitch classes are shared.

The later shift to ^D retains the decatonic's interval pattern and raises every pitch class by one sixth-step. Degree melodies use the destination key. Chord names are absolute in SongMap, so the destination progressions were also respelled. Transposing the lead alone would leave the bass and harmony in the earlier collection.

The final sections keep ^D as their center. Thus the piece does not cancel its change by returning to the opening D. The same rhythmic language is remembered at a different pitch level. The new instrument panels let the reader check this directly: bass, organ, arpeggio and lead all use the higher collection.

The name comma crossing refers to the tempered operation. An exact golden comma is slightly smaller than one step of 36-ET. The video demonstrates the equal-tempered shift used by the score.

Computed sets. The D 4|2 collection and D 35 decatonic have six common pitch classes. Their cardinalities are seven and ten, so the move removes one and adds four. The exact sets are obtained by transposing their chain-window definitions by six sixth-steps, the pitch class D.

Derivation. Transposition by one step is $T_1(x)=x+1\pmod{36}$. In frequency it multiplies a note by $2^{1/36}\approx1.01944$, a shift of $1200/36=33.33$ cents. The exact golden comma is $\varphi^{13}/2^9$, about $30.17$ cents. These are related by the temperament, not numerically equal.

Unlike neighboring chain keys, these two 35 decatonic collections share no pitch classes. This follows by enumerating their ten-element sets and their translates by one. A reduced texture precedes the new level; the destination section assigns every pitched part to the higher collection.

Try it. Follow only the bass through the change, then replay it following the lead. Explain why a shared rhythmic pattern can identify the return when there are no shared pitch classes.

Two Windows: complementary pentatonics

The chamber piece changes the source of complexity. It has no drum part. A sampled cello, grand piano, folk harp and a low cantus share a three-beat bar. Two subjects first appear separately, then meet. Later passages exchange their instruments and registers, develop material in the 53 mode and bring the parts together in a longer phrase.

The first subject enters at 0:15, the second at 0:30, and they meet at 0:45. The 53-mode development starts at 1:15. A quiet passage at 1:45 clears the texture before the return. The twelve-bar reunion begins at 2:30. Its phrase length differs from the eight-bar statements that introduced the material.

A subject is a recognizable melodic idea that can return and combine with another line. Here the two subjects draw their notes from the complementary pentatonics inside G's 35 decatonic. Their union supplies the ten-note collection, while each line draws from its own five-note pool. The octave does not add another pitch class to that count.

The construction is easiest to see on C. The first pentatonic is C D vF ^G ^A. The second is C♯ vE vF♯ ^A♭ B. They alternate when the ten notes of the 35 decatonic are sorted in pitch order. On G the whole construction is transposed. These are the two separate note pools of the initial subjects, not a requirement that the complete accompaniment remain inside either pentatonic.

The harmony supports their held notes and their arrivals. It changes when the subjects meet, rather than repeating every chord from their separate statements. Moving non-chord tones remain part of the counterpoint. Their rhythm, duration and continuation matter as much as set membership.

The later mirror passage turns the opening rise downward, keeps its breathing points and adjusts the pitches to the current harmony. It is not a strict pitch inversion or a full retrograde of the opening subject. The piece uses the practical idea of an invention: a small amount of material receives several distinct treatments.

Computed partition. On C, the two pitch-class sets in sixth-steps are $$P_A=\{0,6,14,22,28\},\qquad P_B=\{3,11,17,25,33\}.$$ They are disjoint, and $P_A\cup P_B$ is the 35 decatonic. The written subjects use even and odd degree indices respectively. In this ten-note scale the next octave is degree ten, so degree ten returns to the first pentatonic's tonic class.

Every pitch in the initial first subject belongs to $P_A$, and every pitch in the initial second subject belongs to $P_B$, after transposition to G. The later development is checked against its own declared mode. The partition of the opening subjects therefore does not incorrectly constrain every later section.

Try it. Name the two pitch classes at the first moment when both subjects sound. Use the two panels to separate them. Then find a moment when one part rests while the other continues, and explain how that rest makes the second line easier to follow.

Night Orchard: a melody through darker modes

The final piece gives the pitch changes more space. A golden-spectrum pad, sampled folk harp, glass bells, low French horn and sine bass support longer phrases. The bell's rests are substantial, and no steady drum part marks the bar. The melody remains the thread through several changes of collection. The largest bloom occurs once; the remaining sections move away from it and settle.

The centered mode arrives at 0:39. The darker passage begins at 1:03, and the decatonic bloom at 1:24. The neighboring-key detour starts at 1:48. At 2:27 the music finds the familiar collection again, and the last phrase begins at 2:48. The remaining tail lets the final sound decay.

The opening is A golden7 5|1. The centered 3|3 mode follows, then the darker 1|5 mode. In the brightness order of Chapter 36, each move down one mode lowers one pitch by five sixth-steps. Moving two places therefore changes two members of the collection. These are changes in the arrangement of intervals around A, while A remains the named center.

The darkest passage has a useful harmonic consequence. Its collection does not contain Ag, the golden triad on A. The accompaniment uses other golden roots available in that mode. It therefore does not force an A golden triad under a melody whose collection has removed its fifth. Both the harp and the harmony participate in the change.

The following decatonic is A golden10 4|5. It gives the bloom a larger pitch vocabulary. A later passage visits vC♯ golden7, a neighboring chain key. The final two sections return to centered A golden7. The overall journey therefore differs from Parallax Arcade: it returns to its earlier center instead of ending one step higher.

The section lengths are five, eight, eight, seven, eight, thirteen, seven and eight bars. They provide unequal spans for the melody and its responses. Their use is a compositional choice. Counting bars alone does not make a rhythm golden-ratio timed, and this song does not include a binaural-beat layer.

Computed tonic test. The 1|5 mode has chain window $[-1,5]$. A tonic golden triad requires chain positions $0,-1,-2$; position $-2$ is absent. Thus a golden triad cannot sit on the tonic of this mode. The score's dark passage uses other scale-contained golden triads and sevenths.

Computed form. The section lengths sum to sixty-four bars. At eighty quarter-note beats per minute in four-four time, the music lasts $64\cdot4\cdot60/80=192$ seconds. The eight-second tail brings the file to two hundred seconds. The visual pages use the actual bar boundaries, so a thirteen-bar section receives thirteen successive one-bar views.

Reading the instrument windows

Choose one panel and follow it through a complete phrase. Bright noteheads are sounding; dim noteheads belong to the current bar but have not yet sounded or have already ended. Horizontal lines show held duration. The vertical cursor moves through one bar and then begins the next. A printed rest in the upper corner means that the part currently has no held note.

The five song videos now use 1080p performance cards. Large chips at the top of each card name every sounding note in that instrument, including each member of a chord. Octave doublings remain separate, and ^ and v retain their sixth-step meanings. The header also lists the current chord's individual pitch classes. Follow the piano card to see how its actual voicing contributes to that harmony.

The colored pitch strip below each staff has three cells per semitone in 36-ET. Its lit cells identify the actual tempered pitches. A brief note-on glow, a colored card border and a filling line under each chip help track entrances and held durations. A resting card stays in place. The staff fits the current bar, so an instrument's distant low or high notes elsewhere in the song do not shrink this passage.

The chord at the top describes the current harmony of the whole piece. The colored panels separate the instrumental contributions. A bass note and a lead note that happen to share a pitch remain in their own windows. The section strip at the bottom shows where the present phrase sits within the whole form.

The renderer now has an instrument layer. Each instance selects its own parts, label, position and size. The part gets its own staff register and clef, so a high lead and a low bass no longer compress one shared pitch range. The default panel shows one complete bar. It can also show several bars or switch to sounding-notes-only mode, where future noteheads are omitted.

This is proportional score notation: note positions represent onset times and horizontal lines represent duration. It is not a fully engraved performance edition with conventional beams and rhythmic rests. The written note gate determines highlighting. Instrument release and reverberation may continue after that gate has ended, so a dimmed note can still have an audible tail.

SongMap Studio offers an Instrument grid layout and controls for the selected panel's instrument, label, viewing mode and number of bars. The saved .view.yaml file stores these visual decisions separately from the .sm music. The classic layout remains available.

The new Performance grid preset selects 1080p cards. Individual note names, the pitch strip and note glow can be toggled separately. Compact score cards and the classic header are still available. This visual revision reuses the existing melody-revision-2 audio; the notes, instruments and mix are unchanged.

Animated cartoon performers and score bubbles are a possible later presentation layer. They are not part of this implementation. The independently rendered instrument windows provide reusable cropped notation for that future work without changing how the score represents music.

Timing. For constant tempo $T$ in quarter-note beats per minute, a bar containing $B$ such beats lasts $60B/T$ seconds. A four-four bar has $B=4$, and a three-four bar has $B=3$. The actual timeline supplies bar boundaries when tempo or meter changes; the panel does not assume every bar has an average duration.

Verification boundary. The note filters, actual-bar pagination, empty selections, independently drawn panels and sounding-note mode have regression tests. Chapter source translations preserve the revised canonical note-event digests. Finished videos are checked for their video and audio streams, frame rate and duration, and sampled frames are inspected. Musical and audio checks address specific faults; they do not prove that a piece will sound convincing to every listener.

The score analyses in this chapter are reproducible set comparisons and timeline calculations. Their compositional interpretations remain proposals for listening. A mode label, an interval count or a clean spectrogram cannot replace the listener's judgment of the resulting phrase.
