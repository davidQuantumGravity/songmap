The golden triad

Western harmony is built from triads: three notes stacked in thirds. Golden harmony is built the same way, with golden thirds. Two golden thirds on C give Cg, the golden triad: C, a slightly lowered E, and a slightly raised G. Next to C major it sounds like the same chord drained of certainty. The third is neither bright nor dark, and the fifth leans outward. Nudge its third up and its fifth down by one small step each, and it settles into C major.

In exact golden ratios the chord can also be spread out along the golden chain, each note a golden sixth above the last. That spacing has a calmer, more open sound.

Definition. The golden triad on C is Cg = C vE ^G, at (0, 11, 22) sixth-steps above C, with exact frequency ratios

$$1 : \frac{2}{\varphi} : \frac{4}{\varphi^2} = 1 : 1.2361 : 1.5279.$$

On middle C (261.63 Hz) its exact frequencies are 261.63, 323.39 and 399.73 Hz. Its intervals are two golden thirds of 366.91 c, closed to the octave by 466.18 c. In 36-ET these are 11, 11 and 14 sixth-steps.

The piano roll shows the three layers in colour: each row is a sixth-step. C major, C minor and C augmented use only layer-0 rows; Cg has its third one row below E and its fifth one row above G.

Neighbours. Counting voice-leading distance in sixth-steps (the total motion of the voices), Cg is 2 from C major (third up one, fifth down one), 3 from C minor (third down two, fifth down one), 3 from C augmented (third up one, fifth up two) and 4 from A♭ major (third down two, fifth up two). It sits in the middle of the Western triads on C, which is the averaging principle of this chapter.

Chain form. The chord can also be voiced as three consecutive notes of the golden chain, 1 : φ : φ². With the fifth at the bottom: ^G3, a golden sixth up to vE4, another to C5 (199.86, 323.39 and 523.25 Hz). So 1 : φ : φ² is Cg in second inversion with the third in the middle: the chain read upward goes fifth, third, root. The golden triad is the three chain notes $j = 0, -1, -2$ (Chapter 23), read downward from the root.

Is it the least stable chord? No. With ordinary harmonic tones (strings, voices, organ) it is rougher than major, minor and augmented, but smoother than diminished, and most of its tension sits in the golden fifth (Chapter 26). With golden-spectrum tones it becomes the smoothest triad of all. Those are measurements in a model, not facts about every instrument.

Western mirror. Chapter 9 builds major, minor, diminished and augmented triads from major and minor thirds (Triads). The golden triad uses one intermediate third twice, like the diminished and augmented triads, which use the minor or major third twice (the symmetric chords).

Definition. For $r \in \mathbb{Z}_{36}$, the golden triad on $r$ is $G_r = \{r, r+11, r+22\}$. Exactly, it is $f \cdot (1, 2/\varphi, 4/\varphi^2)$ for a root frequency $f$.

Proposition. (i) The 36 golden triads $G_0, \ldots, G_{35}$ are distinct. (ii) Each is inversionally symmetric: $-G_r = G_{14 - r}$. (Theorem.)

Proof. (i) $G_r = G_s$ would make $s - r$ a symmetry of $\{0, 11, 22\}$, i.e. $\{0,11,22\} + t = \{0,11,22\}$ for some $t \ne 0$; but the gaps 11, 11, 14 are not a rotation of themselves except trivially. (ii) $-G_r = \{-r, -r-11, -r-22\} = \{-r-22, (-r-22)+11, (-r-22)+22\} = G_{-r-22} = G_{14-r}$, since $-22 \equiv 14$. For $r = 0$: $-\{0, 11, 22\} = \{0, 25, 14\} = G_{14}$, the golden triad on vF. ∎

The exact triad has the same symmetry: any chord $\{1, a, a^2\}$ with two equal stacked intervals is its own inversion (around the middle note).

The golden seventh

Add a third golden third on top of Cg and you arrive at the major seventh: Cg7, the golden seventh chord. In 36-ET its top note is exactly B; in exact golden tuning it is 0.73 cents higher, far too little to hear. So Cg7 is C major seventh with its third and fifth bent: the third down a little, the fifth up a little. Alternate the two chords and the outer voices stand still while the middle shimmers. Then listen to the golden triad "breathe": it gains a note (Cg becomes Cg7) and then loses its root, which leaves the golden triad on vE.

Definition. The golden seventh chord on C is three stacked golden thirds, Cg7 = C vE ^G B, at (0, 11, 22, 33) sixth-steps, with exact ratios

$$1 : \frac2\varphi : \frac4{\varphi^2} : \frac8{\varphi^3} = 1 : 1.2361 : 1.5279 : 1.8885.$$

In cents its notes are 0, 366.91, 733.82 and 1100.73. 36-ET renders the seventh as 33 sixth-steps, which is exactly the 12-ET major seventh B (1100 c); the exact golden seventh is 0.73 c sharper. So in 36-ET

Cg7 = Cmaj7(v3,^5).

One golden semitone below the octave. The golden seventh satisfies

$$\frac{8}{\varphi^3} = 2 - 2\varphi^{-6},$$

so it lies exactly $2\varphi^{-6}$ below the octave, and the octave lies a golden apotome ($\varphi^3/4$ = 99.27 c) above it. This identity produces one of the beats of the golden seventh (Chapter 26).

Chain form. Octave-equivalently, Cg7 is the chain chord 1 : φ : φ² : φ³: B, ^G, vE, C going up by golden sixths. The seventh is the bottom of the chain and the root is its top, three golden sixths higher. In this sense the golden seventh is "the chord of φ³".

Breathing. Cg7 is the union of two neighbouring golden triads: Cg (C vE ^G) and vEg (vE ^G B). Adding B to Cg and then dropping C moves the harmony one step along the golden circle (Chapter 31).

golden7 (Chapter 23) contains four golden sevenths: Cg7, C♯g7, vFg7 and ^A♭g7.

Western mirror. Chapter 9's major seventh chord is also three stacked thirds, major–minor–major (Seventh chords). The golden seventh uses three equal thirds, so it is the golden counterpart of the major seventh in spelling but of the diminished seventh in construction. The two differ at the top: after the diminished seventh's three minor thirds, a fourth minor third returns exactly to the octave, while after three golden thirds only a golden apotome (99.27 c) is left below the octave, so a fourth golden third would overshoot it.

Proposition. $8/\varphi^3 = 2 - 2\varphi^{-6} = 8\sqrt5 - 16$. (Theorem.)

Proof. $\varphi^3 = 2\varphi + 1 = 2 + \sqrt5$ and $\varphi^{-3} = 1/(2+\sqrt5) = \sqrt5 - 2$, so $\varphi^3 - \varphi^{-3} = 4$. Dividing by $\varphi^3$: $1 - \varphi^{-6} = 4\varphi^{-3}$, and multiplying by 2: $2 - 2\varphi^{-6} = 8/\varphi^3$. Numerically $\varphi^{-6} = 9 - 4\sqrt5 = 0.055728$, and $2 - 0.111456 = 1.888544$. ∎

Layers. $33 \equiv 0 \pmod 3$, so the seventh is on the root's layer: Cg7 has two notes on its root's layer (C, B) and one on each other layer (^G, vE).

As a chain window. $\{0, 11, 22, 33\} = \{25j : j = 0, -1, -2, -3\}$. Hence $G7_r = G_r \cup G_{r+11}$: the golden seventh on $r$ is the union of the golden triads on $r$ and on $r + 11$, which share two notes.

The averaging principle

The golden triad is not a stranger to Western harmony. It is the exact average of three familiar chords: C major, C minor and C augmented, voice by voice. Hear the three chords, then their average. A darker average also exists: C major, C minor and C diminished average to the silver triad Cs, with a third a little above minor and a fifth a little below perfect.

Write each triad on C as (third, fifth) in sixth-steps: major (12, 21), minor (9, 21), augmented (12, 24), diminished (9, 18). Averaging three of them voice by voice always gives whole numbers of sixth-steps, because three semitone positions divided by 3 are always a whole number of thirds of a semitone. In particular

$$\text{Cg} = \frac{\text{C} + \text{Cm} + \text{Caug}}{3}: \quad \text{third } \frac{12 + 9 + 12}{3} = 11,\quad \text{fifth } \frac{21 + 21 + 24}{3} = 22,$$ $$\text{Cs} = \frac{\text{C} + \text{Cm} + \text{Cdim}}{3}: \quad \text{third } \frac{12 + 9 + 9}{3} = 10,\quad \text{fifth } \frac{21 + 21 + 18}{3} = 20.$$

This is the author's original description made exact: the golden third is "2/3 major, 1/3 minor", the golden fifth "2/3 perfect, 1/3 augmented". Golden is the balance point of the bright triads, silver of the dark ones.

Exact or tempered? The averaging principle is a statement about 36-ET. Averaging cents gives $(400 + 400 + 300)/3 = 366.67$ c, the 36-ET golden third, while the exact golden third $2/\varphi$ is 366.91 c, 0.24 c higher (and the fifth 0.49 c higher). Averaging just triads instead (5/4, 6/5, 3/2, 25/16) would give a third of 362.76 c and a fifth of 725.51 c, further from golden. So averaging does not derive $\varphi$; it explains where the golden chord sits among Western chords, to within a quarter of a cent.

Passing chords are averages too. On the strict passing paths of Chapter 27, the two passing chords between $X$ and $Y$ are $P_1 = (2X + Y)/3$ and $P_2 = (X + 2Y)/3$, voice by voice. For example Cg $= (2\cdot\text{C} + \text{A♭/C})/3$ on the move C → A♭.

A caution. Every 36-ET chord is the average of three 12-ET chords, because any sixth-step pitch is a sum of three semitone pitches divided by 3. The principle has musical content only when the averaged chords are related: same root, neighbouring qualities, or neighbours in a progression.

Geometry. In Tymoczko's chord spaces ("The Geometry of Musical Chords", Science, 2006), voice leading is distance and the augmented triad, the most even triad, lies at the centre (Chapter 9). Averaging is moving along straight lines in that space. Golden and silver are near-central points, which is one reason they connect efficiently to many chords and make good passing chords.

Proposition (the centroids). Among averages of three triads from {major, minor, augmented, diminished} on a common root, Cg arises exactly from maj + min + aug and from aug + aug + dim; Cs arises exactly from maj + min + dim and from aug + dim + dim. (Theorem.)

Proof. Let $n_{\text{maj}}, n_{\text{min}}, n_{\text{aug}}, n_{\text{dim}} \ge 0$ count the chords, with sum 3. The third is 12 for major and augmented and 9 otherwise, and the fifth is $21 + 3$ for augmented, $21 - 3$ for diminished and 21 otherwise. So the average is

$$(\text{third}, \text{fifth}) = \big(9 + n_{\text{maj}} + n_{\text{aug}},\ 21 + n_{\text{aug}} - n_{\text{dim}}\big).$$

Golden (11, 22) needs $n_{\text{maj}} + n_{\text{aug}} = 2$ and $n_{\text{aug}} = n_{\text{dim}} + 1$; with the sum 3 this forces $n_{\text{maj}} = n_{\text{min}} = 1 - n_{\text{dim}}$, so $n_{\text{dim}} = 0$ (maj + min + aug) or $n_{\text{dim}} = 1$ (aug + aug + dim). Silver (10, 20) needs $n_{\text{maj}} + n_{\text{aug}} = 1$ and $n_{\text{dim}} = n_{\text{aug}} + 1$, forcing $n_{\text{maj}} = n_{\text{min}} = 1 - n_{\text{aug}}$: maj + min + dim or aug + dim + dim. ∎

The same formula gives the whole table: the 20 multisets map onto 16 points, with exactly four points hit twice: (10, 20), (10, 21), (11, 21) and (11, 22). (Checked by enumeration.)

The equal-third line: silver, golden, bronze

The diminished triad stacks two minor thirds; the augmented stacks two major thirds. Golden stacks two thirds in between. 36-ET offers one more third between minor and major (the silver third, just above minor) and one beyond each end, and stacking each one twice gives a whole family of chords in a row: from a chord narrower than diminished, through diminished, silver, golden and augmented, to bronze, wider than augmented. One smooth path also runs straight along this row: from C augmented to Cg to Cs to C diminished, every voice gliding.

"Exact chain third" means the nearest golden-chain interval: the silver third is chain distance 14 inverted and the bronze third chain distance 11 inverted (Chapter 23). Silver is defined by averaging and lives naturally in 36-ET; its exact tuning is a choice, not a consequence.

One line. These chords are evenly spaced points on a single straight line of chord space, and one uniform path runs along it: Caug → Cg → Cs → Cdim, the root holding while the third falls one sixth-step and the fifth two per chord. Bronze is golden reflected through augmented ($2 \cdot \text{aug} - \text{golden}$), and T8 is silver reflected through diminished.

Golden's partner. On every strict passing path between two major triads (or two minor triads) a third or fifth apart, the passing chord that is not golden is bronze; between augmented and diminished it is silver. Over all 240 strict golden paths with Western chords at both ends (these also include a few with a diminished or suspended endpoint, such as C♯dim → Esus4, whose partner is bronze), the partner is bronze in 168 and silver in 72. Example: V → I passes G – vE♭T13 – vCg – C (Chapter 27).

On the names. "Silver" is fixed by the averaging principle (the dark-side centroid). "Bronze" continues the sequence of metallic means, golden (1), silver (2), bronze (3). The metallic-mean numbers themselves, $1 + \sqrt2$ and $(3 + \sqrt{13})/2$, are not the ratios of these chords; the names follow the ordering, not the numbers.

Proposition. (i) The points $T_n = (0, n, 2n) = n\,(0, 1, 2)$ lie on one line through the origin of $\mathbb{R}^3$, evenly spaced; with the voices paired root–root, third–third, fifth–fifth, the total voice-leading distance from $T_n$ to $T_m$ is $3|n - m|$ sixth-steps. (ii) $T_{13} = 2T_{12} - T_{11}$ and $T_8 = 2T_9 - T_{10}$. (iii) Stacking a third of $n$ sixth-steps returns to the start after $36/\gcd(n, 36)$ steps. (Theorem.)

Proof. (i) $|n - m| + 2|n - m|$. (ii) $(0, 24, 48) - (0, 11, 22) = (0, 13, 26)$ and $(0, 18, 36) - (0, 10, 20) = (0, 8, 16)$. (iii) The order of $n$ in $\mathbb{Z}_{36}$. ∎

The path Caug → Cg → Cs → Cdim is $P_k = (0, 12, 24) + k\,(0, -1, -2)$, $k = 0, 1, 2, 3$: a uniform path with displacement (0, −1, −2) semitones, which is the first of the twelve path types of Chapter 27.

The layer theorem

Move C major up a sixth-step and every note moves to the next layer together: the chord keeps its character and only its overall pitch changes. The golden triad cannot be moved onto a single layer, because its notes already sit on all three: C on the ordinary layer, ^G on the up layer, vE on the down layer. This is what makes it sound like a chord from somewhere else rather than a mistuned Western chord.

Recall the layer of a 36-ET note: 0 for the 12-ET notes, 1 for ^ notes, 2 for v notes (Chapter 24).

The layer theorem. A golden triad has exactly one note on each of the three layers. Western chords live on one layer.

A golden seventh has two notes on its root's layer (the root and the seventh) and one on each other layer. Call the number of layers a chord touches its irrationality degree: Western chords have degree 1, golden triads the maximum, 3. Micro-altered chords sit in between: C(^5) = C E ^G has degree 2.

Why it matters. In a passing progression between two Western chords (both on layer 0), the passing chords are reached by moving each voice up or down by sixth-steps. For a passing chord to be golden it must touch all three layers, so the voice leading must contain one voice that holds, one that moves into the ^ layer and one that moves into the v layer. That single condition, the admissibility condition, is what makes golden passing chords rare and predictable (the four-path theorem).

Not only golden. The theorem's argument works for every equal-third triad whose third is not a multiple of 3 sixth-steps: silver (10), bronze (13) and T8 also have one note per layer. The converse fails too: the cluster C ^C vC♯ has one note per layer but is nothing like a triad. (It is carried onto Cg by multiplication by 11, Chapter 33.) The layer theorem says golden chords are fully irrational; it does not single them out.

Theorem (layer theorem). With $\lambda(p) = p \bmod 3$: $\lambda(r + 11) = \lambda(r) + 2$ and $\lambda(r + 22) = \lambda(r) + 1$ (mod 3), so $\{\lambda(r), \lambda(r+11), \lambda(r+22)\} = \{0, 1, 2\}$.

Proof. $11 \equiv 2$ and $22 \equiv 1 \pmod 3$. ∎

Generalization. A three-note chord $\{r, r + a, r + b\}$ has one note per layer if and only if $\{0, a \bmod 3, b \bmod 3\} = \{0, 1, 2\}$. For $T_n$ ($a = n$, $b = 2n$) this holds exactly when $3 \nmid n$.

Chain reading. $G_{25j} = \{25j, 25(j-1), 25(j-2)\}$ has layers $j, j-1, j-2 \pmod 3$: three consecutive chain notes are on three different layers, because the chain itself cycles through the layers.

Naming chords in 36-ET

A chord name should tell you what to play. In 36-ET a name has up to four parts: the root (possibly with an up or down arrow), the quality, any bent chord tones, and the bass note. For example vCg/G is the golden triad on vC (a sixth-step below C) with G in the bass: the golden counterpart of the cadential six-four, heard here on its way to C.

Alterations. A parenthesis after a Western chord bends named chord tones by sixth-steps:

  • C(v3,^5) = C vE ^G = Cg, and Cmaj7(v3,^5) = Cg7;

Roman numerals carry the same arrows, relative to the key: in C major, vIg = vCg, vIVg = vFg, IIg = Dg, ♭VIg = A♭g and v♭VIg = vA♭g, v♯IVg = vF♯g and ^♯IVg = ^F♯g. The golden ii–V–I, for example, is Dm – Dg – vGg/D – G – vE♭T13/G – vCg/G – C (Chapter 29).

In SongMap (Chapter 36) the same symbols are typed in ASCII: Cg, Cg7, Cs, CT13, ^C, vEbm, C(v3,^5), vCg/G.

Western mirror. Chapter 9's chord symbols (inversions and voicing) carry over unchanged: every Western symbol is still valid in 36-ET, on any layer. The golden system only adds roots with arrows, four qualities (g, g7, s, T13) and bent chord tones.

From symbol to pitch classes. A symbol $R\,Q(\text{alts})/B$ denotes the set $\{\text{steps}(R) + q_i + a_i\}$, where $q_i$ are the quality's sixth-step offsets (Western qualities have offsets divisible by 3; g = (0, 11, 22), g7 = (0, 11, 22, 33), s = (0, 10, 20), T13 = (0, 13, 26)), $a_i$ is the alteration of chord tone $i$ (number of ^ minus number of v), and the bass $B$ is placed lowest.

Uniqueness of golden names. Because the 36 golden triads are distinct (first section), every golden triad has exactly one symbol $R$g with $R$ in standard spelling. Among the equal-third triads $T_n$ only the augmented triad has more than one root ($\{0, 12, 24\}$ is invariant under $+12$, so it has three); every other $T_n$ in the table, golden included, has exactly one.

Exercise 3. Show that $-T_{13} = T_{13}$ shifted, and find the shift.

Answer

$-\{0, 13, 26\} = \{0, 23, 10\} = \{10, 23, 36\} = 10 + \{0, 13, 26\}$: the shift is 10, the bronze triad on ^E♭.

Exercise 4. How many layers do C(^5), Cm(^3,v5) and Cg7 touch?

Answer

C(^5) = {0, 12, 22}: layers 0, 0, 1, so 2. Cm(^3,v5) = Cs = {0, 10, 20}: layers 0, 1, 2, so 3. Cg7 = {0, 11, 22, 33}: layers 0, 2, 1, 0, so 3.

Exercise 5 (listening). In the averaging example, is Cg closer in sound to C or to Cm? Compare with the distances (2 and 3 sixth-steps).

Answer

You may well hear Cg as nearer to C major: its third is one sixth-step below E and two above E♭, and its fifth one above G. The distances agree: 2 to C, 3 to Cm. (A listening judgement; your ear may differ.)
