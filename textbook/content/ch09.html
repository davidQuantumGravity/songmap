Triads

Play C, E and G together and you hear one thing, not three. That fused sound is a chord, and the three-note chord built this way is the most important object in Western harmony: the triad. Change a single note by a semitone and the character changes completely. Lower the E to E♭ and the bright chord turns dark. Lower the G as well and it turns tense and pinched. Put the E back and raise the G instead, and it floats, unresolved, as if it could go anywhere.

These four sounds are the major, minor, diminished and augmented triads. Every one of them is made of two thirds stacked on top of each other. Inside a major scale, stacking thirds on each note gives three major triads, three minor triads and one diminished triad, and those seven chords are the raw material of most of the music you know.

A third is an interval of three or four semitones: the minor third (3 semitones, 300 cents in 12-ET) and the major third (4 semitones, 400 cents). A triad is a root with a third above it and another third above that. Two kinds of third, two positions: four triads.

The names of the chord's notes come from their position in the stack: the root at the bottom, then the third and the fifth, named for their intervals above the root. These names stay with the notes even when the chord is rearranged, as the next section shows.

Why major and minor sound settled. The major triad sits in the harmonic series. Harmonics 4, 5 and 6 of any tone form a major triad in just intonation, 4 : 5 : 6 (Chapter 2). Its thirds are 5/4 = 386.31 c and 6/5 = 315.64 c, and its fifth is 3/2 = 701.96 c, so 12-ET's major third is 13.69 c wide of just and its minor third 15.64 c narrow. The minor triad is the major triad turned upside down: the same two thirds in the opposite order. In whole numbers it is 10 : 12 : 15, which uses larger numbers than 4 : 5 : 6. One common explanation of why minor sounds less simple than major starts from this. The diminished and augmented triads have no perfect fifth at all, and both sound unstable in tonal music.

Triads of a key. In the C major scale C D E F G A B, a third means "skip one note": C–E, D–F, E–G and so on. Three of these seven thirds are major (C–E, F–A, G–B) and four are minor. Stacking two of them on every note gives the diatonic triads, named by Roman numerals (upper case for major, lower case for minor, ° for diminished):

In the harmonic minor scale (C D E♭ F G A♭ B), the raised seventh changes the picture: the triads are i (Cm), ii° (Ddim), III+ (E♭aug), iv (Fm), V (G), VI (A♭) and vii° (Bdim). The augmented triad appears naturally in the minor mode, on the third degree of harmonic (and ascending melodic) minor.

Average the three "bright" triads on C, major, minor and augmented, note by note, and you land on a chord that none of them is: the golden triad Cg = C vE ^G, whose intervals approximate 1 : 2/φ : 4/φ² (a golden third of 366.91 c and a golden fifth of 733.82 c). Its third is two-thirds major and one-third minor; its fifth is two-thirds perfect and one-third augmented. See the golden triad and the averaging principle.

Definition. Write pitch classes as elements of $\mathbb{Z}_{12}$ (C = 0, C♯ = 1, …, B = 11; Chapter 8). A tertian triad with root $r$ is the set $\{r,\ r+a,\ r+a+b\}$ with $a, b \in \{3, 4\}$. The four choices $(a,b) = (4,3), (3,4), (3,3), (4,4)$ give the major, minor, diminished and augmented triads.

How many three-note chords are there? There are $\binom{12}{3} = 220$ sets of three pitch classes. Grouping sets that are transpositions of each other gives the transposition classes. Burnside's lemma counts them: average, over the 12 transpositions $T_k$, the number of sets each one fixes. $T_0$ fixes all 220. A three-note set fixed by $T_k$ with $k \ne 0$ must be a union of orbits of $T_k$, and an orbit has size $12/\gcd(k,12)$, so the orbit size must be 3, which forces $k = 4$ or $8$; each of these fixes exactly the 4 augmented triads. Hence $$\frac{220 + 4 + 4}{12} = 19$$ transposition classes. If inversion (mirroring, $p \mapsto -p$) is also allowed, the count drops to 12. (Both numbers were also checked by direct enumeration.) Of the 19 classes, exactly four are tertian.

Thirds in the diatonic scale. The diatonic scale's step pattern is 2 2 1 2 2 2 1 semitones. A diatonic third spans two consecutive steps, so it is 4 semitones (2 + 2) or 3 (2 + 1 or 1 + 2). Each of the two half steps (E–F and B–C) lies inside exactly two thirds, and no third contains both, because the half steps are three and four steps apart around the cycle. So four thirds are minor and three are major, as counted above. In cents: just major triad $1 : \tfrac54 : \tfrac32$, with $1200\log_2\tfrac54 = 386.31$ c and $1200\log_2\tfrac65 = 315.64$ c.

Exercise 9.1 (listening). Play the first example again with your eyes closed and name each chord as it sounds. Then describe, in one word each, the difference between the diminished and augmented triads.

Answer

The order is major, minor, diminished, augmented. Many listeners hear the diminished triad as tight or anxious (two small thirds, a tritone outside) and the augmented triad as floating or suspended (two large thirds, symmetric, with no obvious bottom). The words vary; the point is that both lack the perfect fifth of major and minor.

Exercise 9.2 (calculation). Use Burnside's lemma to count the transposition classes of four-note chords in $\mathbb{Z}_{12}$.

Answer

There are $\binom{12}{4} = 495$ four-note sets. A four-note set fixed by $T_k$ ($k \ne 0$) is a union of $T_k$-orbits of size $12/\gcd(k,12)$, which must divide 4: size 2 ($k = 6$, sets made of two tritones: $\binom{6}{2} = 15$ of them) or size 4 ($k = 3, 9$: the 3 diminished seventh chords). So the count is $(495 + 15 + 3 + 3)/12 = 516/12 = 43$. Direct enumeration agrees (43, or 29 if inversion is allowed).

Inversions and voicing

A chord keeps its identity when you rearrange it. Play C, E and G with C on the bottom, then with E on the bottom, then with G on the bottom: you still hear "a C major chord", but the floor under it changes. With C in the bass it feels grounded. With E in the bass it feels lighter, as if it were leaning forward. With G in the bass it feels propped up, waiting.

The second half of the example keeps C in the bass but spreads the other notes differently. Packed close together the chord is dense and a little muddy in a low register; spread out, with the wide gaps at the bottom, it opens up and rings.

The note in the bass decides the inversion:

Figured bass, the shorthand of Baroque keyboard players, writes the intervals above the bass instead of the chord's name. The slash symbol of popular music writes the chord and then its bass: C/E. Roman numerals take the figures as superscripts: I⁶ is C/E, I⁶₄ is C/G.

A voicing is one concrete arrangement of a chord: which octave each note goes in, which notes are doubled, and which instrument or voice takes which note. The common-practice habits for four voices (soprano, alto, tenor, bass) are these:

  • Doubling. Four voices and three notes means one note is doubled. In root position the root is the usual choice. The leading tone (the seventh degree, B in C major) is normally not doubled, because it has a strong tendency to rise and two voices rising together to the same note make parallel octaves (Chapter 14).
  • Spacing. Upper voices usually stay within an octave of each other, while the bass may be further away. This copies the harmonic series, whose low harmonics are far apart (octave, fifth, fourth) and whose high harmonics are close together.
  • Close and open. In close position the three upper voices fit within an octave; in open position they span more.

The six-four chord. In common-practice harmony (roughly 1650–1900) the second inversion was treated with care. The fourth between the bass and the root (G–C in C/G) counted as a dissonance against the bass, so a six-four chord usually appears in a few fixed patterns: as a cadential six-four before the dominant (Chapter 10), as a passing six-four (Chapter 12), or over a held bass.

The idea that C–E–G, E–G–C and G–C–E are "the same chord" was given its classic theoretical form by Jean-Philippe Rameau in his Traité de l'harmonie (1722). Rameau traced every chord to a root, and the succession of roots, his basse fondamentale (fundamental bass), became the backbone of later harmony teaching.
In 36-ET the golden six-four chord replaces the cadential six-four: over a held dominant bass G, the golden triad a sixth-step below the tonic, vCg = vC ^E♭ G, slides into C. Its fifth is the bass note itself. See the golden six-four.

Inversion as rotation. A triad in close position is determined by its bass and its step vector: the three intervals around the octave, starting from the bass, that add up to 12. For the major triad on its root the step vector is (4, 3, 5): C to E, E to G, G up to the next C. Moving the bass to the next chord tone rotates the vector: (3, 5, 4) for first inversion and (5, 4, 3) for second. The intervals above the bass are the partial sums:

Spacing and the harmonic series. The distances between consecutive harmonics, $1200\log_2\frac{n+1}{n}$, shrink as $n$ grows: 1200, 701.96, 498.04, 386.31, 315.64 c for $n = 1, \dots, 5$. A voicing with large intervals at the bottom and small ones at the top imitates this shape.

Seventh chords and beyond

Stack one more third on a triad and you get a seventh chord, four notes deep. Each kind has its own color. The major seventh is soft and glowing, the sound of a lot of jazz ballads and bossa nova. The dominant seventh is restless: it wants to move, and it almost always does. The minor seventh is mellow. The half-diminished is wistful. The diminished seventh is the stock sound of melodrama: four notes evenly spaced, with no home at all.

Keep stacking thirds through the scale and you run out of new notes after seven: C E G B D F A is the whole C major scale, piled up. Jazz harmony lives in this territory, choosing a few of those notes and leaving the rest out.

Three stacked thirds, each minor (3) or major (4), give $2^3 = 8$ stacks. One of them, major–major–major (4 + 4 + 4 = 12), lands back on the root an octave up, so it is not a new chord. The other seven are the seventh chords:

The dominant seventh and the harmonic series. Harmonics 4, 5, 6 and 7 form a dominant seventh chord in just intonation. The seventh harmonic, 7/4 = 968.83 c, is 31.17 c flatter than the 12-ET minor seventh (1000 c), so the 12-ET dominant seventh is only an approximation of that natural chord. Its instability in tonal music comes mainly from its tritone, the diminished fifth between the third and the seventh (B and F in G7), which Chapter 10 follows into its resolution.

Inversions of seventh chords have four positions, with the figures 7 (root position), 6/5 (third in the bass), 4/3 (fifth in the bass) and 4/2, or just 2 (seventh in the bass).

Extensions. Beyond the seventh, the next thirds are the ninth, eleventh and thirteenth: the second, fourth and sixth degrees an octave higher. On C in C major they are D, F and A. Two practical facts shape how they are used:

  • A full thirteenth chord contains all seven notes of the scale, so players almost never play every note. A common dominant thirteenth voicing keeps the root, the seventh, the third and the thirteenth (G F B E for G13) and drops the fifth, the ninth and the eleventh.
  • The natural eleventh sits a minor ninth above the major third (E to F in Cmaj11), a harsh clash, so over major and dominant chords jazz players usually omit it or raise it to ♯11.
Stack one more golden third on the golden triad and you reach the golden seventh Cg7 = C vE ^G B, approximating $1 : 2/\varphi : 4/\varphi^2 : 8/\varphi^3$. The exact golden seventh $8/\varphi^3$ is 1100.73 c; 36-ET renders it as 33 sixth-steps, which is exactly the 12-ET major seventh, so the exact value is 0.73 c sharper. See the golden seventh.

Proposition. In a seven-note scale, stacking thirds (two scale steps at a time) visits every degree before it repeats, and seven stacked thirds return to the root exactly two octaves up.

Proof. Number the degrees by $\mathbb{Z}_7$. A third adds 2. Since $\gcd(2,7) = 1$, the multiples $0, 2, 4, 6, 1, 3, 5$ are all distinct, and $7 \cdot 2 = 14 \equiv 0 \pmod 7$ returns to the start after 14 scale steps, which is two octaves. In the diatonic case the seven thirds are three major and four minor, and indeed $3\cdot 4 + 4 \cdot 3 = 24$ semitones = two octaves. $\square$

So the thirteenth chord is the end of tertian stacking in a diatonic scale: the next third would be the fifteenth, the root again.

The just dominant seventh. $4:5:6:7$ has intervals above the root of $0$, $386.31$, $701.96$ and $1200\log_2\tfrac74 = 968.83$ c. The tritone between harmonics 5 and 7 is $7/5 = 582.51$ c, against 600 c in 12-ET.

Chord space

Some chords are close neighbors and some are far apart, in a sense your ear can follow. From C major, move just one note by a semitone, G up to G♯, and you reach the augmented triad. From there, any one of its three notes can step up or down a semitone, and each move lands on a different major or minor triad. The augmented triad is like a hub: six familiar chords sit one tiny step away from it.

Chords as points. Write a three-note chord as a list of three pitches, one per voice: C major in close position is (60, 64, 67) in MIDI numbers. A voice leading from one chord to another moves each voice to a note of the next chord. Its size is the total motion in semitones. From C major (C E G) to A minor (C E A) the size is 2 (G up to A). From C major to E minor (B E G) it is 1 (C down to B).

Dmitri Tymoczko ("The Geometry of Musical Chords", Science, 2006; A Geometry of Music, 2011) showed that if you treat chords as points and voice leadings as straight-line paths, and identify chords that differ only by octaves or by which voice has which note, the result is a curved geometric space.

  • Two-note chords form a Möbius strip.
  • Three-note chords form a triangular prism whose two ends are glued together with a twist.
  • Chords with a doubled note (such as C C E) lie on the boundary.
  • The perfectly even chords, the augmented triads, run down the center.

Major and minor triads sit near the center, close to the augmented triads, which is why they can be joined by small voice leadings. Clusters such as C C♯ D sit near the edges.

Evenness measures how far a chord is from the perfectly even shape. Here it is the straight-line distance, in semitones, from the chord to the nearest perfectly even chord (a chord of the form $x$, $x+4$, $x+8$, where $x$ need not be a whole number). The values below were computed for this book with that definition:

The six neighbors. Each augmented triad is one semitone (in one voice) away from exactly three major and three minor triads, and each major or minor triad is one semitone away from exactly one augmented triad. The four augmented triads therefore split the 24 major and minor triads into four families of six. The smooth hexatonic cycles of Chapter 10 and Chapter 18 weave between two of these families, alternating a neighbor of one augmented triad with a neighbor of another.

36-ET fills in, and extends, the line through the diminished and augmented triads. Stacking two equal thirds of $n$ sixth-steps gives the chords $T_n = \{0, n, 2n\}$: diminished (9), silver (10), golden (11), augmented (12) and bronze (13), equally spaced points on one straight line of chord space. On the measure above, golden and bronze sit at 0.471 from perfectly even, closer than major and minor; silver sits at 0.943. See the equal-third line.

Definition (distance from even). Let a three-note chord have pitches $p_1 \le p_2 \le p_3 < p_1 + 12$ (in semitones, real numbers allowed). Its distance from evenness is $$\varepsilon(p) = \min_{\text{rotations}}\ \min_{x \in \mathbb{R}}\ \big\| (p_1, p_2, p_3) - (x, x+4, x+8) \big\|_2,$$ where a rotation replaces $(p_1, p_2, p_3)$ by $(p_2, p_3, p_1 + 12)$. The inner minimum is solved by subtracting the mean: with $d = p - (0, 4, 8)$, the distance is $\|d - \bar d\|$.

Examples. C major: $d = (0, 0, -1)$, $\bar d = -1/3$, residual $(1/3, 1/3, -2/3)$, so $\varepsilon = \sqrt{6}/3 \approx 0.816$. Diminished: $d = (0, -1, -2)$, residual $(1, 0, -1)$, $\varepsilon = \sqrt 2 \approx 1.414$.

Proposition (the equal-third line). For $T_n$ with thirds of $n$ sixth-steps (that is, $n/3$ semitones), $\varepsilon(T_n) = \sqrt 2\,|n - 12|/3$.

Proof. $d = (0,\ n/3 - 4,\ 2n/3 - 8) = (0, k, 2k)$ with $k = (n-12)/3$. The mean is $k$ and the residual is $(-k, 0, k)$, of length $\sqrt2\,|k|$. The other rotations give the same or larger values (checked numerically). $\square$

So $\varepsilon = 0.471$ for golden ($n = 11$) and bronze ($n = 13$), $0.943$ for silver ($n = 10$) and $1.414$ for diminished ($n = 9$). The exact golden triad $1 : 2/\varphi : 4/\varphi^2$ gives 0.468. These numbers depend on the metric chosen (Euclidean, in semitones); other metrics order chords slightly differently, so treat the table as a measurement, not a law.

Theorem (augmented neighbors; checked exhaustively). Moving one note of an augmented triad by $\pm 1$ semitone gives six chords, all major or minor: from Caug, E, C♯m, A♭, Fm, C and Am. Since there are 4 augmented triads, 24 major and minor triads, and $4 \times 6 = 24$, and no major or minor triad appears twice, each major or minor triad has exactly one augmented neighbor.

The symmetric chords

Two chords in the Western vocabulary have no single natural "bottom": the augmented triad and the diminished seventh chord. Because they divide the octave into equal parts, they sound the same however you rearrange them, and a composer can make the same four notes lead to completely different places. Listen to one diminished seventh chord resolving four times, each time to a different key.

An augmented triad divides the octave into three major thirds (4 + 4 + 4). Transposing it up a major third gives the same three notes: C E G♯ becomes E G♯ C. So there are only 4 different augmented triads, not 12:

Ambiguity as a resource. In C major (or minor), B D F A♭ is vii°7, and its B is the leading tone that rises to C. But any of its four notes can be heard as the leading tone. Respelled D F A♭ C♭, it is vii°7 of E♭; as F A♭ C♭ E𝄫 (or E♯ G♯ B D), vii°7 of F♯; as G♯ B D F, vii°7 of A. The four tonics it can resolve to, C, E♭, F♯ and A, themselves form a diminished seventh chord. This is the classic device of enharmonic modulation (Chapter 11). The augmented triad has a similar ambiguity: each of its three notes can move a semitone either way, so it can resolve to six different major or minor triads (previous section).

What about the diminished triad? It is not symmetric in this sense: its steps are 3 3 6, so all 12 transpositions are different. It is symmetric under mirroring: C E♭ G♭ turned upside down around E♭ gives itself. Every triad with two equal steps (diminished, augmented, and the suspended C F G with steps 5 2 5) has this mirror symmetry.

In 36-ET, the augmented triad $\{0, 12, 24\}$ is still a symmetric chord, while the golden triad $\{0, 11, 22\}$ has 36 different transpositions. Because 11 shares no factor with 36, stacking golden thirds walks through all 36 notes before it returns; the same is true of bronze (13), while minor thirds return after 4 notes, silver thirds after 18 and major thirds after 3. That is why the golden circle exists (Chapter 31, Chapter 33). A golden triad also touches all three 12-ET layers of 36-ET, one note on each (the layer theorem), while every Western chord lives on one layer.

Definition. A pitch-class set $S \subseteq \mathbb{Z}_{12}$ is transpositionally symmetric if $T_k(S) = S$ for some $k \not\equiv 0$, where $T_k(p) = p + k$.

Theorem. $T_k(S) = S$ if and only if $S$ is a union of cosets of the subgroup $\langle k \rangle = \langle \gcd(k,12) \rangle$. The number of distinct transpositions of $S$ is $12/|\mathrm{Stab}(S)|$, where $\mathrm{Stab}(S) = \{k : T_k(S) = S\}$.

Proof sketch. If $T_k$ fixes $S$, then so does every power $T_k^m = T_{mk}$, so $S$ is closed under adding elements of $\langle k\rangle$, which makes it a union of cosets. Conversely a union of cosets of $\langle k\rangle$ is fixed by $T_k$. The count is the orbit-stabilizer theorem for the group $\mathbb{Z}_{12}$ acting by transposition. $\square$

Corollary. Among tertian chords, the only transpositionally symmetric ones are the augmented triad and the diminished seventh. A symmetric three-note set is a union of cosets of a nontrivial subgroup, and the coset size must divide 3, so it is a single coset of the order-3 subgroup $\{0,4,8\}$. A symmetric four-note set is a union of cosets of a subgroup of order 2 or 4; both contain 6, so the set is fixed by $T_6$ and consists of two tritones. Of the seven seventh-chord qualities only the diminished seventh contains two tritones (the dominant seventh and the half-diminished contain one, the others none), leaving $\{0, 3, 6, 9\}$. The general theory of these sets, the modes of limited transposition, is in Chapter 19.

The four tonics of a diminished seventh. $S = \{11, 2, 5, 8\}$ (B D F A♭). Each $s \in S$ can be a leading tone to $s + 1$, giving tonics $\{0, 3, 6, 9\} = S + 1$: a transposition of $S$, hence again a diminished seventh.
