Debussy and the colors of symmetry

Around 1900 Claude Debussy (1862–1918) began to use chords for their color rather than their pull. Three techniques do much of the work, and you can hear each in a few bars. In this chapter the audio examples are short passages written for this book to show the technique, not quotations.

First, the whole-tone scale (Chapter 19). Parallel major thirds drift down by whole tones over a low pedal, and nothing resolves, because nothing in the scale can.

Second, the pentatonic scale: the five black keys, F♯ G♯ A♯ C♯ D♯. With no semitones there is no dissonance to resolve, and the melody can wander and stop anywhere.

Third, planing: a melody doubled by a chord that moves in parallel with it, like a line drawn with a broad brush. If every chord is the same exact shape, the planing is chromatic. If the chords follow the notes of a scale, it is diatonic.

Each technique dissolves a different part of the tonal system:

  • Whole-tone harmony removes the leading tone and the perfect fifth. Debussy's piano prelude "Voiles" (Préludes, Book I, 1909–10) is almost entirely whole-tone over a pedal B♭, with a short pentatonic middle section.
  • Pentatonic writing removes the semitone and the tritone, the two intervals that drive tonal resolution (the interval vector ⟨0, 3, 2, 1, 4, 0⟩, Chapter 20). "Pagodes" (Estampes, 1903) is pentatonic through long stretches. Debussy had heard Javanese gamelan at the Paris Exposition of 1889, and that encounter is often linked to such pieces.
  • Planing removes independent voice leading. Parallel fifths and octaves, forbidden in classical counterpoint (Chapter 14), become a sound in their own right, as in the parallel chords of "La cathédrale engloutie" (Préludes, Book I). Chromatic planing also leaves the key: parallel major triads on C, D, E♭ and F use F♯, E♭ and B♭ as well as the notes of C major.

What these techniques have in common is symmetry or near-symmetry. A whole-tone scale has no distinguished note. A pentatonic scale has a note that sounds central only when the music insists on it. Planing treats a chord as one object, so the same object can be put anywhere. Tonal function depends on asymmetry, such as the single leading tone and the single tritone of the major scale, and Debussy's colors come from removing it.

Olivier Messiaen took this further. His modes of limited transposition (Chapter 19) are systematic versions of Debussy's symmetric colors, and Messiaen described experiencing his chords and modes as colors.

"Impressionism". The label was borrowed from painting, and Debussy himself disliked it. It is used here only as a conventional name for a style period.
Golden mirror. Planing is natural in 36-ET too, where a golden triad can be planed by sixth-steps, a third of a semitone at a time. Golden planing is one of the "golden home" techniques of Chapter 31, and the symmetric scales of 36-ET, which offer many more floating colors, are in Chapter 33.

Planing as a group action. Chromatic planing of a chord $X$ along a melody $m_1, m_2, \dots$ plays $T_{m_i - m_1}(X)$: every chord is a transposition of the first, so its set class never changes. Diatonic planing inside a scale $D$ instead plays the image of $X$ under the scale's own step map $s: D \to D$, which shifts each note to the next note of the scale. That map is not a transposition of $\mathbb{Z}_{12}$, so it changes the chord quality as it goes: in C major, C → Dm → Em → F, major, minor, minor, major.

Why the whole-tone scale cannot cadence. The dominant-to-tonic cadence needs a semitone (leading tone to tonic) and a perfect fifth (root motion). The whole-tone scale has interval vector ⟨0, 6, 0, 6, 0, 3⟩, with no interval class 1 and no interval class 5. So neither ingredient is available inside it. This is a theorem, not a matter of taste.

Bartók and Stravinsky

Play a C major arpeggio and an F♯ major arpeggio at the same time. The two triads share no notes and lie a tritone apart, the farthest pair in the circle of fifths, yet together they sound bright and brittle rather than chaotic. This bitonal sound is associated with Stravinsky's ballet Petrushka (1911), and it is called the Petrushka chord. Then hear the octatonic scale that contains both triads.

Now a different idea, from analyses of Béla Bartók. Treat C, A, F♯ and E♭, the four roots a minor third apart, as members of one "tonic" family. Then any of the four dominant-family chords G7, D♭7, B♭7 and E7 can lead to C. Listen to the tonic family, and then to the four dominants each resolving to C.

Stravinsky and the octatonic

The Petrushka chord C–E–G + F♯–A♯–C♯ is the set {0, 1, 4, 6, 7, 10}. It lies inside the octatonic collection {0, 1, 3, 4, 6, 7, 9, 10}, and it is symmetric under $T_6$: transposing it by a tritone swaps the two triads. Arthur Berger (1963) and then Pieter van den Toorn (The Music of Igor Stravinsky, 1983) argued that the octatonic collection organizes large parts of Stravinsky's music of the 1910s, from Petrushka and The Rite of Spring (1913) onward. Their readings, like all analyses, are interpretations, and other scholars weigh the octatonic and diatonic elements in Stravinsky differently.

Bartók and the axis system

The Hungarian musicologist Ernő Lendvai proposed in the 1950s that Bartók's tonality is organized by three axes. Each is a diminished-seventh set of roots, treated as one function:

FunctionAxis (roots)As pitch classes
TonicC, E♭, F♯, A{0, 3, 6, 9}
DominantG, B♭, C♯, E{7, 10, 1, 4}
SubdominantF, A♭, B, D{5, 8, 11, 2}

The idea starts from the circle of fifths. Tonal theory already lets a relative minor stand in for its major (C and A), and the axis system extends this to every member of a diminished seventh. In each axis, the roots a tritone apart (C and F♯) are called the "poles". Lendvai's work also includes the golden-section analyses of Bartók's forms mentioned in Chapter 16.

Status. The axis system is an analytical interpretation due to Lendvai, not a method Bartók described. It has been both influential and criticized, for instance for how selectively it fits the music. Bartók's harmony also draws on folk modes, on the "acoustic" (Lydian–Mixolydian) scale and on octatonic and chromatic cells.
Golden mirror. The three axes are the three cosets of the subgroup {0, 3, 6, 9}: functional harmony reinterpreted by group theory, exactly as in Chapter 19. In $\mathbb{Z}_{36}$ the same subgroup {0, 9, 18, 27} has nine cosets, one for each layer and root family, and the golden circle of 36 golden triads (generated by 11, a unit) cuts across all of them (Chapter 33).

The axes as cosets. Let $D = \{0, 3, 6, 9\}$. The tonic, dominant and subdominant axes are $D$, $D + 7$ and $D + 5$, the three cosets of $D$, so they partition the twelve roots. Since $7 \equiv 1$ and $5 \equiv 2 \pmod 3$, the dominant axis is $D + 1 = D + 7$ and the subdominant is $D + 2 = D + 5$. So "dominant" and "subdominant" functions are the root classes $\equiv 1$ and $\equiv 2 \pmod 3$.

Why the substitutions are smooth (computed). Each dominant-axis seventh chord resolves to C with its upper three voices moving a total of at most 4 semitones in the voicings of the example: G7 → C moves F→E, B→C and holds G (2 semitones, plus the bass). D♭7 → C moves F→E, A♭→G, B→C (3 semitones), the familiar tritone substitution. B♭7 → C moves F→E, A♭→G, D→C (4 semitones). E7 → C moves D→E, G♯→G, B→C (4 semitones).

The Petrushka chord's symmetry. $T_6$ maps $\{0,4,7\} \cup \{6,10,1\}$ to $\{6,10,1\} \cup \{0,4,7\}$, itself. So it has 6 transpositions, and it is a union of the three tritone cosets $\{0,6\}, \{4,10\}, \{7,1\}$.

Clusters and texture

Now abandon chords made of separate intervals and play every note in a span at once: a tone cluster. Listen first to a chromatic cluster built up one semitone at a time, held, so that it becomes a single shimmering band of sound. Then hear two clusters struck like a forearm on a piano: all the white keys across two octaves, then all the black keys.

A held cluster no longer sounds like twelve notes. It sounds like a texture: a band with a top, a bottom, a density and a loudness. That shift, from harmony as notes to harmony as texture, is one of the main developments of 20th-century music.

The American composer Henry Cowell (1897–1965) named and explored the tone cluster. In piano pieces such as The Tides of Manaunaun (c. 1917) he played clusters with the fist, palm and forearm, and his book New Musical Resources (written around 1919, published 1930) gave them a theory. Charles Ives had already asked for a cluster pressed with a wooden board in his Concord Sonata.

In the 1960s György Ligeti (1923–2006) made the sustained cluster a whole musical world. In Atmosphères (1961) for orchestra and Lux aeterna (1966) for choir, dense clusters slowly widen, narrow and shift color. Ligeti called the dense canonic writing inside such textures micropolyphony: so many lines so close together that no single one can be heard. Krzysztof Penderecki's Threnody to the Victims of Hiroshima (1960) for strings uses quarter-tone clusters as massive blocks.

In set-theory terms, a cluster is the most chromatic set of its size, the prime form (0, 1, 2, …, n−1). Its interval vector is as lopsided as possible: for a cluster of $n \le 7$ consecutive semitones, the entry for interval class $k$ is $n - k$ (or 0 once $k \ge n$), so the small intervals dominate. For {0, 1, 2} the vector is ⟨2, 1, 0, 0, 0, 0⟩.

Golden mirror. In 36-ET the tightest cluster is three adjacent sixth-steps, C, ^C, vC♯, only 66.67 c wide. The multiplication $M_{11}$ maps exactly this cluster {0, 1, 2} onto the golden triad {0, 11, 22}, as $M_7$ maps the 12-ET cluster {0, 1, 2} onto {0, 2, 7}. In 36-ET, the densest sound and the golden chord are images of each other under a symmetry of the pitch-class circle (Chapter 33).

Clusters and multiplication. The chromatic cluster $C_n = \{0, 1, \dots, n-1\}$ is an arithmetic progression with difference 1. Any unit multiplier $M_k$ maps it to the progression $\{0, k, 2k, \dots, (n-1)k\}$ with difference $k$. In $\mathbb{Z}_{12}$, $M_7$ turns semitone clusters into stacks of fifths: $M_7(C_5) = \{0, 7, 2, 9, 4\}$, the pentatonic scale. In $\mathbb{Z}_{36}$, $M_{11}$ turns sixth-step clusters into stacks of golden thirds: $M_{11}(C_3) = \{0, 11, 22\}$ and $M_{11}(C_4) = \{0, 11, 22, 33\}$, the golden seventh.

So the white-key and black-key clusters of the audio are $M_7$-images of chromatic clusters. The black keys {1, 3, 6, 8, 10} are $M_7$ of the cluster {6, 7, 8, 9, 10} (F♯ to B♭, five consecutive semitones), and the white keys are $M_7$ of {11, 0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5} (B to F, seven consecutive semitones).

Spectralism: harmony from the overtone series

Take one low note, E1 at 41.20 Hz, and build a chord from its own overtones: the 2nd, 3rd, 4th, up to the 16th harmonic, each as a pure sine wave. Listen as they enter one by one from the bottom. At first you hear a chord. As the partials accumulate, they fuse into something closer to one rich tone, because that is what a harmonic spectrum is (Chapter 2). Then hear the same chord with each partial moved to the nearest key of a piano, and finally to the nearest quarter-tone.

Spectralism began in Paris in the 1970s with the composers of the ensemble L'Itinéraire, founded in 1973, above all Gérard Grisey (1946–1998) and Tristan Murail (born 1947). Their starting point was sound itself: the spectrum of an instrument, analyzed with the new tools of acoustics and computers, became the source of the harmony. Grisey's Partiels (1975), part of his cycle Les espaces acoustiques, is built from the spectrum of a low E on the trombone. Its harmonies are orchestrations of that note's partials, played by the instruments of the ensemble. Murail's Gondwana (1980) and Désintégrations (1982–83) followed. The label "spectral music" is usually credited to the composer Hugues Dufourt (1979).

Spectral composers also used inharmonic spectra, such as bells and the "distorted" or stretched spectra of electronic processing, and techniques from the electronic studio (frequency modulation, ring modulation, filtering) as harmonic models. The general principle is that timbre and harmony are one continuum, a theme of Chapter 17.

Golden mirror. Spectralism with golden spectra is a natural next step, though a purely electronic one: no acoustic instrument has such a spectrum. On a synthesized tone whose partials lie at products of powers of 2 and φ instead of at whole-number multiples, the golden triad is the smoothest chord, as major triads are among the smoothest on harmonic tones (in a roughness model: a measurement, not a theorem). See Chapter 26 for the timbres and Chapter 33 for golden spectral harmony.

The harmonic series in cents. Harmonic $k$ lies $1200 \log_2 k$ cents above the fundamental. Its deviation from 12-ET is $1200\log_2 k - 100 \cdot \operatorname{round}(12 \log_2 k)$. The deviation depends only on the odd part of $k$ (doubling adds exactly 1200 c), so harmonics 7 and 14 share −31.17 c, and 5, 10 share −13.69 c.

Fusion and periodicity. The exact harmonics $f_0, 2f_0, \dots, 16 f_0$ add up to a waveform that repeats every $1/f_0$ seconds, which the ear tends to hear as a single pitch $f_0$ (virtual pitch, Chapter 3). Rounding the partials to 12-ET destroys the common period: the rounded chord has no small common frequency, and its partials beat against each other. This is a physical statement about the signal. How strongly a listener hears fusion is a measurement and depends on level, register and listener.

Minimalism

Two players play the same short pattern over and over. One plays a tiny bit faster. At first you hear a single line with a slight echo. Then the echo becomes a blur, then a new composite pattern, then, after twelve repetitions, the two players line up again, one note apart, making a different interlocking melody. Nothing in the music changed except the timing, and yet the listening experience keeps changing.

Minimalism arose in the United States in the 1960s, partly as a reaction against the complexity of serial music. Its harmony is often simple, diatonic or modal, and slow to change. The interest moves to process, repetition and gradual transformation.

  • La Monte Young explored very long sustained tones from the late 1950s, often in just intonation.
  • Terry Riley's In C (1964) gives the performers 53 short figures, each repeated as many times as a player likes over a steady pulse on C, so that the figures overlap in ever-changing combinations.
  • Steve Reich discovered phasing with tape loops (It's Gonna Rain, 1965) and transferred it to live players in Piano Phase (1967). Music for 18 Musicians (1974–76) builds a large form from a cycle of slowly changing chords.
  • Philip Glass developed additive processes, in which a figure grows or shrinks by one note at a time. His opera Einstein on the Beach (1976) is a landmark.

Harmonically, minimalism largely returns to the consonant, centered world that the earlier chapters of this Part had moved away from. Its novelty is in time: harmony that changes so slowly that each chord becomes an environment rather than an event. Young's work with just intonation also connects minimalism with the microtonal tradition of the next section.

Golden mirror. Slow, repetitive textures are where small tuning differences become audible. A held golden chord on harmonic tones beats at rates tied to the golden ratio (Chapter 26), and a minimalist process that moves a pattern by sixth-steps would make 36-ET's layers audible one by one (the layers as cosets of the 36-note system are in Chapter 33). Whether such beating has special effects on listeners is a hypothesis, discussed in Chapter 34.

The arithmetic of phasing. Let the pattern have $p$ notes, player 1 play one note every $d$ seconds, and player 2 every $d' = d(1 - \varepsilon)$. After $N$ notes player 2 is ahead by $N d \varepsilon$ seconds. The players realign, shifted by one note, when $N d\varepsilon = d$, i.e. after $N = 1/\varepsilon$ notes. In the example $p = 8$ and $\varepsilon = 1/96$, so the shift is complete after 96 notes of the faster player (twelve repetitions of its pattern, 14.25 s, while the slower player has played 95 notes). In between, the relative offset passes through every fraction of a note, and the composite rhythm changes continuously.

With an eight-note pattern there are eight aligned positions (offsets 0 to 7 notes). Reich's procedure lingers on each one, so a phasing piece is a walk through the cyclic group $\mathbb{Z}_p$ of offsets, with continuous transitions between its elements.

The microtonal pioneers

Here is one whole tone, from C to D, divided four ways: into 2 semitones (the piano), into 4 quarter-tones, into 6 sixth-tones, and into 16 sixteenth-tones. Then a C major triad, the same triad a quarter-tone higher, and both at once, the sound of two pianos tuned a quarter-tone apart.

And here is a scale with no equal steps at all: Harry Partch's 43 notes to the octave, every one a whole-number ratio. Listen for the uneven, close spacing, and then for one of the chords he built from it, harmonics 4, 5, 6, 7, 9 and 11 of one fundamental.

Golden mirror. Busoni's sixth-tones are exactly 36-ET, the grid on which the golden system lives. Busoni reached it by subdividing the whole tone. The golden system reaches it by a different road: 36 golden sixths nearly close to 25 octaves, so 36-ET closes the golden chain into a circle, as 12-ET closes the chain of fifths (Chapter 24). The chromatic and serial methods of this Part, transplanted to that grid, are Chapter 33.

Which equal divisions contain 12-ET? An $n$-ET contains every 12-ET pitch exactly when 100 c is a whole number of its steps, i.e. when $12 \mid n$. So 24-ET (quarter-tones), 36-ET (sixth-tones), 48-ET and 96-ET (sixteenth-tones) all contain the piano, but 18-ET (third-tones alone) does not: its step is 66.67 c, and the semitone 100 c is $1\tfrac12$ steps. This is why Busoni combined two third-tone series a semitone apart. The union of $18$-ET and its shift by 100 c is $\{0, 1, 2, \dots\}\cdot \tfrac{1200}{36}$, which is 36-ET.

Proof of the union. In 36-ET units, 18-ET is the even steps $\{0, 2, 4, \dots, 34\}$, and the shift by a semitone adds 3 steps, giving the odd steps $\{3, 5, \dots, 35, 1\}$. Together, all 36. $\square$

Partch's symmetry. If $r$ is in the scale then so is $2/r$, so the scale is its own inversion about 1/1 and the octave. Its steps are unequal: from 14.37 c to 38.91 c (computed). Like any just scale, it cannot be transposed freely: moving 3/2 up by 81/80 does not land on a member of the scale.

Exercise 22.1. Show that the Petrushka chord {0, 1, 4, 6, 7, 10} lies inside exactly one octatonic collection, and name it.

Answer

The octatonic collections are $D\cup(D+1)$, $D\cup(D+2)$, $(D+1)\cup(D+2)$ with $D = \{0,3,6,9\}$. The chord contains 0, 6 ∈ D and 1, 4, 7, 10 ∈ D + 1, so it lies in $D \cup (D+1) = \{0,1,3,4,6,7,9,10\}$ and in no other (both others miss either $D$ or $D+1$).

Exercise 22.2. In Lendvai's system, to which axis does a D7 chord belong, and why does D7 → C not count as a dominant resolution?

Answer

D is 2 ≡ 2 (mod 3): the subdominant axis {D, F, A♭, B}. So D7 → C is subdominant to tonic, a plagal-type motion in this reading.

Exercise 22.3. Which harmonic of E1 is the first that lies more than 10 cents from a 12-ET pitch, and by how much?

Answer

The 5th: G♯3, −13.69 c (the just major third 5/4 is 13.69 c flatter than 12-ET's).

Exercise 22.4. In the phasing example, how long does it take (in seconds) for player 2 to get one note ahead?

Answer

Player 2's notes last $0.15 \times 95/96 = 0.1484$ s. The two line up again, one note apart, when player 2 has played 96 notes and player 1 only 95: after $95 \times 0.15 = 14.25$ s.

Exercise 22.5. Does 30-ET (fifth-tones) contain 12-ET? Does it contain 36-ET's sixth-tone?

Answer

No to both: 12 does not divide 30 (100 c is 2.5 steps of 40 c), and 36 does not divide 30 (33.33 c is not a multiple of 40 c).
