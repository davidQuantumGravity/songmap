Z36: the structure of 36 pitch classes

Part V treated the twelve pitch classes as a little number system, "Z12", clock arithmetic with twelve hours, and found in its arithmetic the symmetric scales, set classes and twelve-tone rows of twentieth-century music (Chapter 19, Chapter 20). This chapter does the same for 36-ET, whose pitch classes form a clock with 36 hours, "Z36". That number system is richer: it contains 12-ET three times over, once on each layer, and it holds structures that Z12 cannot, among them the golden triad and the golden chain.

Start with the equal divisions hidden inside 36. Some are old friends on the 12-ET layer; two are new, and they cannot stay on one layer.

A pitch class of 36-ET is a number of sixth-steps above C, counted mod 36: C = 0, ^C = 1, vC♯ = 2, C♯ = 3, and so on. Its layer is its remainder mod 3: 0 for the 12-ET notes, 1 for ^, 2 for v (Chapter 24).

Subgroups: the equal divisions inside 36

A subgroup is a set of pitch classes closed under adding and subtracting, which musically is an equal division of the octave. Because $\mathbb{Z}_{36}$ is cyclic, it has exactly one subgroup for each divisor of 36: orders 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 9, 12, 18 and 36.

Multiplication and the units

In $\mathbb{Z}_{12}$ the multiplications by 5 and 7 are the interesting symmetries beyond transposition and inversion (Chapter 20). In $\mathbb{Z}_{36}$ the numbers that can serve as multipliers, the units, are those sharing no factor with 36:

1, 5, 7, 11, 13, 17, 19, 23, 25, 29, 31, 35.

Multiplying every pitch class by a unit $u$ is a symmetry of the whole system, written $M_u$. Two quick rules describe what it does to layers and to the 12-ET notes:

  • If $u$ leaves remainder 1 when divided by 3 (1, 7, 13, 19, 25, 31), $M_u$ keeps every layer in place. If the remainder is 2 (5, 11, 17, 23, 29, 35), $M_u$ swaps the ^ and v layers.
  • On the 12-ET layer $M_u$ acts like the 12-ET multiplication by $u$ mod 12. So $M_{13}$, $M_{25}$ and $M_1$ all fix every 12-ET note; $M_{11}$, $M_{23}$, $M_{35}$ act on 12-ET notes as inversion; $M_{7}$, $M_{19}$, $M_{31}$ act as Western $M_7$.

Interval vectors and the Z-relation

An interval class in 36-ET is a distance from 0 to 18 sixth-steps, so a set's interval vector has 18 entries (Chapter 20). Two sets are Z-related if they have the same interval vector without being transpositions or inversions of each other (Chapter 20). An exhaustive search finds no Z-related trichords in $\mathbb{Z}_{36}$ (as in $\mathbb{Z}_{12}$) and exactly four Z-related pairs of tetrachords:

Theorem (subgroups). The subgroups of $\mathbb{Z}_{36}$ are $d\mathbb{Z}_{36}$ for $d\mid 36$, of order $36/d$. $d\mathbb{Z}_{36}\subseteq 3\mathbb{Z}_{36}$ iff $3\mid d$ iff the order divides 12. Units. $\mathbb{Z}_{36}^\times = \{u: \gcd(u,36)=1\}$ has $\phi(36)=12$ elements; $M_u: x\mapsto ux$ is an automorphism exactly when $u$ is a unit. The orders of the units are 1 (for 1), 2 (17, 19, 35), 3 (13, 25) and 6 (5, 7, 11, 23, 29, 31), so $\mathbb{Z}_{36}^\times\cong\mathbb{Z}_2\times\mathbb{Z}_6$ (computed; by the Chinese remainder theorem it is $\mathbb{Z}_4^\times\times\mathbb{Z}_9^\times$).

Layer action. $ux \bmod 3 = (u\bmod 3)(x \bmod 3)$, so $M_u$ fixes the layers if $u\equiv1$ and swaps layers 1 and 2 if $u\equiv 2 \pmod 3$. On $3\mathbb{Z}_{36}$, $u\cdot 3q = 3\,(uq \bmod 12)$, which is $M_{u \bmod 12}$ of $\mathbb{Z}_{12}$.

The golden transform M11

In 12-ET, multiplying by 7 turns the chromatic scale into the circle of fifths. It turns the cluster C C♯ D into C D G, three adjacent fifths. In 36-ET, multiplying by 11 does the same thing for the golden system: it turns the tightest possible cluster, C ^C vC♯ (three notes a sixth-step apart), into the golden triad. Apply it again and the golden triad becomes the bronze triad; a third time, and you are back to a cluster.

From clusters to chains

$M_{11}$ sends 1 to 11, so it sends the sixth-step ladder 0, 1, 2, 3, … to 0, 11, 22, 33, …, the roots of the golden circle (Chapter 31). Three adjacent sixth-steps become three adjacent golden-circle notes: $M_{11}\{0,1,2\} = \{0,11,22\}$ = Cg. This is exactly parallel to 12-ET, where $M_7$ sends 0, 1, 2, … to the circle of fifths and $M_7\{0,1,2\} = \{0,2,7\}$.

The parallel continues to scales. In 12-ET the diatonic scale is $M_7$ of a seven-note chromatic cluster. In 36-ET, $M_{11}$ maps the seven-note sixth-step cluster B ^B vC C ^C vC♯ C♯ (−3 to +3) onto golden7, and the thirteen-note cluster (−6 to +6) onto golden13. The golden scales are the clusters of 36-ET seen through the golden transform.

The golden transform's own arithmetic

  • Order 6. The powers of 11 mod 36 are 11, 13, 35, 25, 23, 1. So $M_{11}^2 = M_{13}$ (the bronze transform) and $M_{11}^3 = M_{35}$, which is the inversion $x\mapsto -x$ about C. In 12-ET, $M_7$ undoes itself after two steps; the golden transform needs six.
  • Cluster → golden → bronze → cluster. Since $M_{11}^2 = M_{13}$, applying $M_{11}$ to Cg gives $\{0,13,26\}$, bronze, and applying it to bronze gives $\{0,35,34\}$, a cluster again. $M_{13}$ runs the same cycle backwards: cluster → bronze → golden (vFg) → cluster.
  • Layers. $11\equiv 2 \pmod 3$, so $M_{11}$ swaps the ^ and v layers and keeps the 12-ET layer. On 12-ET notes it acts as inversion about C: C major {C, E, G} goes to {C, A♭, F}, F minor; C minor goes to F major.
  • Interval classes. $M_{11}$ leaves every 12-ET interval class (3, 6, 9, 12, 15, 18 sixth-steps) where it is and cycles all the others in threes: 1 → 11 → 13 → 1, 2 → 14 → 10 → 2, 4 → 8 → 16 → 4, 5 → 17 → 7 → 5. In particular it permutes the sixth-step, the golden third and the bronze third.
What $M_{11}$ is not: it does not turn golden13 into silver13. silver13 is golden13 with ^ and v swapped while every 12-ET note stays put, and no map of the form $x\mapsto ux + t$ does that (exhaustive check over all 12 units and 36 transpositions). The layer mirror is a symmetry of note names, not of the group.

Symmetric scales in 36

A scale of limited transposition is one that comes back to itself when transposed by less than an octave, like the whole-tone scale or the octatonic scale (Chapter 19). 36-ET has far more of them than 12-ET, and some can be built from golden triads. Here are two: the golden triad copied up by major thirds, and copied up by minor thirds.

Counting them

A set has period $p$ if $p$ is the smallest number of sixth-steps that transposes it onto itself. It then has exactly $p$ distinct transpositions, and it is a union of cosets of the subgroup generated by $p$. The number of sets with a given period depends only on $p$, so 36-ET contains the 12-ET counts for small periods and adds new ones:

The Western symmetric scales reappear on each layer: a whole-tone scale on the 12-ET layer has period 6 in 36-ET, the octatonic period 9, and so on. Two kinds of symmetry are new. Period 3 means invariance under a semitone: such a set is a union of whole layers (one layer, or two). Periods 2 and 4 come from 18-ET and 9-ET, which mix layers. Messiaen's seven modes are a handful of the fifteen symmetric classes of 12-ET; in 36-ET the field is about a thousand times larger.

Answer

36-tone rows and the golden chain

A twelve-tone row orders all twelve pitch classes, each once (Chapter 21). A 36-tone row orders all 36. The most natural one in the golden system is the golden chain itself: start on C and keep moving by a golden sixth (or, folded into one octave, either up a golden sixth or down a golden third). After 36 steps you have played every pitch class of 36-ET exactly once.

The row is P = (0, 25, 14, 3, 28, 17, 6, 31, 20, 9, 34, 23, …), that is, pitch class $25j$ mod 36 for $j = 0, 1, \dots, 35$:

C ^A♭ vF · C♯ ^A vF♯ · D ^B♭ vG · E♭ ^B vA♭ · E ^C vA · F ^C♯ vB♭ · F♯ ^D vB · G ^E♭ vC · A♭ ^E vC♯ · A ^F vD · B♭ ^F♯ vE♭ · B ^G vE

Its properties, all checked by computation:

  • Constant interval. Every step is the same, 25 sixth-steps, a golden sixth. It is the opposite of an all-interval row.
  • Golden everywhere. Every three consecutive notes form a golden triad (all 34 overlapping windows). The twelve groups of three (the discrete trichords, separated by dots in the list above) are the golden triads vFg, vF♯g, vGg, …, vEg, whose roots lie on the v layer and rise by semitones. Each trichord plays its triad from the top down: fifth, third, root.
  • Layers in rotation. The layers run 0, 1, 2, 0, 1, 2, …. Taking every third note gives three ascending chromatic scales, one on each layer: C C♯ D …; ^A♭ ^A ^B♭ …; vF vF♯ vG …. The row is three chromatic scales interleaved, because three golden sixths make two octaves and (very nearly) a semitone.
  • Few forms. A row normally has 4 × 36 = 144 forms (transpositions of P, I, R and RI). For the golden chain row, rotating it by one note is the same as transposing it by 25, and its retrograde is a transposed inversion, R = T₁₁I. Only 72 distinct forms exist.
  • Combinatorial halves. The first 18 notes, transposed by a tritone (18 sixth-steps), give exactly the last 18. So do the first 18 inverted by $x\mapsto 11-x$. In twelve-tone language the row is combinatorial at the tritone and under one inversion (Chapter 21).

All-interval rows

An all-interval row uses every interval exactly once between consecutive notes (Chapter 21). For 36-ET that means the 35 successive intervals are 1, 2, …, 35 in some order. They exist; one is the "zigzag" 0, 35, 1, 34, 2, 33, 3, …, whose intervals are 35, 2, 33, 4, 31, 6, …, all different (checked). As in 12-ET, any all-interval row begins and ends a tritone apart (here 18 sixth-steps; the zigzag ends on F♯). How many all-interval 36-rows exist, and whether any of them has golden properties such as golden-triad trichords, we have not determined: the search space is far too large for the simple enumeration used for 12-ET.

Golden serialism

Serialism organizes music by orderings of the pitch classes and the operations that transform them (Chapter 21). A golden serialism would use the golden system's own orderings and operations. Nothing of the kind exists yet as a repertoire; this section collects proposals. The first is a way to turn any ordinary twelve-tone row into a golden 36-tone row: replace each pitch class by the golden triad on it.

Status: proposals. The constructions below are mathematically exact (each claim was verified by computation), but their musical value is untested. No body of golden serial music exists to learn from.

Proposal 1: golden derivation

The twelve golden triads whose roots lie on the 12-ET layer (Cg, C♯g, Dg, …, Bg) never share a note, and together they contain every pitch class of 36-ET exactly once. The same is true of the twelve golden triads with roots on the ^ layer and of the twelve with roots on the v layer. So the 36 golden triads fall into three layer families, each a partition of the 36 notes. Consequently any twelve-tone row becomes a 36-tone row by replacing each pitch class $p$ by the golden triad on $p$, in any order of its three notes. The example uses the row C B G A♭ E♭ C♯ D B♭ F♯ F E A and plays each triad upward.

The golden chain row of the previous section is itself such a derivation: it is the chromatic scale vF, vF♯, vG, … with each note replaced by the golden triad on it (v-layer family), played fifth–third–root.

Proposal 2: M11 as a row operation

Pitch-class set theory adds the multiplications $M_5$ and $M_7$ to transposition and inversion (Chapter 20). Golden serialism can add $M_{11}$. Applied to a row it keeps the row a row (it permutes the pitch classes), turns sixth-step motion into golden-third motion and golden thirds into bronze thirds, leaves every 12-ET interval class unchanged, and swaps the ^ and v layers. A row heard through $M_{11}$, $M_{11}^2 = M_{13}$ and $M_{11}^3 = I$ gives a family of six related rows (the powers of $M_{11}$).

Proposal 3: layer as a serial dimension

Every pitch class has a layer (0, ^ or v), so a row has a layer sequence, as it has a register or dynamic sequence. The golden chain row rotates its layers 0, 1, 2, …; a golden-derived row has the pattern of the triads chosen. Transposing by one sixth-step, $T_1$, rotates all layers at once. A composer can treat layer color, the "prism" of Chapter 31, as a serial parameter.

Proposal 4: timbre as a serial dimension

Because consonance depends on timbre, the same row segment can be stable on golden-spectrum tones and tense on harmonic ones (Chapter 30). Assigning timbres to row segments, harmonic for some and golden for others, would let a serial piece control tension through spectrum rather than through pitch alone.

Exercise 4. Prove that Cg and C♯g share no note, and that Cg and vEg share two. What is the general rule for when two golden triads overlap?

Answer

$G_r$ and $G_s$ meet when some $r + a \equiv s + b$ with $a, b\in\{0,11,22\}$, i.e. when $s - r\in\{0, \pm 11, \pm 22\} = \{0, 11, 14, 22, 25\}$. C♯g is $s - r = 3$, not in the list, so no overlap; vEg is $s-r = 11$: overlap of two (vE and ^G). None of 11, 14, 22, 25 is a multiple of 3, so two triads with roots on the same layer never overlap, which proves the partition claim of Proposal 1.

Proof: by the exercise, $G_r\cap G_s\ne\varnothing$ iff $s-r\in\{0, 11, 14, 22, 25\}$; for $r\equiv s$ only $s=r$ qualifies. Twelve disjoint 3-sets fill 36 classes. ∎

Corollary. If $(p_1,\dots,p_{12})$ is a twelve-tone row, then the concatenation of $(3p_i, 3p_i+11, 3p_i+22)$ is a 36-tone row (verified for the example).

Rows under $M_u$. $M_u$ is a bijection for each unit $u$, so it maps rows to rows; it maps the interval succession $(i_1, \dots, i_{35})$ to $(u i_1,\dots, u i_{35})$.

Golden spectralism

The spectral composers of the 1970s, Gérard Grisey and Tristan Murail among them, built harmony from the overtone series itself: the partials of a low tone, analyzed and re-orchestrated as chords (Chapter 22). A golden spectralism would start from a golden spectrum instead. Listen to one golden-spectrum tone, then its ten partials entering one at a time as pure sines, then those same ten frequencies played as a chord, first on harmonic tones and then on golden-spectrum tones.

Finally, the timbre continuum between the two worlds. A golden triad and a just major triad each pass through a slow glide of their partials from harmonic to golden.

Golden chords are thinned golden spectra

Play an exact close Cg (1 : 2/φ : 4/φ²) on these ten-partial golden tones. Its 30 partials fall on only 20 distinct frequencies, and all of them are of the form $2^m\varphi^n$ times the root frequency: the chord is itself a piece of one golden spectrum. In chain voicing (1 : φ : φ²) the 30 partials fall on 16 frequencies. A just C major triad on the same tones has 30 distinct partials and none of them coincide. On harmonic tones the situation reverses: a just C major triad's 30 partials (ten per tone) fall on 24 frequencies, while Cg's stay 30. This is the spectral reason behind the timbre flip: a chord is smooth on a timbre when the chord is a fragment of that timbre's own spectrum (theorem for the coincidence counts, which are exact; the link to smoothness is the Sethares model, a measurement).

The partials' difference tones are golden too, though not all of them. Of the 55 pairwise differences among the eleven golden partials below 9 used in Chapter 26, 34 are again of the form $2^m\varphi^n$, including all ten differences between neighbouring partials (for example $\varphi^2 - 2 = \varphi^{-1}$ and $4 - 2\varphi = 2\varphi^{-2}$). Some are not: $4 - 1 = 3$ is not.

Proposals for a golden spectral practice

Status: proposals. These extend spectral technique to golden spectra. They are untested compositionally.
  • Spectral chords. Orchestrate the partials of a golden spectrum as chords, as in the example. In 36-ET they are chain pitches within a cent; in exact tuning they are exact.
  • Spectral progressions along the chain. Move the fundamental by chain steps. The ten-partial spectra on C and on the note a golden third above (ratio 2/φ) share five partial frequencies exactly, so neighbouring golden spectra overlap the way neighbouring golden triads do.
  • Timbre interpolation. Treat the path from harmonic to golden spectra as a dimension of the music, as in the second example. For the exact chords of that example the Sethares model gives the golden triad 0.503 on the harmonic tone and 0.638 on the golden tone, and the just major triad 0.432 and 0.826 (eight partials at amplitude $k^{-1.2}$; measurement). The order of the two chords flips during the glide.
  • Golden combination-tone fields. Use the exact chain voicing, whose difference tones fall exactly on the chain (Chapter 26), as a self-generating spectral texture.

Exercise 5. Show that $\varphi^3 - 4 = \varphi^{-3}$ and $2\varphi^2 - 4 = 2\varphi^{-1}$, two neighbouring-partial differences of the golden spectrum.

Answer

Use $\varphi^2 = \varphi + 1$, so $\varphi^3 = 2\varphi + 1$, and $\varphi^{-1} = \varphi - 1$. Then $\varphi^{-3} = (\varphi-1)^3 = \varphi^3 - 3\varphi^2 + 3\varphi - 1 = (2\varphi+1) - 3(\varphi+1) + 3\varphi - 1 = 2\varphi - 3 = \varphi^3 - 4$. And $2\varphi^2 - 4 = 2(\varphi + 1) - 4 = 2(\varphi - 1) = 2\varphi^{-1}$.

Partial set. $S_{10}$ = the ten smallest elements of $\{2^m\varphi^n: m,n\ge 0\}$ $= \{1, \varphi, 2, \varphi^2, 2\varphi, 4, \varphi^3, 2\varphi^2, 4\varphi, \varphi^4\}$ (the instrument code takes $m, n < 8$ and sorts). Their pitch classes are $c(n)$ for $n = 0,\dots,4$ since multiplying by $2^m$ is octave equivalence.

Coincidences. For a chord $\{1, 2/\varphi, 4/\varphi^2\} = \{1, 2\varphi^{-1}, 4\varphi^{-2}\}$, every product with an element of $S_{10}$ is $2^a\varphi^b$, and two products coincide exactly when their exponent pairs agree (since $2^a\varphi^b = 2^{a'}\varphi^{b'}$ forces $b=b'$, $\varphi$ being irrational, and then $a=a'$). Counting distinct pairs gives 20; for the chain voicing $\{1,\varphi,\varphi^2\}$, 16. A just triad $\{1, 5/4, 3/2\}$ times $S_{10}$ has no coincidences, because $5/4$ and $3/2$ are not of the form $2^a\varphi^b$.

Neighbouring spectra. $S_{10}\cap (2/\varphi)S_{10}$ has 5 elements (computed: $2, 2\varphi, 4, 2\varphi^2, 4\varphi$).
