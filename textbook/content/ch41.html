How to read this chapter. Parts VI and VII were mostly theorems and measurements. This chapter is different: it is about the idea that started the whole project, that golden sound might help the mind into a calm, open state, and that idea is a conjecture. Every claim below is labelled. A theorem is proved or checked exhaustively; a measurement depends on a model, a timbre or a register; a hypothesis or conjecture is an idea to be tested, not a fact. Nothing here is medical advice, and none of the audio promises any state of mind.

Binaural beats and entrainment

Two pure tones a few hertz apart, played together, make a single tone whose loudness swells and fades: a beat (Chapter 3). The first half of the example below is exactly that, 200 Hz and 206 Hz mixed together, beating six times a second. You can hear it on loudspeakers.

The second half needs headphones. Now the 200 Hz tone goes only to the left ear and the 206 Hz tone only to the right. Neither ear receives anything that beats: each ear hears one steady sine. Yet most listeners still hear a faint flutter, softer and more "inside the head" than the first one, at the same six per second. That flutter is a binaural beat. It is made by the brain, from the comparison of the two ears.

Level and safety. Each example with binaural content starts with a two-second reference tone. Set a comfortable, moderate volume on it; everything after it is 12 dB softer. Binaural beats are heard only on headphones. If you have epilepsy or are sensitive to rhythmic stimulation, be cautious with any sustained pulsing sound, and never pair it with flashing visuals.

The next example stacks three binaural beats. Three pure tones in the golden-chain ratios 1 : φ : φ² each arrive slightly differently in the two ears, so that the lowest flutters ten times a second, the middle about six, the top about four. Their rates are themselves in golden ratio: 10, 6.18, 3.82. The voices enter one at a time.

Monaural and binaural. A monaural beat exists in the sound wave itself: two tones of frequencies $f$ and $f+\Delta$ add up in the air, and their sum rises and falls in loudness $\Delta$ times a second. A binaural beat does not exist in any sound wave. Each ear receives a steady tone. What changes, $\Delta$ times a second, is the relation between the ears: the right ear's wave slides ahead of the left ear's wave, a full cycle every $1/\Delta$ seconds. Neurons in the brainstem that compare the timing of the two ears are thought to follow this slow slide, and we hear it as a flutter. The figure shows the two ears' signals and, for contrast, what their sum would look like if both reached one ear.

Binaural beats are a well-documented perceptual effect. They were described by Heinrich Wilhelm Dove in 1839 and made widely known by Gerald Oster's article "Auditory beats in the brain" in Scientific American (1973). They are faint compared with monaural beats, they are heard most clearly with carrier tones of a few hundred hertz and differences of a few hertz, and they fade as the carrier rises toward a kilohertz and beyond. That is why the examples use carriers between 200 and 600 Hz.

Entrainment, the hypothesis. The popular claim is stronger: that a binaural beat at, say, 10 Hz entrains the brain, pulling its electrical rhythms (measured by EEG, next section) toward 10 Hz, and with them a mental state. Two different questions hide in that claim, and the evidence for them differs.

This book takes the cautious reading. Binaural beats are real percepts, they can be designed precisely, and their effects on mind and brain are modest and contested. In the golden project they are a design parameter for listening experiments, not a treatment.

The golden beat ladder. The second example uses a design from the bliss hypothesis (§41.4): beat rates $B,\ B/\varphi,\ B/\varphi^2$, here 10, 6.18 and 3.82 Hz. Its one exact property is self-similarity: the difference of two neighbouring rates is the next rate down, $10 - 6.18 = 3.82$. So the rhythm of the rhythms is again on the ladder. Whether that matters to a listener is exactly the kind of thing §41.5 shows how to test.

Monaural beats (theorem). By the sum-to-product identity,

$$\sin(2\pi f t) + \sin(2\pi (f+\Delta) t) = 2\cos(\pi\Delta t)\,\sin\!\big(2\pi (f+\tfrac{\Delta}{2}) t\big).$$

The sum is a tone at the mean frequency $f+\Delta/2$ inside the envelope $2|\cos(\pi\Delta t)|$, which has period $1/\Delta$: the loudness swells $\Delta$ times per second.

Binaural beats (theorem about the signals). With $x_L(t) = \sin(2\pi f t)$ and $x_R(t) = \sin(2\pi(f+\Delta)t)$ each ear's envelope is constant. The interaural phase difference is

$$\theta(t) = 2\pi(f+\Delta)t - 2\pi f t = 2\pi\Delta t \pmod{2\pi},$$

which turns through a full cycle every $1/\Delta$ seconds. Any physiological response to $\Delta$ must therefore come from a binaural comparison of timing, since there is no amplitude modulation to respond to in either ear.

The mirrored detune. The golden-ladder example sends $f-\Delta/2$ to the left and $f+\Delta/2$ to the right, so that the perceived pitch stays at $f$ while the difference is $\Delta$.

The ladder is self-similar (theorem). Dividing $\varphi^2 = \varphi + 1$ by $\varphi^{k+2}$ gives $\varphi^{-k} = \varphi^{-k-1} + \varphi^{-k-2}$. Hence, for rates $\Delta_k = B\varphi^{-k}$,

$$\Delta_k - \Delta_{k+1} = \Delta_{k+2}.$$

With $B = 10$ Hz: $\Delta_0 = 10$, $\Delta_1 = 6.1803$, $\Delta_2 = 3.8197$, $\Delta_3 = 2.3607$ Hz.

The golden-ratio EEG hypothesis

The brain's electrical activity, recorded from the scalp, is full of rhythms: slow waves in deep sleep, a famous rhythm near ten per second when you rest with your eyes closed, faster ones when you concentrate. Those rates are far below the range of pitch. As sound, they are pulsations. The example lets you hear four of them as the pulsing of a soft tone: about four, six, ten and sixteen pulses per second. They were not chosen at random. Each is the previous one times the golden ratio, the series a published hypothesis uses to describe how the brain keeps its rhythms apart.

EEG rhythms. The electroencephalogram (EEG) records voltage fluctuations at the scalp produced by large populations of neurons firing in step. Hans Berger reported the first human EEG, with its rhythm near 10 Hz, in 1929. By convention the rhythms are sorted into bands. The boundaries vary between laboratories, and a person's peak frequencies vary with age, state and task, so the table gives typical values only.

The hypothesis. Pletzer, Kerschbaum and Klimesch (2010, Brain Research), in a paper titled "When frequencies never synchronize: the golden mean and the resting EEG", argued that the center frequencies of neighbouring EEG bands relate by a ratio near φ, and that this is functional: because φ is the number least well approximated by fractions, rhythms in golden ratio are the least prone to accidental cross-frequency synchronization, so the brain can keep separate processes separate and couple them only when it needs to. Roopun and colleagues (2008, Frontiers in Neuroscience) had reported ratios near φ between coexisting cortical rhythms. Both are hypotheses about how brain rhythms are organized. They are cited here because they inspired the project, not because they are established; band definitions are conventions, and alternative accounts of the spacing of brain rhythms exist.

What this book can say for certain is the mathematical part: two periodic processes in golden frequency ratio never fall back into step, and, in the sense of Hurwitz's theorem, their near-returns to step are the least exact of any pair of rhythms. That is the Derive layer.

A bridge, not a proof. Even if brain rhythms are spaced by φ, nothing follows yet about music. Sound at 6 Hz is not a brain rhythm at 6 Hz, and a chord in golden ratios is not a brain in golden ratios. The bridge from one to the other is the conjecture of §41.4.

Realignment (theorem). Let two oscillators have frequencies $f_1$ and $f_2 = r f_1$. If $r = p/q$ in lowest terms, then after time $T = q/f_1$ the first has made $q$ cycles and the second $p$, so the joint pattern repeats with period $T$, and no shorter period exists. If $r$ is irrational there is no common period at all: the pair is quasi-periodic.

Near-realignment (Hurwitz's theorem, 1891). Irrational $r$ still comes close to fractions. For every irrational $r$ there are infinitely many fractions $p/q$ with

$$\left| r - \frac{p}{q} \right| < \frac{1}{\sqrt5\, q^2},$$

and the constant $\sqrt5$ cannot be increased for $r = \varphi$ (or any number equivalent to it). In this precise sense φ is the irrational number worst approximated by fractions: its near-realignments are the rarest and the least exact. The Fibonacci ratios are its best approximations, and $q^2\,|\varphi - p/q|$ converges to $1/\sqrt5 = 0.44721$:

Even spreading (three-gap theorem). Sample the second oscillator's phase each time the first completes a cycle: the phases are $\{n r\} = nr \bmod 1$. By the three-gap theorem (Chapter 6) the first $N$ phases cut the circle into gaps of at most three sizes. For $r = 1/\varphi$ and $N$ a Fibonacci number there are exactly two, in ratio φ: for $N = 8$ the gaps are 0.0902 and 0.1459 of a cycle; for $N = 13$, 0.0557 and 0.0902. The golden phases fill the circle as evenly as a sequence can, never clustering where a rational ratio would pile them up.

The link from these theorems to brains is the hypothesis of Pletzer, Kerschbaum and Klimesch. The theorems themselves are certain; their relevance to EEG is not.

Quasi-periodicity and KAM theory

Rhythm makes the idea audible. In the example a low pulse ticks steadily while a high pulse ticks a little faster: first 3 high ticks for every 2 low ones, then 5 for 3, then 8 for 5. Each of these patterns comes back around: you can hear the moment when both pulses land together, every 2, then every 3, then every 5 low ticks. The ratios 3/2, 5/3, 8/5 are ratios of Fibonacci numbers, and they close in on φ. In the last section the ratio is φ. The two pulses start together and never coincide again. Nothing repeats; the pattern keeps unfolding.

The same holds for pitches. A drone tuned to exact powers of φ never settles into a repeating waveform. The suite's reference drone walks the golden circle in exact golden ratios on a golden-spectrum tone.

Periodic and quasi-periodic. A sum of tones whose frequencies are whole-number multiples of a common frequency repeats exactly: it is periodic. A sum of tones whose frequency ratios are irrational never repeats; it is quasi-periodic. Quasi-periodic is not random. Every ingredient is perfectly regular, and the whole is perfectly determined; it simply has no period.

Fibonacci tuning versus exact φ. The golden chain on 55 Hz, $55\varphi^k$, lies astonishingly close to the Fibonacci numbers:

In a short excerpt the two are indistinguishable: they differ by a hundredth of a hertz or so. Over minutes the difference is a breath-slow drift of 25 to 120 seconds. Periodicity as such is therefore not something the ear hears directly; what the ear hears are the beats and roughness that the frequencies create. Keep that in mind in §41.4.

Locking. When oscillators interact, nearby rational ratios attract them. Christiaan Huygens noticed in 1665 that two pendulum clocks hanging from the same beam fell into step. In general, coupled oscillators tend to mode-lock onto a rational frequency ratio p/q, and the ratios with small denominators have the widest basins of attraction. A ratio far from every simple fraction is the hardest to capture.

KAM theory. The mathematics of which motions survive a perturbation is the theory of Kolmogorov (1954), Arnold (1963) and Moser (1962), called KAM theory. It concerns conservative systems such as planetary orbits: if an idealized system moves quasi-periodically, on what mathematicians call an invariant torus, then after a small perturbation most of those quasi-periodic motions survive, namely those whose frequency ratios are "sufficiently irrational". Motions with rational or nearly rational ratios are the first to break into resonance and chaos. As the perturbation grows, more and more tori break. John Greene (1979) studied the best-known test case, the standard map, and gave numerical evidence that the last torus to break is the one with the golden ratio, at a coupling of about 0.9716.

History. Andrey Kolmogorov announced the persistence theorem in 1954; Vladimir Arnold (1963) and Jürgen Moser (1962) gave the proofs that made it a theory. Greene's 1979 paper introduced the "residue criterion" used in the Derive layer and established the golden-mean torus as the most robust in the standard map.

What this does and does not imply. KAM theory and Greene's result are about deterministic dynamical systems. They give a precise meaning to "φ is the frequency ratio most resistant to locking". They say nothing about brains, listeners or moods. The bliss conjecture borrows the image, coherence without locking; it does not inherit the theorem.

The standard map. On the cylinder with angle $x$ (mod 1) and momentum $p$, one step of the (Chirikov–Taylor) standard map with strength $k$ is

$$p_{n+1} = p_n + \frac{k}{2\pi}\sin(2\pi x_n), \qquad x_{n+1} = x_n + p_{n+1}.$$

For $k=0$ every orbit rotates uniformly with rotation number $\omega = p$. KAM theory says that for small $k$ the orbits with Diophantine rotation numbers, those with $|\omega - p/q| \ge c/q^{\tau}$ for all fractions, survive as invariant curves. Hurwitz's theorem shows that, for $\tau = 2$ and large $q$, φ (equivalently $1/\varphi$) admits the largest possible constant: the products $q^2|\varphi - p/q|$ of its best approximations converge to $1/\sqrt5$, and no irrational number keeps them larger in the limit. That is why it is the natural candidate for the last survivor.

Greene's residue criterion (measurement, numerical). Approximate the golden rotation number by the Fibonacci ratios $F_{j-1}/F_j$ and find the corresponding periodic orbits. For an orbit of period $q$ with monodromy matrix $M$ (the Jacobian of $q$ steps), the residue is $R = (2 - \operatorname{tr} M)/4$. Greene's criterion: the golden torus exists if the residues of the approximating orbits tend to 0, and is broken if they grow without bound; at the threshold they approach a constant near 1/4. The bliss conjecture

Here is the sound the conjecture is about. A golden seventh in close voicing, tuned exactly, on a plain harmonic tone. Hold still and listen past the chord to its inner motion: several soft, interlocking pulsations, each at its own rate, none of them ever lining up with the others in a steady pattern. The rates are fixed by the register. The first time, the chord is placed so that its slowest beat is exactly ten per second; the second time, lower, the beats slow to between six and twelve per second.

Where the idea came from. As the Preface tells, the project began with a claim the author heard: that bliss occurs when the brain's rhythms stand in golden ratio to each other. A neuroscientist he asked dismissed it, pointing out that rational, harmonic relations between rhythms are what the brain falls into in pathological hypersynchrony. (Epileptic seizures do involve abnormally excessive or synchronous neural activity; that much is standard neurology.) The author turned the objection around. If rational relations invite locking, perhaps the most irrational relations invite something else: activity that stays coherent without ever locking.

A second thread is personal. In the author's model of how golden harmony feels, the golden triad is a local minimum of tension: a delicate, metastable home one can dwell in, while C major remains the global minimum and the ultimate release. He also reports that extended listening to golden harmony changes how he feels. That is a personal observation, and it is the motivation for the conjecture below, not evidence for it. Measurement does not settle it either way: on harmonic tones, the spectra of all physical instruments, the standard roughness and entropy models show no local minimum at the golden triad (C major is smoother), and only on the digital golden spectrum is the golden triad a strict local minimum (Chapter 26).

The conjecture, stated honestly. This is the author's hypothesis, and this book states it as one:

Bliss conjecture (hypothesis). Sound whose structure is golden at several scales at once, with pitches in golden ratios, beat rates in golden ratios and (optionally) a digital golden-spectrum timbre on which golden chords are smooth, and which contains no steady rational pulse, tends to be experienced as calmer and more absorbing than matched sound built on rational ratios. The proposed mechanism is "coherence without locking": a structured, self-similar, never-periodic stimulus that invites attention without entraining it.

Design principles that follow. If one wants to test the conjecture, the music must embody it cleanly (Chapter 31 gives the harmonic vocabulary):

  1. Golden at every scale. Pitches in φ ratios (chain voicing or close golden chords), beat rates in φ ratios (from the close-voicing beat spectrum or a binaural ladder), section lengths in Fibonacci numbers of bars (8, 13, 21).
  2. No rational lock. No drums or strict metric pulse in the sections under test; the motion comes from the beats and from slow walks around the golden circle.
  3. Golden timbres for the sustained chords, where golden chords are acoustically smooth. These spectra exist only digitally (no physical instrument has them), so they are a laboratory condition, to be compared with the same music on harmonic tones.
  4. Safety and honesty. Moderate levels, no strong infrasound, binaural layers optional and on headphones only, no flashing visuals, and descriptions that describe the sound rather than promise a state.

An honest difficulty. §41.3 showed that periodicity itself is inaudible over short spans: the Fibonacci-integer drone and the exact golden drone sound the same for minutes. Whatever a listener responds to, it is not the abstract irrationality but its audible consequences: which beats occur, at which rates, how rough the sound is. A good test must therefore name the audible variable it changes.

Placing the beat band (theorem, harmonic partials). For the exact close golden seventh on root $f_0$, the beat rates are

$$b_1 = f_0\varphi^{-6},\quad b_2 = \tfrac{2}{\varphi}b_1,\quad b_3 = \tfrac{4}{\varphi^2}b_1,\quad b_4 = 2b_1,$$

where $\varphi^{-6} = 13 - 8\varphi = 0.055728$. The first three come from the Fibonacci approximant 13/8 of φ in the partials of neighbouring chord tones; the fourth from $8/\varphi^3 = 2 - 2\varphi^{-6}$, the seventh against the root's octave. To put the slowest beat at a chosen rate $B$, take $f_0 = B\varphi^6$, with $\varphi^6 = 17.944$:

The conjecture as a statistical hypothesis. Let $X$ be a golden stimulus and $Y$ a matched rational stimulus that differs from $X$ in one named variable. For a rating scale $s$ (calm, interest, pleasantness, absorption) let $D_s = s(X) - s(Y)$ for a listener. The conjecture predicts $\mathbb{E}[D_{\text{calm}}] > 0$ and $\mathbb{E}[D_{\text{absorption}}] > 0$; the null hypothesis is $\mathbb{E}[D_s] = 0$. A preference that disappears when listeners do not know which stimulus is which counts against the conjecture, not for it.

How to test it

Try a test on yourself. The example contains two excerpts, A and B, separated by a pause. Both are the same golden triad in exact ratios, the same register, the same length, the same loudness. Exactly one thing differs between them. Before reading the answer below, listen once, then rate each excerpt from 1 to 7 on four scales: how calm, how interesting, how pleasant and how absorbing. Then decide which one you would rather keep listening to.

A single listener's impression, the author's included, is where every idea starts and where none can end. A test that could change one's mind needs a few disciplines, all standard in listening research.

  1. Blind. The listener must not know which excerpt is golden. Better, the person running the session should not know either (double-blind): a script assigns the labels and keeps the key.
  2. One variable at a time. Each pair differs in exactly one named property. If timbre and tuning both change, a preference cannot be attributed to either.
  3. Matched everything else. Same length, same envelope, same register (unless register is the variable) and the same integrated loudness. Louder usually wins a comparison, so loudness must be matched by measurement (ITU-R BS.1770 loudness, which SongMap's QA computes; Chapter 44), not by ear.
  4. Randomized and counterbalanced order. Half the trials present the golden excerpt first. Order effects are large in listening tests.
  5. Fixed scales, decided in advance. Ratings from 1 to 7 for calm, interest, pleasantness and absorption, plus a forced choice. Decide the scales and the analysis before collecting data, and write them down (pre-registration).
  6. Catch trials. Some pairs are identical. If listeners report differences there, the reported differences elsewhere are partly noise.
  7. Several listeners, sessions and days. And report every result, including the null ones.
  8. No suggestion. Do not tell listeners the hypothesis, and do not call any excerpt "blissful".

The forced choice as a sign test. Over $n$ independent blind trials, let $K$ be the number in which the golden excerpt is preferred. Under the null hypothesis each trial is a fair coin, $K \sim \mathrm{Binomial}(n, 1/2)$, and the one-sided p-value of an outcome $k$ is

$$P(K \ge k) = 2^{-n}\sum_{i=k}^{n}\binom{n}{i}.$$

Ratings. For each scale, the paired differences $D_s$ can be tested with the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. With four scales tested, a simple Bonferroni correction uses $\alpha = 0.05/4 = 0.0125$ for each.

Exercises

Exercise 41.1 (calculation). A binaural ladder starts at 8 Hz and divides by φ at each rung. Give the first four rates, and check that each is the difference of the two above it.

Answer

8, 8/φ = 4.944, 8/φ² = 3.056, 8/φ³ = 1.889 Hz. Indeed 8 − 4.944 = 3.056 and 4.944 − 3.056 = 1.889, because $\varphi^{-k} = \varphi^{-k-1} + \varphi^{-k-2}$.

Exercise 41.2 (calculation). At what root $f_0$ does an exact close-voiced Cg7 have its slowest beat at 8 Hz, and what are its four beat rates?

Answer

$f_0 = 8\varphi^6 = 143.55$ Hz. The beats are 8.00, 8·2/φ = 9.89, 8·4/φ² = 12.22 and 16.00 Hz.

Exercise 41.3 (listening). In the blind pair of §41.5, which excerpt did you rate calmer? Which one is on the golden spectrum?

Answer

A is on the golden spectrum (partials $2^m\varphi^n$), B on the harmonic spectrum (partials 1, 2, 3, …). Under the Sethares roughness model the golden triad is the smoothest of the triads compared on the golden spectrum, while on the harmonic spectrum it is rougher than major, minor and augmented (Chapter 26). The model ranks chords within one timbre; it does not say which of the two excerpts is smoother overall. Your rating is one data point; the protocol of §41.5 needs many, blind and counterbalanced. Note that this exercise was not counterbalanced: A was golden for every reader.

Exercise 41.4 (proof). Show that if $f_2/f_1 = p/q$ in lowest terms, the two oscillators realign first after exactly $q$ cycles of the first.

Answer

They are in step at time $t$ when both $f_1 t$ and $f_2 t$ are integers. If $f_1 t = m$ then $f_2 t = mp/q$, an integer exactly when $q \mid mp$, i.e. (since $\gcd(p,q) = 1$) when $q \mid m$. The smallest positive $m$ is $q$.

Exercise 41.5 (design). In a test of 20 blind forced-choice trials a listener prefers the golden excerpt 14 times. Is that significant at the one-sided 5% level? What would you do next?

Answer

No: $P(K \ge 14) = 0.0577$ for $n = 20$; 15 would be needed ($P = 0.0207$). Next: decide in advance on a larger number of trials (about 37 for 80% power if the true rate were 0.70) and more listeners, keep the analysis fixed, and report the result whatever it is.
