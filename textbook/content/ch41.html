Organum and the perfect consonances

Start with a single line of melody, sung by one voice or by many in unison. For centuries this was the sacred music of the Latin West: plainchant, often called Gregorian chant, which is monophonic, a single line with no harmony at all. Now add a second voice that shadows the first a perfect fourth below, moving in strict parallel. Then try the same with a perfect fifth. The result is bright, hollow and very stable. Nothing in it beats quickly: the two voices seem to merge into one richer voice.

This is parallel organum, the earliest written polyphony in European music. It is worth hearing with fresh ears, because it shows what "consonant" meant at the start of the story: the octave, the fifth and the fourth, and nothing else.

The first written polyphony. The anonymous treatise Musica enchiriadis and its companion Scolica enchiriadis, from the 9th century, describe a principal voice (vox principalis) carrying the chant and an added voice (vox organalis) a fourth or a fifth below it. Either voice could be doubled at the octave, giving three- and four-voice textures built only from octaves, fifths and fourths. The treatise also describes a modified organum at the fourth, in which the added voice holds a note instead of moving in parallel, to avoid the augmented fourth (the tritone) that strict parallel fourths would produce at certain points of the scale.

Chant and organum. Parallel fourths and fifths belong to early medieval organum (9th–10th centuries), not to Gregorian chant itself, which was sung in unison. Over the following centuries organum grew freer: voices moved in contrary and oblique motion, and by around 1200 the composers of the Notre-Dame school in Paris, Léonin and Pérotin, wrote elaborate organa over long-held chant notes. But for the whole period the stable sonorities, the places where music could rest, remained the octave, fifth, fourth and unison.

Why these intervals. Medieval theory, inherited from Greek writers through Boethius (early 6th century), held that consonance comes from simple ratios: the octave 2:1, the fifth 3:2, the fourth 4:3, all made from the numbers 1, 2, 3, 4. Acoustics agrees, for harmonic tones (Chapter 3): in a 3:2 fifth every third partial of the lower note coincides with every second partial of the upper, and the partials that do not coincide are far apart. Nothing is left close enough to beat.

The last column of numbers is a common rough measure of a ratio's complexity, the logarithm of numerator times denominator (sometimes called Tenney height). The three perfect consonances are the three simplest ratios after the unison.

Coincident partials (theorem, harmonic tones). Let two harmonic tones have fundamentals $f$ and $(p/q)f$ with $\gcd(p,q)=1$. Partial $m$ of the lower tone and partial $n$ of the upper coincide exactly when $mf = n(p/q)f$, i.e. when $m = pj$ and $n = qj$ for some integer $j \ge 1$. Among partials up to $N$, the number of coincidences is $\lfloor N/\max(p,q)\rfloor$. With eight partials per tone: the octave has 4 coincidences, the fifth 2, the fourth 2, the just major third 5:4 one, and the Pythagorean third 81:64 none.

The example is in 12-ET. Its fourths and fifths are 1.96 c away from 4:3 and 3:2, so the nearly coinciding partials drift slowly: for the fifth D4–A4, $3 \cdot 293.665 - 2 \cdot 440 = 0.99$ Hz, an undulation of about once a second, far below the speed heard as roughness. Medieval singers, tuning by ear, would have sung them pure.

The Pythagorean third's beat. In 81:64 the nearest pair is partial 5 of the lower tone and partial 4 of the upper, which differ by

$$4\cdot\frac{81}{64}f - 5f = \frac{f}{16}.$$

At $f = 220$ Hz that is a 13.75 Hz beat, a clear roughness; the ratio of the two partials is the syntonic comma $81/80$ (21.51 c). Parallel thirds in Pythagorean tuning cannot fuse the way fourths and fifths do.

The arrival of thirds

The first example plays the same major third three ways on a steady organ tone: as Pythagorean tuning makes it (the ratio 81:64), as the harmonic series makes it (5:4), and as a modern piano makes it (12-ET). Then the same for the minor third. The Pythagorean thirds buzz quickly, the just thirds are still, and the equal-tempered thirds sit in between, beating gently.

The second example is in the style of a 15th-century technique: a melody in the top voice, a second voice a sixth below it, and a middle voice a fourth below the top. Every sonority is a third with a sixth above it, a sound of gliding sweetness quite unlike the open fifths of organum, until the cadence opens out onto an octave with a fifth.

Thirds as imperfect consonances. By the 13th century, theorists classed major and minor thirds as imperfect consonances: acceptable in passing, not stable enough to end on. In Pythagorean tuning that verdict is also what the ear reports, since its thirds beat (§41.1).

England. English sources of the 13th to 15th centuries make unusually free use of thirds and sixths, in two-voice singing in parallel thirds and in the style historians call English discant. In the early 15th century the music of John Dunstable (c. 1390–1453), with its full, consonant sound rich in thirds and sixths, was admired on the Continent. A contemporary French poem by Martin le Franc praised Guillaume Du Fay and Gilles Binchois for adopting the contenance angloise, "the English manner", a phrase usually taken to mean this sound, though exactly what it referred to is debated.

Fauxbourdon. In the 15th century, continental composers, Du Fay among them, used fauxbourdon: a melody harmonized by a voice a sixth below, with an unwritten middle voice sung a fourth below the melody. The texture is a chain of sixth-three sonorities, as in the example. Cadences still closed on the octave and fifth.

Thirds explained, and tuned. In 1558 Gioseffo Zarlino's Le istitutioni harmoniche gave thirds a theoretical home: the just ratios 5:4 and 6:5, built from the numbers 1 to 6 (his senario). Meantone temperaments, described in the same era (Chapter 7), narrowed the fifths slightly so that thirds became pure or nearly so. Over the 16th and 17th centuries the full triad became the normal final sonority, and by 1722 Jean-Philippe Rameau's Traité de l'harmonie could build an entire theory of harmony on the triad.

The syntonic comma separates the two thirds. The Pythagorean major third is four fifths minus two octaves, $(3/2)^4/4 = 81/64$; the just third is $5/4$. Their ratio is

$$\frac{81/64}{5/4} = \frac{81}{80}, \qquad 1200\log_2\frac{81}{80} = 21.51\ \text{c}.$$

Likewise $\dfrac{6/5}{32/27} = \dfrac{81}{80}$: the Pythagorean minor third (294.13 c) is a syntonic comma narrower than the just one (315.64 c).

Sevenths, ninths and beyond

Once the triad was home, the frontier moved up the stack of thirds. Listen to a C major triad grow: add a minor seventh (the sound of a dominant seventh, pulling forward), change it to a major seventh (softer, suspended), add a ninth (lush). Then a short progression in the harmonic language of jazz: a minor ninth chord, a dominant chord with a thirteenth, and a major ninth chord to finish. Chords that a Renaissance musician would have heard as a pile of dissonances now sound, to most listeners raised on popular music, simply warm.

The harmonic series has its own seventh, lower than the piano's. The next example plays a seventh chord tuned as harmonics 4, 5, 6 and 7 of A2, and then the 12-ET dominant seventh on the same root.

Sevenths. In Renaissance counterpoint a seventh above the bass occurred as a passing tone or a prepared suspension, always resolving. During the 17th century the dominant seventh became a chord in its own right, and in Rameau's theory (1722) it is the model of a dissonant chord that demands resolution (Chapter 9).

Ninths and beyond. 19th-century composers used ninth chords increasingly freely; around 1900 Debussy and his contemporaries let ninth chords and other extended sonorities move in parallel as colors rather than as tensions to resolve (Chapter 22). By the bebop era of the 1940s, jazz harmony treated ninths, elevenths and thirteenths as normal chord tones; today they are everyday sounds in jazz, film and popular music.

The fit is loose, not a law. The fourth is near the bottom of the ladder, yet in common-practice harmony a fourth above the bass is treated as a dissonance; and the harmonic seventh 7/4, low on the ladder, is not in 12-ET at all. Complexity is one ingredient of consonance; context and habit are others.

The harmonic seventh chord is beat-free (theorem, harmonic tones). The chord tones $4g, 5g, 6g, 7g$ are harmonics of one frequency $g$, so every partial of every tone is a whole-number multiple of $g$, and every difference between two partials is a multiple of $g$. In the example $g$ = A2 = 110 Hz (the chord is 440, 550, 660, 770 Hz): two partials either coincide or are at least 110 Hz apart, so there is no slow beat. In the 12-ET dominant seventh the seventh is 31.17 c sharp of 7/4 and the third 13.69 c sharp of 5/4, and slow beats appear.

Acculturation

Here is a sound many listeners would call "out of tune" on first hearing and others hear as the whole point. Two struck metal bars are first played in exact unison; then one is tuned a little higher and the other a little lower, so that together they shimmer, beating six times a second. Some musical traditions build their instruments in pairs precisely to get this shimmer.

And here is a sound almost everyone raised with Western music accepts without noticing: the major third of a piano, which beats. On middle C the 12-ET major third beats about ten times a second. We have learned not to hear it as wrong.

Nature and nurture in consonance. Some ingredients of consonance are physical and shared by all listeners: roughness from close partials (Chapter 3) and the fusion of harmonic tones. Whether a listener likes those properties is another question. Cross-cultural studies of consonance preference exist, and they suggest a large learned component. In a widely discussed 2016 study in Nature, Josh McDermott and colleagues tested the Tsimane', a society in the Bolivian Amazon with little exposure to Western music. They rated consonant and dissonant chords as about equally pleasant, while groups with more exposure to Western music showed progressively stronger preferences for consonance. Other research, including studies of infants, has been read as showing some early preference for consonance, and the balance between innate and learned components remains debated.

Traditions that value beating. Balinese gamelan instruments are often made in pairs tuned slightly apart, so that the pair shimmers; the beating is called ombak, "wave". Some vocal traditions of the Balkans cultivate close seconds sung together. These are not mistakes; they are aesthetic choices, learned and loved within a culture.

Familiarity. In psychology the mere-exposure effect (Robert Zajonc, 1968) describes the general tendency to like things more after repeated exposure. It is one plausible reason the Western ear accepted the beating thirds of equal temperament: they arrived with the piano and the convenience of playing in every key, and generations grew up hearing them as normal.

What this means for golden tones. If consonance were purely a matter of simple ratios, golden intervals, built on the least rational number, would be ruled out forever. The evidence of history and of cross-cultural research says otherwise: what sounds right is partly physics and partly habit. That leaves room for a new color to become familiar. It does not show that it will.

The beating piano third (theorem, harmonic tones). For a 12-ET major third on root $f$, the nearest partial pair (5 of the root, 4 of the third) beats at

$$\left|4\cdot 2^{1/3} - 5\right| f = 0.039684\,f .$$

On C3 (130.81 Hz) that is 5.19 Hz; on C4 (261.63 Hz), 10.38 Hz; it doubles with each octave. The 12-ET third is 13.69 c wider than 5:4.

A way to organize the hypotheses. Write a listener's pleasantness rating of a sound as a combination of a sensory part, which depends on the spectrum (roughness, harmonicity), and a learned part, which depends on the listener's history (familiarity, style). The models of Chapters 3 and 26 compute only the sensory part, and for a given timbre they rank chords the same way for every listener. The cross-cultural results suggest that how much the sensory part matters to liking varies with exposure. Golden chords score differently on the sensory part depending on timbre (Chapter 26); their learned part, for almost every listener today, starts at zero.

A next color

Here is the claim this book has been building toward, in sound. First a plain cadence, C – F – G – C, the most familiar progression in Western music. Then the same cadence again, but each move now passes through golden chords that the voice leading itself picks out: the bass and inner voices glide through steps a third of a semitone wide on the way to the next familiar chord. The Western chords are still the homes. Between them, for a moment, there is a color no Renaissance or Romantic composer could have used.

The pattern so far. Each widening of Western harmony admitted intervals that are, in one sense or another, more complex: from 3:2 to 5:4, from triads to sevenths, from the harmonic series to the tempered elevenths of jazz. Each time the new sound was first heard in passing, between familiar ones, before it became a place to rest. The perfect fourth and fifth were the homes of organum; thirds passed between them, and later became homes themselves.

The golden step is different in kind. Golden intervals are not more complex rationals; they are irrational, and no simple ratio is close to them. But they are not alien to the old intervals either. Their best rational approximations start exactly where Western harmony lives:

That is the precise sense in which golden chords are "almost Western, and then not": they are recognizably related to the familiar chords, and yet no simple ratio captures them. It also explains a measurement from Chapter 26: the golden fifth is more ambiguous than 69% of the octave's intervals in the harmonic-entropy model, the highest of the intervals compared there, sitting between the attraction of 3/2 and nothing else simple.

Golden chords as averages. The averaging principle (Chapter 25) gives the same relation in harmony: the golden triad is the average of major, minor and augmented, and a golden passing chord is a 2/3 : 1/3 blend of two familiar chords. A listener hears it as a mixture of known sounds, which is how every new harmony has entered the ear: in passing, between two homes.

The hope, stated as a hope. The author's view is that golden tones can become a next color, extending Western harmony rather than replacing it: first as passing chords, where their instability is an asset, and in time, perhaps, as places to dwell. In the author's model the golden triad is a local minimum of tension, a delicate, metastable home, while C major remains the global minimum and the ultimate release. That is a model and a listening experience, not a measurement: on harmonic tones, which is to say on every physical instrument, the standard roughness and entropy models show no local minimum at the golden triad. Only on the digital golden spectrum, a laboratory timbre, is it a strict local minimum (Chapter 26). Whether listeners will come to love golden chords is not a theorem. It is the open question that the rest of this Part tries to make answerable, with clean sound (Chapter 43) and fair tests (Chapter 40).

Continued fractions of the golden intervals (theorem). Since $\varphi = (1+\sqrt5)/2$,

$$\frac{2}{\varphi} = \sqrt5 - 1, \qquad \frac{4}{\varphi^2} = 8 - 4\varphi, \qquad \frac{8}{\varphi^3} = 8\sqrt5 - 16 .$$

With $\sqrt5 = [2; 4, 4, 4, \dots]$, the golden third is $2/\varphi = [1; 4, 4, 4, \dots]$, whose convergents are $1,\ 5/4,\ 21/17,\ 89/72,\ 377/305, \dots$. The golden fifth expands as $[1; 1, 1, 8, 2, 8, 2, \dots]$, with convergents $1, 2, 3/2, 26/17, 55/36, 466/305, \dots$; the golden seventh as $[1; 1, 7, 1, 34, 1, 7, 1, 34, \dots]$, with convergents $1, 2, 15/8, 17/9, 593/314, 610/323, \dots$ (all computed to 60 digits). A large partial quotient (the 4s, 8s and 34s) means the preceding convergent is an unusually good approximation, followed by a long gap before the next. That is the arithmetic behind "almost Western, and then not".

36-ET keeps the relation. In 36-ET the golden third is 11 sixth-steps (366.67 c), between the major third (12) and the minor third (9), and the golden fifth is 22 (733.33 c), between the perfect fifth (21) and the augmented fifth (24). The grid itself records that golden chords live between familiar ones.

Exercises

Exercise 41.1 (listening). In the organum example, which sounds more fused, the fourths or the fifths? Then in the thirds example, can you count the beats of the Pythagorean major third? About how many in one second?

Answer

Most listeners find both fused, the fifths slightly more so (the fifth has the simpler ratio). The Pythagorean major third on 220 Hz beats 13.75 times per second, too fast to count one by one: it is heard as a buzz or roughness rather than as separate pulses.

Exercise 41.2 (calculation). Show that the Pythagorean minor third 32/27 is a syntonic comma narrower than 6/5, and compute its beat rate on 220 Hz.

Answer

$(6/5)/(32/27) = 162/160 = 81/80$. Beat: $|5\cdot(32/27) - 6|\cdot 220 = (6 - 160/27)\cdot 220 = (2/27)\cdot 220 = 16.30$ Hz.

Exercise 41.3 (calculation). How many partials coincide, among the first 12 of each tone, for a perfect fifth, a just major third and a harmonic seventh 7:4?

Answer

$\lfloor 12/\max(p,q)\rfloor$: fifth 3:2 → 4; major third 5:4 → 2; 7:4 → 1.

Exercise 41.4 (proof). Show that $2/\varphi = \sqrt5 - 1$ and that its continued fraction is $[1; 4, 4, 4, \dots]$.

Answer

$1/\varphi = \varphi - 1 = (\sqrt5 - 1)/2$, so $2/\varphi = \sqrt5 - 1$. Write $\sqrt5 = 2 + x$ with $0 < x < 1$. Then $(2+x)^2 = 5$ gives $x(4 + x) = 1$, so $x = 1/(4 + x)$, which unfolds to $x = [0; 4, 4, 4, \dots]$. Hence $\sqrt5 - 1 = 1 + x = [1; 4, 4, 4, \dots]$.

Exercise 41.5 (reflection). Name one interval or chord that you hear as normal but that a listener of an earlier century would probably have heard as unstable. What made it normal for you?

Answer

Many answers are reasonable: the 12-ET major third (which beats), the dominant seventh as a final chord in blues, a major seventh or added ninth as a closing chord in popular music. The usual reason is exposure: the sound was everywhere in the music you grew up with.
