Equal temperament closed the chain of fifths into a circle. This chapter studies that circle as a mathematical object. Once octaves are ignored, the twelve notes of 12-ET form a small, perfectly regular number system, the integers modulo 12. Transposition is addition, the circle of fifths is what you get by adding 7 over and over, and keys, key signatures and the distance between keys all fall out of that one operation. The same ideas, in a system of 36 pitch classes, organize the golden keys of Part VI.

Pitch classes and Z12

A C is a C in any octave. Low or high, it plays the same role in a melody or a chord (Chapter 1). Listen to a short tune, then to the same tune with every note thrown into a different octave. The contour becomes wild, but the notes, as names, are the same, and so is the harmony they outline.

Music theory captures this by grouping all the Cs together into one pitch class, all the C♯s into another, and so on. In 12-ET there are exactly twelve pitch classes.

Arithmetic with these numbers is clock arithmetic: count around and wrap at 12. Going up 7 semitones from F (5) lands on $5 + 7 = 12$, which is 0, C. We write $12 \equiv 0 \pmod{12}$. In this arithmetic:

  • an interval between two pitch classes is a difference: from E (4) up to C (0) is $0 - 4 \equiv 8$ semitones, a minor sixth; down from E to C is 4;
  • transposition by $n$ semitones adds $n$ to every pitch class: C major {0, 4, 7} transposed by 2 becomes {2, 6, 9}, D major;
  • enharmonic names are the same class: C♯ and D♭ are both 1. In 12-ET they are the same pitch; in Pythagorean tuning they differ by a comma (Chapter 5), and the numbers 0 to 11 do not describe Pythagorean pitches exactly.

From a MIDI note number (C4 = 60) the pitch class is simply the remainder after dividing by 12: middle C is $60 \bmod 12 = 0$, A4 = 69 is $69 \bmod 12 = 9$.

In 36-ET the pitch classes are the numbers 0 to 35, counted in sixth-steps: C = 0, ^C = 1, vC♯ = 2, C♯ = 3, and so on. The twelve 12-ET pitch classes are the multiples of 3, one of three layers (Chapter 24). Everything in this chapter carries over to $\mathbb{Z}_{36}$, with richer results (Chapter 33).

Definition. $\mathbb{Z}_{12} = \{0, 1, \dots, 11\}$ with addition modulo 12. The pitch class of a 12-ET pitch with MIDI number $p$ is $p \bmod 12$.

$\mathbb{Z}_{12}$ is a group (Appendix A). Addition mod 12 is closed and associative; 0 is the identity; the inverse of $x$ is $12 - x$ (for $x \ne 0$), the interval that brings $x$ back to 0. It is cyclic: every element is a multiple of 1. It is also commutative, which says that two transpositions in either order give the same result.

Transposition and interval class. Transposition by $n$ is the map $T_n(x) = x + n$. Since $T_m T_n = T_{m+n}$, the transpositions form a copy of $\mathbb{Z}_{12}$. The interval from $x$ up to $y$ is $y - x$; the interval class of $\{x, y\}$ is the smaller of $y - x$ and $x - y$ (mod 12), a number from 0 to 6, which identifies an interval with its inversion (Appendix A).

The circle as an orbit

Start on C and keep adding the same interval until C comes back. Add a semitone and you pass through all twelve notes. Add a fifth and you also pass through all twelve, in the order of the circle of fifths. But add a whole tone and you return after six notes; a minor third, after four; a major third, after three. Only some intervals can carry you everywhere.

The circle of fifths is also a progression. Chords whose roots fall by fifths are one of the strongest motions in tonal harmony (Chapter 11). Follow it all the way round and you visit every key and arrive home.

Adding 7 again and again from 0 gives

$$0,\ 7,\ 14 \equiv 2,\ 9,\ 16 \equiv 4,\ 11,\ 6,\ 1,\ 8,\ 3,\ 10,\ 5,\ 12 \equiv 0,$$

that is C G D A E B F♯ C♯ G♯ E♭ B♭ F and back to C. This sequence is the orbit of 0 under adding 7. It contains every pitch class once, so 7 is a generator of $\mathbb{Z}_{12}$. Drawn on the clock face, the orbit is the star-shaped circle of fifths.

In 12-ET the orbit closes after twelve steps because twelve 12-ET fifths are exactly seven octaves. The circle of fifths is the Pythagorean chain of Chapter 5 with the comma erased, so that G♯ and A♭, twelve links apart, are the same note.

There is a neat link between the chromatic circle and the circle of fifths: multiplying every pitch class by 7 turns one into the other. $7 \times 1 = 7$, $7 \times 2 = 14 \equiv 2$, $7 \times 3 = 21 \equiv 9$, and so on. Since $7 \times 7 = 49 \equiv 1$, doing it twice gives back the original. This multiplication, M7, reappears in set theory (Chapter 20).

In 36-ET the 12-ET fifth is 21 sixth-steps, and 21 shares the factor 3 with 36. So adding 21 visits only 12 of the 36 pitch classes: the circle of fifths of 36-ET is three separate twelve-note circles, one on each layer. The golden sixth, 25 sixth-steps, shares no factor with 36, so the golden chain visits all 36 pitch classes before returning, a 36-tone row (Chapter 23, Chapter 33). The units of $\mathbb{Z}_{36}$ are 1, 5, 7, 11, 13, 17, 19, 23, 25, 29, 31 and 35; among them are the golden third (11), the bronze third (13) and the golden sixth (25).

Theorem (orbits in $\mathbb{Z}_n$). The orbit of 0 under repeated addition of $k$ in $\mathbb{Z}_n$ is the set of multiples of $d = \gcd(k, n)$, and it has $n/d$ elements. In particular $k$ generates $\mathbb{Z}_n$ if and only if $\gcd(k, n) = 1$.

Proof. Every multiple $jk$ is a multiple of $d$ modulo $n$, since $d$ divides both $k$ and $n$. Conversely, by Bézout's identity there are integers $u, v$ with $uk + vn = d$, so $uk \equiv d \pmod n$, and every multiple of $d$ is a multiple of $k$. The multiples of $d$ in $\{0, \dots, n-1\}$ are $0, d, 2d, \dots, n - d$: there are $n/d$ of them. ∎

For $n = 12$ the generators are the four units 1, 5, 7, 11, the numbers that share no factor with 12. The orbits of the other nonzero elements are the subgroups of orders 2, 3, 4 and 6 listed in the table; the orbit of 0 itself is the trivial subgroup $\{0\}$. For $n = 36$, the fifth $k = 21$ has $\gcd = 3$ and an orbit of 12, while $k = 25$ has $\gcd = 1$.

M7. The map $M_7(x) = 7x \bmod 12$ sends the chromatic order $0, 1, 2, \dots$ to the circle-of-fifths order $0, 7, 2, \dots$. Since $7^2 = 49 \equiv 1 \pmod{12}$, $M_7$ is its own inverse. The same holds for $M_5$, because $5^2 = 25 \equiv 1$; $M_5$ gives the circle of fourths.

Keys and key signatures

A key is a home note, the tonic, together with a scale built on it. C major uses only the white keys. Move the home note up a fifth, to G, and the major scale on G needs one black key, F♯. Up another fifth, D major needs two, F♯ and C♯. Each step around the circle adds one sharp. Listen to the same tune in C, G, D and A major.

Musicians do not write those sharps in front of every note. Instead, a key signature at the start of each line of music lists the sharps or flats once.

The major scale on tonic $t$ is the set $\{t, t+2, t+4, t+5, t+7, t+9, t+11\}$. Seen on the circle of fifths it is seven consecutive notes: the tonic, the note one fifth below it and the five notes above it. C major is F C G D A E B. G major moves the whole window one step clockwise: it drops F and gains F♯.

So the sharps enter in circle-of-fifths order, F C G D A E B, and the flats in the reverse order, B E A D G C F:

Two rules of thumb follow. In a sharp key the last sharp is the leading tone, a semitone below the tonic (F♯ in G major). In a flat key the last flat is the fourth degree, so the next-to-last flat names the tonic (B♭ in the signature of B♭ major, B♭ E♭).

There are 15 signatures but only 12 tonic pitch classes, so three pairs of keys are enharmonic: B major (5 sharps) and C♭ major (7 flats), F♯ major and G♭ major (6 each), C♯ major (7 sharps) and D♭ major (5 flats). In 12-ET each pair sounds identical; in Pythagorean tuning they would differ by a comma. Counting minor keys as well, there are 24 keys, the 24 of Bach's Well-Tempered Clavier.

36-ET has a tonic on each of its 36 pitch classes, so it has 72 major and minor keys, and 72 golden scales besides (36 golden13 and 36 silver13). Their names use the ups-and-downs accidentals ^ and v as prefixes, ^C major or vE♭ minor (Chapter 24, notation).

Chain coordinates. Place the natural notes at chain positions F = −1, C = 0, G = 1, D = 2, A = 3, E = 4, B = 5 (as in Chapter 5), and let a key signature with $s$ sharps be $s$, with $s < 0$ for $|s|$ flats.

Proposition. The major key with signature $s$ ($-7 \le s \le 7$) consists of the chain positions $s - 1, s, \dots, s + 5$, and its tonic is at position $s$, pitch class $7s \bmod 12$.

  • For $s > 0$ the altered notes are the positions 6 to $s + 5$, which are the first $s$ letters of F C G D A E B, each sharped. The newest sharp, position $s + 5$, is five fifths above the tonic: the leading tone.
  • For $s < 0$ the altered notes are the positions $s - 1$ to $-2$, the first $|s|$ letters of B E A D G C F, each flatted. The newest flat, position $s - 1$, is one fifth below the tonic: the fourth degree.

Proof. The C major scale is positions −1 to 5. Transposing by a fifth adds 1 to every chain position, and $s$ transpositions give positions $s - 1$ to $s + 5$. A position $k$ outside −1…5 carries $\lfloor (k+1)/7 \rfloor$ sharps (Chapter 5), which is $+1$ for $6 \le k \le 12$ and $-1$ for $-8 \le k \le -2$. ∎

Enharmonic keys. Signatures $s$ and $s - 12$ have tonic pitch classes $7s$ and $7s - 84 \equiv 7s$: the same. Within $-7 \le s \le 7$ this happens for exactly three pairs, $(5, -7)$, $(6, -6)$ and $(7, -5)$.

Near and far keys

Some keys feel close to each other and some feel far apart. Listen to a phrase in C major, then the same phrase in G major: the change is gentle, a brightening. Return to C, then jump to F♯ major: the shift is abrupt, almost a change of world. G is one step from C on the circle of fifths; F♯ is six steps away, the far side of the circle.

The simplest measure of key distance is the number of steps around the circle of fifths between the two tonics, counted the shorter way round, so it is never more than 6. It equals the difference of the two key signatures, or 12 minus that difference when it is larger than 6 (E major, 4 sharps, and A♭ major, 4 flats, are 4 steps apart, not 8). Neighboring keys differ in a single note, moved by a semitone (C major to G major: F becomes F♯). That is why a modulation to the dominant or subdominant key is so smooth: almost everything stays. The closely related keys of C major are traditionally the keys whose signatures differ from C's by at most one accidental: G major, F major and the three relative minors A, E and D minor. Modulations to them are the everyday modulations of tonal music (Chapter 11).

The circle is a measure of note content, not of everything. C major and C minor share their tonic and their dominant chord but sit three steps apart on the circle; the next section looks at that relationship.

36-ET adds a second direction of travel. Moving a whole key up one sixth-step, from C major to ^C major, keeps no pitch class at all, yet every voice moves by only 33 c. Near and far are then measured in two directions, around the circle of fifths and across the layers (Chapter 32).

Theorem (common tones). Let $D_t$ be the major scale on tonic $t$. For $d = 0, 1, \dots, 6$, $$|D_0 \cap D_{7d}| = \begin{cases} 7 - d, & 0 \le d \le 5,\\ 2, & d = 6.\end{cases}$$ By symmetry, $D_{-7d}$ gives the same counts.

Proof. On the circle of fifths (positions mod 12), $D_0$ is the window $\{-1, \dots, 5\}$ and $D_{7d}$ the window $\{d - 1, \dots, d + 5\}$. Directly, the windows share the $7 - d$ positions $d - 1, \dots, 5$. Going round the circle, the second window's top end $d + 5$ is also $d - 7$, so it can overlap the bottom of the first window, positions $\{d - 13, \dots, d - 7\} \cap \{-1, \dots, 5\}$, which is empty for $d \le 5$ and is $\{-1\}$ for $d = 6$. So the count is $7 - d$ for $d \le 5$ and $1 + 1 = 2$ for $d = 6$. ∎ (Checked directly for every $d$.)

For $d = 6$ the two common notes of C major and F♯ major are B and F (= E♯).

Relative and parallel keys

Every major key has two minor partners. The relative minor uses exactly the same notes but treats a different note as home: A minor is C major's white keys heard from A. The parallel minor keeps the home note and changes the notes: C minor has the same tonic as C major, with E♭, A♭ and B♭. Listen to a cadence and tune in C major, then in A minor, then in C minor.

Relative keys share a key signature, which is why the key-signature table lists each signature with a major and a minor key. The relative minor's tonic is the sixth degree of the major scale, three semitones below the major tonic. In the language of Chapter 6, the relative minor is the Aeolian mode of the same collection.

Parallel keys share a tonic. C minor has the notes of E♭ major, three fifths counterclockwise, and so it shares only four notes with C major (C, D, F, G). But the two keys share a home, and in practice minor keys borrow the major key's leading tone: the dominant chord in C minor is usually G major (G B D), with B natural, the harmonic minor form. Composers move freely between parallel major and minor; borrowing chords from the parallel key is modal mixture (Chapter 11), and ending a minor piece on a major tonic chord is the Picardy third.

A famous tour of all 24 keys uses the relative pairs: Chopin's 24 Preludes, Op. 28, go around the circle of fifths pairing each major key with its relative minor, C major, A minor, G major, E minor, and so on.

The golden triad lives exactly between parallel major and minor. Its third, 11 sixth-steps, is two thirds of the way from the minor third (9) to the major third (12), and its fifth, 22, sits one third of the way from the perfect fifth (21) to the augmented (24): Cg = (C + Cm + Caug)/3 (Chapter 25). In golden harmony the move between parallel major and minor can pass through gold: golden modal mixture C – Cg – Cm, and the golden Picardy Cm – Cm(^3) – Cg – C (Chapter 29).

Proposition (relative). As sets, the major scale on $t$ equals the natural minor scale on $t + 9$.

Proof. The natural minor on $u$ is $\{u, u+2, u+3, u+5, u+7, u+8, u+10\}$. With $u = t + 9$ this is $\{t+9, t+11, t, t+2, t+4, t+5, t+7\}$ (mod 12), the major scale on $t$. ∎

Proposition (parallel). The natural minor scale on $t$ equals the major scale on $t + 3$, so its signature is $s - 3$, where $s$ is the signature of $t$ major.

Proof. By the previous proposition the minor scale on $t$ is the major scale on $t - 9 \equiv t + 3$. On the circle, $+3 \equiv 7 \times (-3) \pmod{12}$, which is three steps counterclockwise: three more flats (or three fewer sharps). ∎

C major and C minor therefore share $7 - 3 = 4$ notes, by the common-tone theorem of the last section.

Exercise 8.1. Compute the orbit of 0 under adding 5 in $\mathbb{Z}_{12}$, and name the notes.

Answer

0, 5, 10, 3, 8, 1, 6, 11, 4, 9, 2, 7: C F B♭ E♭ A♭ D♭ G♭ B E A D G, the circle of fourths (the circle of fifths backwards). It has 12 elements because $\gcd(5, 12) = 1$.

Exercise 8.2. Which major key has four flats, and what is its relative minor? Use chain positions.

Answer

$s = -4$: the tonic is at position −4, pitch class $7 \times (-4) = -28 \equiv 8$, A♭. The relative minor is $8 + 9 = 17 \equiv 5$, F minor. The flats are B, E, A, D.

Exercise 8.3. How many notes do E major and B♭ major share? Check by listing them.

Answer

E major has 4 sharps and B♭ major 2 flats: the signatures are $4 - (-2) = 6$ steps apart, so they share 2 notes. E major is E F♯ G♯ A B C♯ D♯; B♭ major is B♭ C D E♭ F G A. The common notes are A and D♯ = E♭.

Exercise 8.4. In $\mathbb{Z}_{36}$, how many distinct pitch classes does the orbit of the bronze third (13 sixth-steps) contain? And the orbit of the 12-ET major third (12 sixth-steps)?

Answer

$\gcd(13, 36) = 1$, so 36: the bronze third walks through every pitch class. $\gcd(12, 36) = 12$, so $36/12 = 3$: three major thirds close the cycle, an augmented triad, just as in $\mathbb{Z}_{12}$.

Exercise 8.5. (Listening.) In the near-and-far example, the G major phrase contains one note that the C major phrase lacks. Which, and where do you hear it?

Answer

F♯, in the D major chord (D F♯ A) just before the final G chord, where it acts as the new leading tone.
