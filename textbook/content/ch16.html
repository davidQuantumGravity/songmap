Pulse and meter

Most music has a pulse: a steady stream of beats you could clap or tap your foot to. Above the pulse the ear groups beats into larger units, strong and weak, and that grouping is the meter. Listen to six quick notes per bar, grouped two different ways. First they fall into three pairs (ONE-and TWO-and THREE-and), then into two triplets (ONE-and-a TWO-and-a). Finally the two groupings alternate, bar by bar.

The notes are almost the same in all three passages; only the accents and the bass change. Yet the first sounds like a waltz, the second like a jig, and the third has a lilting, unstable swing between them.

Meter creates expectations, and music plays with them. A syncopation is an accent where the meter says there should be none, or a silence where it says there should be a strong beat. Listen to a short tune played straight, then with its notes pushed half a beat early and held across the beat.

A time signature names a meter. In 4/4 there are four quarter-note beats per bar; in 3/4, three; in 6/8, six eighth notes grouped into two beats of three. Meters are classified by how beats divide and group:

Meter is a hierarchy. In 4/4, the bar divides into two halves, each half into two quarter-note beats, each beat into two eighths. Beats higher in the hierarchy are felt as stronger. Fred Lerdahl and Ray Jackendoff's A Generative Theory of Tonal Music (1983) made this precise for Western tonal music with rules for well-formed metrical structures; one of them says that the beats at each level are two or three beats of the next lower level apart. The asymmetric meters common in Balkan and many other traditions mix groups of two and three at the same level, such as 7/8 as 2+2+3.

Tempo is the rate of the pulse, in beats per minute. People can entrain to beats over a wide range of rates, roughly from a few tenths of a second to about two seconds between beats, and many listening and tapping studies put a comfortable "preferred" tempo somewhere around 100 to 120 beats per minute. These are measurements with wide individual variation, not fixed constants.

Polyrhythm sets two pulses against each other: three evenly spaced notes against two, or four against three, in the same span. Polyrhythm and polymeter are fundamental to many musical traditions of West and Central Africa and of the African diaspora, and appear in Western music as cross-rhythm and hemiola.

A hemiola regroups six pulses from 3+3 to 2+2+2 (or the reverse), exactly the change in the meters example. Baroque composers often used it at cadences: two bars of 3/4 are heard for a moment as one bar of 3/2.

Some rhythms distribute a few onsets as evenly as possible over a cycle of pulses. The Cuban tresillo puts 3 onsets in 8 pulses (x . . x . . x .), the cinquillo 5 in 8 (x . x x . x x .). A bell pattern widely used in West African and Afro-Caribbean music puts 7 onsets in 12 pulses with gaps 2 2 1 2 2 2 1, the same pattern as the steps of the major scale, a parallel between rhythm and scale pointed out by Jeff Pressing (1983). Godfried Toussaint called rhythms built this way Euclidean rhythms (2005), after the algorithm that generates them.

Meters as factorizations (theorem). Under the rule that every metrical level groups the level below in twos or threes, a regular metrical hierarchy on $N$ pulses per bar is an ordered factorization of $N$ into 2s and 3s (read from the smallest level up). If $N = 2^a 3^b$, there are $\binom{a+b}{a}$ such hierarchies, since we only choose the order of the factors.

Polyrhythm onsets (theorem). In an $a$-against-$b$ polyrhythm, one layer has onsets at $j/a$ and the other at $k/b$ of the cycle. They coincide exactly at the multiples of $1/\gcd(a, b)$, so the composite rhythm has $a + b - \gcd(a, b)$ distinct onsets per cycle: 4 for 3:2 and 6 for 4:3. All onsets lie on the grid of $\operatorname{lcm}(a, b)$ pulses (6 for 3:2, 12 for 4:3).

Maximally even rhythms. The rhythm $E(k, n)$ has onsets at $\lfloor i n / k \rfloor$, $i = 0, \dots, k - 1$. Every gap between consecutive onsets is $\lfloor n/k \rfloor$ or $\lceil n/k \rceil$, so the rhythm has at most two gap sizes, exactly like a well-formed scale (Chapter 6). $E(3, 8)$ has onsets $(0, 2, 5)$ and gaps $(2, 3, 3)$, a rotation of the tresillo's $(3, 3, 2)$; $E(5, 8)$ has gaps $(1, 2, 1, 2, 2)$, a rotation of the cinquillo's $(2, 1, 2, 1, 2)$; $E(7, 12)$ has gaps $(1, 2, 2, 1, 2, 2, 2)$, a rotation of the bell pattern's $(2, 2, 1, 2, 2, 2, 1)$ and of the major scale's steps.

Counting syncopations. A simple measure: count the beats on which no note begins while a note that began on an off-beat is still sounding. The straight tune scores 0; the syncopated version scores 2 per bar (beats 2 and 3 in each bar). More refined measures, such as the one proposed by H. Christopher Longuet-Higgins and Christopher Lee (1984), weight each syncopation by how much stronger the skipped beat is than the note's own position.

Period and sentence

Phrases group into larger units the way beats group into bars. Two patterns are so common in Classical music that they have names. The period, a question and an answer, was the "Ode to Joy" example of Chapter 13. The sentence works differently. Listen for its three stages: a short idea, the same idea again on a different chord, and then a faster, more urgent continuation that breaks the idea into pieces and drives to a cadence.

The second half feels like a gathering of speed even though the tempo does not change. The units get shorter (two bars, then one bar) and the chords change twice as often. That compression is the sentence's engine.

Both forms are typically eight bars long and are described by William Caplin in Classical Form (1998), building on Arnold Schoenberg's teaching (Fundamentals of Musical Composition, published 1967).

In the example, the basic idea is E G C G | E C over C. Its repetition is the same shape, D G B G | D B, over G. The continuation takes only the first bar's shape, F A C A over F and D F A F over Dm (a sequence down a third), and then cadences: B C D B G B | C over G and C.

Above the bar, many phrases also have a hypermeter: bars group into strong and weak pairs and four-bar units, as if each bar were one beat of a larger meter. Composers exploit this by lengthening a phrase (an expansion), or by letting the last bar of one phrase also be the first bar of the next (an elision).

Proportions and rates. Write a phrase as a list of units with their lengths in bars. The period is $(4 \mid 4)$, symmetric. The sentence is $(2, 2 \mid 1, 1, 2)$ in the example: the units shrink by a factor of 2 in the continuation, and the harmonic rhythm doubles, from one chord per 2 bars (C, G) to one chord per bar (F, Dm, G, C). The presentation and continuation are each 4 bars, a 1 : 1 split, while the basic idea is a quarter of the whole.

Hypermeter. The factorization rule of the previous section applies one level up: 8 bars grouped in twos, twos in twos and fours in twos give the hierarchy $2 \cdot 2 \cdot 2$, so bar 1 is the strongest, bar 5 next, then bars 3 and 7. A five-bar phrase cannot be built this way without a group of 3 or a deviation; that is why composers and listeners hear five-bar phrases as irregular.

Song forms and larger forms

Larger forms are built from sections that return, contrast and develop. The most familiar plan in popular song of the twentieth century is AABA: a main section, its repeat, a contrasting "bridge", and the main section once more. The bridge is what makes the return satisfying: after hearing something different, the familiar A sounds like coming home.

Sonata form deserves a closer look, because it is a form made of key relationships. The exposition presents a first theme in the tonic, a transition, and a second theme group in another key (the dominant in a major-key movement, usually the relative major in a minor-key one), often repeated. The development moves through further keys and works over the themes, fragmenting and recombining them. The recapitulation brings the themes back with the second group now in the tonic, resolving the tension of the exposition's key contrast. Charles Rosen's Sonata Forms (1980) and James Hepokoski and Warren Darcy's Elements of Sonata Theory (2006) are standard modern accounts.

Form is created by the same forces as the smaller units: repetition gives identity, contrast gives interest, and cadences mark the boundaries. A bridge in AABA does on a large scale what a half cadence does in a period: it opens a question that the return answers.

Counting letter schemes (theorem). Call two forms the same if they differ only in letter names (ABBA and BAAB describe the same plan). A scheme with $n$ sections is then a restricted growth string: the first section is A, and each new letter is the next unused one. The number of schemes with $n$ sections is the Bell number $B_n$: 1, 2, 5, 15, 52 for $n = 1, \dots, 5$. For $n = 4$ the 15 schemes are AAAA, AAAB, AABA, AABB, AABC, ABAA, ABAB, ABAC, ABBA, ABBB, ABBC, ABCA, ABCB, ABCC and ABCD. AABA (song form) and ABAC are among the most used; ABCD ("through-composed") uses no repetition at all.

Proof sketch. A scheme is the same thing as a partition of the set of sections into blocks of equal letters, and partitions of an $n$-set are counted by $B_n$. ∎

Symmetry. Ternary ABA and the arch form ABCBA are palindromes: they read the same backwards, the formal analogue of the retrograde symmetry of Chapter 13.

Proportion and the golden section

Where should the high point of a piece fall? Many pieces build to a climax well past the middle and then release it in a shorter final stretch. Here is a constructed example: thirteen bars, eight rising and growing louder, five falling away. The peak arrives at the start of bar 9, about 62 percent of the way through.

The numbers 8, 5 and 13 are consecutive Fibonacci numbers, and 8/13 is close to the golden section. Whether that proportion is special to the ear is a genuinely open question, and the claims made about it in music are worth examining carefully.

The golden section divides a length so that the whole is to the larger part as the larger part is to the smaller. The larger part is $1/\varphi \approx 0.618$ of the whole and the smaller $1/\varphi^2 \approx 0.382$ (see Chapter 23). Ratios of consecutive Fibonacci numbers approach it: 3/5 = 0.600, 5/8 = 0.625, 8/13 = 0.615, 13/21 = 0.619.

Claims about composers. The most influential are these:

  • Bartók. The Hungarian theorist Ernő Lendvai argued, in Hungarian publications from the 1950s and in Béla Bartók: An Analysis of His Music (1971), that Bartók organized form by the golden section and Fibonacci numbers, as in the first movement of Music for Strings, Percussion and Celesta (1936), whose climax he placed at the golden section. Roy Howat ("Bartók, Lendvai and the principles of proportional analysis", Music Analysis, 1983) re-examined such analyses and found some of Lendvai's measurements inaccurate, while defending a more careful kind of proportional analysis. Bartók himself is not known to have mentioned the golden section, and whether such proportions were intended, or are even perceptible, remains contested.
  • Debussy. Howat's Debussy in Proportion (1983) argues that several of Debussy's works, among them Reflets dans l'eau and La mer, are shaped by golden-section and symmetrical proportions. The readings are detailed and influential, and also contested: results depend on choices of unit (bars, beats or seconds) and of which events count as structural.
  • Popular claims that Mozart, Beethoven, Chopin or others "used the golden ratio" usually rest on choosing, after the fact, a pair of numbers that happens to fit. Without documentary evidence of intention and without a comparison against chance, they are better treated as myths.

The derive layer shows why "fits the golden section" is weak evidence: with a few candidate events and a modest tolerance, a fit is likely by chance alone. Even in vision, where Gustav Fechner reported a preference for golden rectangles in 1876, later experiments have given mixed results.

Status in this book. The golden tonal system of Parts VI–VII is about golden pitch ratios (φ as an interval). Golden proportion in time is a separate idea. Some of this book's golden pieces use Fibonacci section lengths (8, 13, 21 bars) as a design choice, in keeping with the "golden at every scale" principle of Chapter 40; that is a compositional decision and a hypothesis to explore by listening, not a claim that listeners perceive golden proportions.

There is one place where golden proportion enters rhythm with full mathematical precision: a rhythm of long and short notes whose lengths are in the ratio φ, ordered by the Fibonacci word L S L L S L S L L S L L S … (each L becomes LS and each S becomes L at every step). It never repeats, yet it is as evenly balanced as a rhythm of two note lengths can be. Listen to it, and then to the plain alternation L S L S with the same two lengths.

How close are the Fibonacci ratios? Relative to $1/\varphi = 0.6180$: 3/5 is 2.92% low, 5/8 is 1.13% high, 8/13 is 0.43% low. In general $F_{n-1}/F_n - 1/\varphi = (-1)^n/(\varphi^{n} F_n)$ exactly, so the error shrinks roughly like $1/F_n^2$. In the example, the golden-section point of 13 bars is $13/\varphi = 8.03$ bars.

Chance fits (a calculation). Suppose a piece has $k$ "notable events" (climaxes, key changes, entries), placed independently and uniformly at random along its length, and suppose we accept a fit if an event falls within $\pm\delta$ of either golden-section point, at $0.382$ or $0.618$ of the length. The two windows have total width $4\delta$, so

$$P(\text{at least one fit}) = 1 - (1 - 4\delta)^k.$$

With a tolerance of $\delta = 2\%$ of the length, this is 8% for one event, 34.1% for five events and 56.6% for ten. Random placement is not a model of real composition, but the calculation shows that a fit has to be very precise, or predicted in advance, before it means much.

The Fibonacci word (theorems). Let $w_1 = \mathrm{L}$ and $w_{n+1} = \mu(w_n)$ with $\mu(\mathrm{L}) = \mathrm{LS}$, $\mu(\mathrm{S}) = \mathrm{L}$. Then $w_n$ has length $F_{n+1}$, with $F_n$ letters L and $F_{n-1}$ letters S (Fibonacci numbers $F_1 = F_2 = 1$). The first 34 letters contain 21 L and 13 S. As $n \to \infty$ the ratio of L to S tends to $\varphi$. The infinite word is Sturmian: any two blocks of the same length contain numbers of L that differ by at most 1, the rhythmic counterpart of the two-step-size property of well-formed scales and of the evenness of Euclidean rhythms. Its onsets are never periodic, because $\varphi$ is irrational. Proof of the counts: $\mu$ maps each L to one L and one S, and each S to one L, so the counts $(\ell, s)$ evolve as $(\ell, s) \mapsto (\ell + s, \ell)$, the Fibonacci recursion. ∎

Exercise 16.1 (listening). In the meters example, tap along with the strongest beats. How many taps per bar do you make in each of the three passages?

Answer

Three in the first passage (three-four), two in the second (six-eight), and in the third passage alternately two and three, which is why it feels as if the bar length changes even though it does not.

Exercise 16.2. How many distinct onsets per cycle does a 5-against-3 polyrhythm have? A 6-against-4?

Answer

$5 + 3 - \gcd(5, 3) = 7$. $6 + 4 - \gcd(6, 4) = 8$: the layers also coincide at the half cycle, because 6:4 is really 3:2 played twice per cycle.

Exercise 16.3. List the ordered factorizations of 18 pulses into 2s and 3s and name a time signature for each.

Answer

$18 = 2 \cdot 3^2$ gives $\binom{3}{1} = 3$ orders: 2·3·3, 3·2·3 and 3·3·2. With eighth-note pulses, 2·3·3 is 9/4 (quarters grouped in threes, three groups); 3·2·3 is three bars' worth of 6/8; 3·3·2 is two bars' worth of 9/8. The count is the point; notation for such long hierarchies varies.

Exercise 16.4. Compute $E(5, 12)$ and its gaps. Which familiar scale has the same step pattern?

Answer

Onsets $\lfloor 12i/5 \rfloor$ = 0, 2, 4, 7, 9, with gaps 2, 2, 3, 2, 3: the pentatonic scale C D E G A.

Exercise 16.5. A piece is 89 bars long and its climax falls in bar 55. How far is that from the golden section, in bars? Using the chance formula with $\delta = 1/89$ (one bar), how likely is at least one of five random events to fit as well?

Answer

$89/\varphi = 55.005$, so the fit is essentially exact. With $\delta = 1/89$, $1 - (1 - 4/89)^5 \approx 0.205$: about a one-in-five chance for five events. A precise fit like this is suggestive only if the event and the unit of measurement were fixed before looking.
