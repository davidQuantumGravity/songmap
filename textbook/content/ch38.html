Staff notation with ups and downs

Five chords, each written on a staff in the figure below: C major; the golden triad Cg, in which E is lowered and G raised by a sixth-step; the golden seventh Cg7, which adds B; and two steps of the golden circle, vEg and ^Gg. As you listen, notice how little the notation has to change to show the new color: the noteheads sit where a C major chord's would, and two small marks say "a little lower" and "a little higher".

The problem. Staff notation was designed for seven letter names and five chromatic alterations. A 36-ET piece needs three times as many pitches per octave. Inventing a new staff would make every existing skill useless; the better way is to keep the staff and add accidentals.

Ups and downs. This book follows Kite Giedraitis' "ups and downs" notation for equal divisions of the octave: an up (^) raises a note by one step of the finer grid, a down (v) lowers it by one, and they combine freely with sharps and flats (Chapter 24). In 36-ET one step is a sixth-tone, 33.33 c. In text the marks are written before the note name (^G, vE♭); on the staff they stand before the notehead like any accidental, and in engraved form they are often drawn as small up and down arrows.

Thirty-six names. Each 12-ET note has an up and a down neighbour. The standard spelling uses the plain note, the note with an up, or the next note with a down, so that no name needs more than one arrow:

Spelling chords. A golden chord is best spelled as the Western chord it bends, so that its structure is visible: Cg as C vE ^G (the letters of C E G), not as C ^^E♭ ^G, which names the same pitches with the letters of a minor triad. This matches the averaging principle (Chapter 25): the golden triad is heard as a bent major-minor-augmented mixture, and the staff shows it so.

Key signatures. A golden scale does not fit the logic of key signatures, which list the sharps or flats of a diatonic scale. golden7 on C contains C♯, vE, vF, ^G and ^A♭: five altered degrees, four of them with arrows. The simplest convention is to write golden music with a plain (C major) signature and every alteration inline, and to keep key signatures for the twelve-tone layers: a piece in ^D major could carry the signature of D major with an up mark, since every note of the scale is raised by the same sixth-step.

For performers. The noteheads are always those of 12-ET notes; ups and downs never move a notehead to another line or space. A singer or string player reads the 12-ET note and bends it by about a third of a semitone; a keyboard player needs an instrument retuned or a second and third keyboard a sixth-step apart, which is the idea behind the three-layer MIDI plan of §38.4.

A spelling choice. The rule "one arrow at most" and the rule "keep the letters of stacked thirds" can conflict. On ^G the golden triad's fifth is 22 sixth-steps above ^G, at step 8. With stacked-third letters (G, B, D) it is ^^D, D raised by two sixth-steps; with at most one arrow it is vE♭, as in the table. SongMap and this book write vE♭ (^Gg = ^G B vE♭). On a staff for performers, ^^D would keep the chord's shape on lines and spaces; both are correct names of the same pitch.

The naming function. For a pitch class $s \in \mathbb{Z}_{36}$ write $s = 3q + r$ with $q \in \{0,\dots,11\}$ and $r \in \{0,1,2\}$, and let $N_q$ be the 12-ET name of semitone $q$ (C, C♯, D, E♭, E, F, F♯, G, A♭, A, B♭, B). Then

$$\operatorname{name}(s) = \begin{cases} N_q & r = 0,\\ {}^\wedge N_q & r = 1,\\ {}^v N_{q+1} & r = 2.\end{cases}$$

Every 36-ET pitch class gets exactly one name of this form, with at most one arrow.

Letter-faithful spelling. To spell a chord tone with a prescribed letter $L$ (for a third, two letters above the root's letter; for a fifth, four), compute its offset $\delta = s - 3\,\mathrm{nat}(L) \pmod{36}$ from the natural note $L$, taken in $(-18, 18]$. Then $\delta = 3a + u$ with $u \in \{-1, 0, 1\}$, written as $a$ sharps (or $-a$ flats) and one up or down. Example: the third of Cg, $s = 11$, letter E (natural 12): $\delta = -1$, so vE. The fifth of ^Gg, $s = 8$, letter D (natural 6): $\delta = 2$, which this rule writes as ^^D if two arrows are allowed, or (with $\delta = 3 - 1$) as vD♯ if sharps are preferred to double arrows. The single-arrow table above chooses vE♭ instead.

Piano rolls in 36-ET

The golden six-four, slowly: a G major chord whose two upper voices climb toward C major through two passing chords. The leading tone B rises to C in three equal sixth-steps; the D above it rises to E in three equal steps of two sixth-steps; G holds in the middle and in the bass until the bass moves to C at the arrival. Follow one line at a time in the piano roll below while listening.

A piano roll is a grid with time running left to right and pitch bottom to top, one row per available pitch. A 12-ET roll has 12 rows per octave. A 36-ET roll has 36: every semitone is split into three rows. In this book's rolls the rows are shaded by layer (Chapter 24): the 12-ET row of each note, the row a sixth-step above it and the row a sixth-step below the next note. The note blocks are colored by layer too, so a chord that sits entirely on the 12-ET layer looks uniform, while a golden chord, which uses all three layers, shows three colors.

Reading the roll: a semitone is three rows, so the alto's climb from B to C is visible as three steps of one row, and the soprano's whole step D to E as three steps of two rows. The bass G is held under both passing chords: a pedal, the signature of the golden six-four (Chapter 27).

A third kind of motion looks different again. In planing (Chapter 31) every voice of a golden triad moves up one row at the same time. The shape of the chord is unchanged; what changes is its layer, and in the roll the colors of the three notes rotate.

A second strict-path roll shows a move between two major triads a major third apart, C → A♭. The first passing chord is the golden triad Cg itself, a third of the way from C to A♭.

Rows. A note with MIDI number $m$ (C4 = 60), $u$ ups and $d$ downs occupies row $s = 3m + u - d$; its layer is $s \bmod 3$ (0 for the 12-ET layer, 1 for up, 2 for down). In the book's figure renderer a roll spans the rows from two steps below the lowest note to two steps above the highest, and each row is drawn with height $h$ (8 pixels in 36-ET), so a note at row $s$ is drawn at height $y = y_0 + (s_{\max} - s)\,h$.

Distances you can read off. A sixth-step is one row, a semitone three, a golden third 11, a major third 12, a golden fifth 22, a perfect fifth 21. In the C → A♭ roll, the voice E4 → E♭4 moves $-3$ rows in $-1, -1, -1$ and G3 → A♭3 moves $+3$ in $+1, +1, +1$; the passing chords are $(2X + Y)/3$ and $(X + 2Y)/3$ voice by voice.

Circles and chord spaces

Two motions that are easiest to understand as pictures. First, the golden circle: golden triads on a golden-spectrum pad, each root a golden third above the last, so that at every step one voice falls by a semitone and two stay. Then a straight line in chord space: augmented, golden, silver, diminished, one sixth-step of the third and two of the fifth at each step, and back.

Pitch-class circles. A pitch-class circle places the octave's steps around a circle, like a clock. In 12-ET the circle of fifths is the path of repeated steps of 7 semitones (Chapter 8). In 36-ET the golden circle is the path of repeated golden thirds, 11 sixth-steps; since 11 and 36 share no factor, it visits all 36 pitch classes before it closes. The triangle marks the golden triad Cg = {0, 11, 22}: three consecutive points of the path.

Scales on the chain. The same circle shows the golden scales. Drawn on the path of the golden chain (generator 25 sixth-steps), golden7 is a window of seven consecutive points, just as the diatonic scale is a window of seven consecutive fifths on the circle of fifths.

Chord spaces. Dmitri Tymoczko's geometry of chords (Chapter 9) treats chords as points, and voice leadings as paths between them. For triads on one root a two-dimensional slice is enough: plot each triad as the point (third, fifth), in sixth-steps above the root. The averaging principle becomes visible. C, Cm and Caug form a triangle whose centroid is Cg; C, Cm and Cdim form one whose centroid is Cs; and the equal-third chords (n, 2n), from T8 through diminished, silver, golden and augmented to bronze, lie on one straight line.

A single golden progression, such as the golden six-four, is clearest when it is seen in all four: the staff says "leading tone and second degree rising", the roll shows the climb row by row, the circle shows which pitch classes are visited, and chord space shows that each passing chord is an average of its neighbours.

Orbits (theorem). In $\mathbb{Z}_{36}$ the path $k \mapsto 11k$ visits every class exactly once before returning, because $\gcd(11, 36) = 1$; so does $k \mapsto 25k$, the golden chain (generator φ ≈ 25 sixth-steps, Chapter 33). Since $25 \equiv -11 \pmod{36}$, the golden chain is the golden circle walked backwards:

$$\text{circle: } 0, 11, 22, 33, 8, 19, \dots \qquad \text{chain: } 0, 25, 14, 3, 28, 17, \dots$$

Any three consecutive points of either path form a golden triad: $\{11k, 11k + 11, 11k + 22\}$ is the triad on root $11k$.

Centroids. With triads as vectors (third, fifth),

$$\tfrac13\big[(12,21) + (9,21) + (12,24)\big] = (11, 22), \qquad \tfrac13\big[(12,21) + (9,21) + (9,18)\big] = (10, 20).$$

The equal-third line. The points $(n, 2n)$ satisfy fifth = 2 × third, a line through the origin of slope 2. Moving along it by one chord changes the third by one sixth-step and the fifth by two; the uniform path Caug → Cg → Cs → Cdim is three such steps.

Tablature and animation

A short plucked phrase, arpeggiated first gently and then in rolling sixteenths, with System 2 passing chords before F and before the final C. It was written as a test piece for the plans in this section: every section, bar and beat of it has an exact time and an exact video frame, which is what an animation needs to stay in sync.

This section describes what exists and what is planned, and labels each.

What exists: cue sheets. Every songmap render writes a cues.json file listing the song's sections, its scored events and named cues, and the frame of every beat and bar, in beats, seconds and video frames. For the phrase above it reports 30 fps, 90 BPM, sections starting at frames 0 and 320, and beats every 20 frames. A video built from the same file (the project's videos are made with a programmatic video tool) can therefore place a title, a cut or a highlight on an exact beat.

Planned: note-triggered animation. The plan is for every audio example of this book to become a short video showing the letter names and the staff, with the sounding notes highlighted as they play, generated by SongMap's own visual renderer. That needs one more export: the time, frame and pitch of every note, which the engine already computes when it expands a song but does not yet write out.

A note-triggered frame could show three synchronized views, each one of this chapter's figures in motion: the staff with the sounding notes lit, a 36-row piano roll scrolling under a playhead, and the 36-point circle with the sounding pitch classes marked. Because each note carries its layer ($s \bmod 3$), the colors of all three views can agree, so a golden chord is recognizable at a glance as a three-color chord. A per-note record would need, at least, the part, the start and end in seconds and frames, the pitch in sixth-steps and the velocity; this is a proposal for the export, not an existing format.

Layered tablature suits instruments that can bend or that have movable frets; on a fixed-fret guitar a 36-ET part would need three instruments tuned a sixth-step apart, or a refretted neck with three frets per semitone.

Frames. At tempo $T$ and frame rate $F$, beat $b$ (counted from 0) is at $t = 60b/T$ seconds and frame $\operatorname{round}(tF) = \operatorname{round}(60bF/T)$. For $T = 90$ and $F = 30$ that is exactly $20b$; the second section, starting at beat 16, starts at frame 320, as the cue sheet reports.

When notes, not beats, fall between frames. Notes in a song are placed on a beat grid, at fractions of a beat; a sixteenth note at 90 BPM lasts $60/(90\cdot 4) = 0.1667$ s, exactly 5 frames at 30 fps, but a triplet eighth lasts $60/(90\cdot 3) = 0.2222$ s, 6.67 frames. Note-triggered animation therefore rounds each note to the nearest frame, an error of at most half a frame: 16.7 ms at 30 fps, 8.3 ms at 60 fps.

Pitch bend for a sixth-step. A MIDI pitch bend is a 14-bit number with center 8192. With the common bend range of ±2 semitones, 8192 units span 200 c, so a sixth-step (33.33 c) needs $8192/6 = 1365.33$ units. Rounded to 1365 it gives $1365\cdot 200/8192 = 33.325$ c, an error of 0.008 c: far below audibility, so a three-channel MIDI rendering of 36-ET is effectively exact.

Exercises

Exercise 38.1 (spelling). Spell the golden triad on D with the one-arrow names of the table, and say which letter pattern it bends.

Answer

D is 6; the triad is 6, 17, 28: D, vF♯, ^A. It bends D F♯ A, the D major triad.

Exercise 38.2 (reading). In a 36-ET piano roll, a voice moves from row 177 to row 180 in three equal steps. Name the four pitches.

Answer

177 = 3·59 is B3; 178 is ^B3; 179 is vC4; 180 is C4. It is the leading tone rising through the golden layers.

Exercise 38.3 (proof). Show that the path $k \mapsto 10k$ in $\mathbb{Z}_{36}$ does not visit all pitch classes, and find how many it visits.

Answer

$\gcd(10, 36) = 2$, so $10k$ is always even and the path visits only the 18 even classes: stacked silver thirds return after 18 steps, as in Chapter 25.

Exercise 38.4 (calculation). At 72 BPM and 30 fps, at which frame does bar 5 (counting from bar 1) start in 4/4?

Answer

Bar 5 starts at beat 16, $t = 60\cdot 16/72 = 13.333$ s, frame $\operatorname{round}(400) = 400$ (25 frames per beat).
