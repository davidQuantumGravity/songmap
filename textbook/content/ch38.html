Chapter 36 stayed inside the golden scales, where every chord is golden and no Western triad can appear. This chapter mixes the two worlds. It asks three questions about scales and one about harmony. What happens if the notes of C major are bent by a sixth-step, one at a time? What scales does the averaging principle give when it is applied to whole scales instead of chords? How many Western and how many golden triads can one seven-note scale hold at once? And how can a Western key borrow golden chords as colour, the way C major borrows from C minor? The answers are exact, several of them are negative, and together they draw a clear line: hybrid scales exist, but they are colour collections, never new diatonics. All counts are exhaustive enumerations from reference/golden/blending.py (sections 12–14) and docs/GOLDEN_BLENDING.md §8–9, recomputed for this chapter; the roughness values were computed for it in the same model.

Golden inflections of the major scale

Start with C major and bend two of its notes: lower E by a sixth-step and raise G by a sixth-step. The tonic triad C E G becomes the golden triad C vE ^G, while F, A, D and B are untouched. The result, C D vE F ^G A B, still sounds like a major scale with a strange, soft middle. Its subdominant side is Western (F, Dm) and its tonic side is golden (Cg, vEg).

Bend more notes and you trade Western triads for golden ones. Here are four scales along the way: none bent, two bent toward one golden chord, two bent toward two, and four bent toward three.

An inflection of C major moves each of its seven notes by −1, 0 or +1 sixth-step: $3^7 = 2187$ scales, from C major itself to its two lifts (all notes up, all notes down). Counting their triads (computed for all 2187):

  • 419 inflections contain at least one golden triad: 354 contain one, 63 two and only 2 contain three. None contains more than three.
  • The golden triads that occur are exactly the six golden projections of C major's consonant triads (Chapter 35): Cg = π(C), vDg = π(Dm), vEg = π(Em), Fg = π(F), Gg = π(G) and vAg = π(Am). Each occurs in 81 inflections (its three notes are fixed, the other four free: $3^4 = 81$).

Write W for the number of consonant Western triads (major or minor) and G for the number of golden triads. More of one costs some of the other. The Pareto frontier, the inflections that cannot gain a triad of one kind without losing one of the other, is:

Why bends come in pairs. Each projection keeps one note of its triad and bends the other two (Chapter 35): Cg needs E↓ and G↑ and keeps C; vEg needs the same two bends and keeps B. So Cg and vEg come together for the price of two bent notes, and that pair is the golden Ionian. Similarly Fg and vAg both need C↑ and A↓. Other pairs clash: Cg keeps C, while Fg must raise it; Gg keeps G, while Cg must raise it. Working through the six projections, the largest clash-free sets are {Cg, vDg, vEg} and {Fg, Gg, vAg}, which is why three is the maximum.

The golden Ionian

The golden Ionian, C D vE F ^G A B, is the best inflection of the major scale in the sense of the frontier. It bends exactly the two notes the tonic needs to become golden. It keeps the subdominant side of the key Western: F and Dm, and also the diminished triad B D F. And it gains vEg = vE ^G B, the circle neighbour of Cg, which reaches Cg by the leading tone B rising to C. Its steps are 6 5 4 7 5 6 3 sixth-steps, five different sizes: it is neither well formed nor Myhill. The name is a proposal (GOLDEN_BLENDING.md §14, question 9).

Well-formed inflections. Only 7 of the 2187 inflections have Myhill's property: C major, its two lifts, and four silver scales, C D E ^F ^G vA vB (5 silver triads), ^C D vE ^F G vA vB, ^C D vE ^F G A vB and ^C D vE ^F ^G A vB (3 silver triads each). None of them contains a golden triad. Bending the major scale toward gold always breaks its regularity.

Western mirror. The Western analogue of an inflection is chromatic alteration within one key: the harmonic minor raises one degree of the natural minor and the melodic minor two, and the modes of Chapter 6, taken on one tonic in order of brightness, differ from their neighbours by one note. Those alterations move a note by a semitone and stay on the 12-ET grid; golden inflections move notes by a third of a semitone and change their layer.

Definition. For $\epsilon \in \{-1, 0, 1\}^7$ the inflection of C major is $\{d_i + \epsilon_i\}$, where $d = (0, 6, 12, 15, 21, 27, 33)$ in sixth-steps.

Proposition (golden triads of an inflection). The golden triads contained in inflections of C major are exactly the projections of its six major and minor triads, and a set of them can occur together exactly when no bend required by one contradicts a note kept or bent the other way by another. The maximal compatible sets are {Cg, vDg, vEg} (bends D↓ E↓ F↑ G↑) and {Fg, Gg, vAg} (bends C↑ D↑ A↓ B↓). Compatible pairs: (Cg, vEg), (Cg, vDg), (vDg, vEg), (Fg, vAg), (Fg, Gg), (Gg, vAg), (Fg, vEg). (Exhaustive over 2187 inflections; the pair counts are 26, 2, 2, 26, 2, 2 and 3 inflections with exactly those two golden triads.)

Frontier. A pair $(W, G)$ is on the Pareto frontier if no inflection has $W' \ge W$ and $G' \ge G$ with one inequality strict. The frontier points are (6, 0), (3, 1), (2, 2), (0, 3). (Exhaustive.)

Centroid scales

The averaging principle says that the golden triad is the average of three Western triads: Cg is the centre of C major, C minor and C augmented (Chapter 25). Apply the same idea to whole scales: average two parts C major with one part C minor, degree by degree. Every degree that differs moves a third of the way toward minor. The result sounds like a major scale seen through smoked glass. Its tonic chord, C vE G, has a golden-sized third but a plain fifth, and it is not one of the named chords at all.

Write each 7-note mode on C as seven numbers of sixth-steps (C major = 0 6 12 15 21 27 33). The centroid of three modes is their degree-by-degree average. A sum of three multiples of 3, divided by 3, is a whole number, so the centroid of any three 12-ET modes lies exactly on the 36-ET grid. Averages of two do not: (Ionian + Aeolian)/2 puts its third at 10.5 sixth-steps, between grid points.

Taking 11 modes on C (the seven diatonic modes, harmonic and melodic minor, Lydian augmented and harmonic major) and all $\binom{13}{3} = 286$ ways of choosing three of them with repetition gives 225 distinct centroid scales. Of these, 44 contain a golden triad and 16 contain the tonic golden triad Cg. Some named centroids:

Three findings, two of them negative

1. (2·major + minor)/3 has no chord. The author's natural first candidate, two parts major to one part minor, gives C D vE F G vA vB. Its tonic chord is C vE G, written C(v3): a golden third under a perfect fifth. That is none of the seven named triad types, and in fact the scale contains no triad of any of those types anywhere (computed). In the roughness model on harmonic tones C(v3) measures 1.346 in close position from C4, slightly above C major (1.319) and C minor (1.342) and well below Cg (1.498) (measurement). It is a coloured major chord, not a new kind of home.

2. Cg needs Lydian augmented. For the centroid to contain Cg its third must average to 11 sixth-steps and its fifth to 22. The third is easy (two major thirds and one minor: 12 + 12 + 9 = 33). The fifth is not: among the eleven modes, fifths are 21 (perfect), 18 (Locrian) or 24 (Lydian augmented only), and $21 + 21 + 24 = 66$ or $18 + 24 + 24 = 66$ are the only ways to reach 22. Both need Lydian augmented, so every one of the 21 mode triples whose centroid contains Cg includes it. The golden fifth lies a third of the way from the perfect fifth to the augmented fifth, and a scale has to supply the augmented fifth for the average to find it.

3. The silver centroid is maximally even. (Ionian + Aeolian + Locrian)/3 averages the three modes whose tonic triads are C, Cm and Cdim, so it is the scale version of Cs = (C + Cm + Cdim)/3. It is C vD ^E♭ F vG ^A♭ ^B♭, steps 5 5 5 5 5 6 5: the maximally even 7-note scale of 36-ET, unique up to transposition (Chapter 36 gives the transposition C vD ^E♭ F vG ^A♭ B♭), with exactly three triads, all silver. The averaging principle produces a regular scale only on the silver side.

The best golden centroid, (harmonic minor + Lydian augmented + harmonic major)/3 = C D vE ^F ^G ^A♭ B, holds three golden triads, Cg, vEg and ^A♭g, consecutive on the golden circle, and shares five notes (C vE ^G ^A♭ B) with golden7. It is irregular, like every golden centroid; the averaging principle explains single chords better than it builds scales.

Theorem (grid). If $x, y, z \in 3\mathbb{Z}$ (12-ET pitches in sixth-steps) then $(x + y + z)/3 \in \mathbb{Z}$, so the centroid of three 12-ET scales is a 36-ET scale. For two scales, $(x + y)/2 \in \mathbb{Z}$ iff $x \equiv y \pmod 2$. Since $x \equiv y \pmod 3$ already, this means $x \equiv y \pmod 6$, a difference of an even number of semitones, and then the average $x + (y - x)/2$ is again a multiple of 3: a 12-ET pitch. ∎

Counts (exhaustive over the 286 multisets of 3 of the 11 modes): 225 distinct centroids; 44 contain a golden triad; 16 distinct centroids, from 21 multisets, contain Cg, and all 21 multisets include Lydian augmented. Twenty of them reach the golden fifth as $(21 + 21 + 24)/3$ and one, (Locrian + 2·Lydian aug.)/3, as $(18 + 24 + 24)/3$; it gives the same scale, C vD vE vF♯ ^G vA vB, as (Phrygian + Lydian + Lydian aug.)/3.

Silver centroid. Ionian $(0, 6, 12, 15, 21, 27, 33)$ + Aeolian $(0, 6, 9, 15, 21, 24, 30)$ + Locrian $(0, 3, 9, 15, 18, 24, 30)$ $= (0, 15, 30, 45, 60, 75, 93)$, divided by 3: $(0, 5, 10, 15, 20, 25, 31)$.

The hybrid limit

If bending C major is not the best way to mix Western and golden chords, what is? Search every seven-note scale of 36-ET and the best mixtures turn out to be small and specific. Here is one that holds three Western and two golden triads: C ^C D E F vA A. It is the pentachord C D E F A with two extra notes, vA and ^C, which turn F and A minor into their golden projections Fg and vAg.

There are $\binom{35}{6} = 1{,}623{,}160$ seven-note scales of 36-ET that contain C. For each one, count its consonant Western triads W and its golden triads G. The results (exhaustive):

  • W can reach 7 (14 scales; for example C C♯ D E F A♭ A, a 12-ET collection with the triads C♯, F, A, C♯m, Dm, Fm, Am) and G can reach 5 (only golden7 itself, in the 7 transpositions that contain C).
  • As soon as a scale has one golden triad it has at most 3 Western ones, and as soon as it has one Western triad it has at most 3 golden ones.
  • The Pareto frontier is (7, 0), (3, 2), (2, 3), (0, 5), reached by 14, 56, 28 and 7 scales. 672 scales have W ≥ 2 and G ≥ 2; 56 have W ≥ 3 and G ≥ 2.

The best hybrids are not inflections of C major. The (3, 2) scale keeps the Western pentachord C D E F A and adds vA and ^C, which are exactly the two notes that turn F (F A C) and Am (A C E) into Fg (^C F vA) and vAg (vA ^C E). F and Am are an L-pair (they share A and C), so their projections are neighbours on the golden circle. The best inflection, the golden Ionian, reaches only (2, 2).

No well-formed hybrid

The most important result is a negative one. Of the 1,623,160 scales, 70 are well formed (Myhill) and contain C. Seven of them contain a Western consonant triad (the diatonic scale, in its seven transpositions that contain C), seven contain a golden triad (golden7, likewise), and none contains both. Method. Each scale is a 36-bit mask containing bit 0; each triad type has 36 masks; W and G are counted by mask inclusion over all $\binom{35}{6}$ scales. Theorem (no well-formed hybrid). No 7-note well-formed scale of 36-ET contains both a major or minor triad and a golden triad. (Exhaustive; the table lists one scale per generator class, and every Myhill scale containing C is a rotation or transposition of one of them.)

Why the frontier example works. π(F) = Fg = ^C F vA and π(Am) = vAg = vA ^C E (Chapter 35): the two projections bend the same two notes, C up and A down. Adding the bent copies ^C and vA to a collection that keeps C and A keeps both Western triads and gains both golden ones, at the cost of two extra notes: C ^C E F vA A has F, Am, Fg and vAg in six notes. The seventh note can add one more Western triad, and only one (W ≤ 3 once G ≥ 1): Dm with D, as in the example, or C with G, A with C♯, Fm with A♭ (computed).

Golden colour chords

Western harmony enriches a major key by borrowing chords from the parallel minor: F minor in C major, or A♭ major (Chapter 11). A golden key can lend chords the same way. In C major, replace F by its golden image Fg, A minor by vAg, G by Gg: the chord keeps one note and bends the other two by a sixth-step. Held on a strong beat, the golden chord is not a passing chord but a colour, and the next Western chord is a release.

The colour chords come in pairs that are neighbours on the golden circle, so the two of a pair can follow each other directly.

Definition. A golden colour chord in a Western key is the golden projection π(X) of a diatonic triad X, used in place of X on a strong beat or held, rather than as a passing chord between two other chords. It is golden modal mixture: the chord is borrowed from the golden layer of the key, not from the parallel mode.

A major triad keeps its root and bends its third down and its fifth up (Cg = C(v3,^5)); a minor triad keeps its fifth and bends its root down and its third up. Every colour chord is exactly two sixth-steps from its diatonic original, and no Western triad is closer than that to any golden triad (Chapter 35). The diminished vii° = B D F is farther from gold: its nearest golden triad, ^B♭g, is four sixth-steps away, so it has no colour chord in this sense.

Three facts about colour chords

  • Colour pairs. The projection turns the leading-tone exchange (L: C ↔ Em, F ↔ Am, G ↔ Bm) into one step of the golden circle (Chapter 35). So Cg → vEg (golden I to golden iii) and Fg → vAg (golden IV to golden vi) move one voice by a semitone, and the pairs can be used as two-chord colour gestures. The third pair, Gg → vBg = π(Bm), leaves the key, since Bm is not diatonic in C.
  • Colour chords are pivots. The same golden chord images a diatonic triad of a second key a silver third away (Chapter 35): Fg is golden IV of C major and golden ii of ^E♭ major. A colour chord held long enough can turn into a modulation.
  • The cost in roughness (measurement, harmonic tones, Sethares model of explore.py). With each diatonic triad in close root position (root between C4 and B4) and its colour chord reached by the smoothest motion, the colour chord is rougher by 0.18 to 0.20 on every degree: C 1.319 → Cg 1.498, Dm 1.238 → vDg 1.417, Em 1.149 → vEg 1.333, F 1.090 → Fg 1.284, G 1.020 → Gg 1.220, Am 0.977 → vAg 1.175. (The one triad that gets smoother, Bdim 1.201 → ^B♭g 1.135, is the one without a colour chord at distance 2.) So on harmonic instruments a colour chord adds a measurable veil, and the return to the diatonic triad is a release.

Using them (proposals)

  1. Anchor the melody on the common tone (F over Fg, E over vAg, G over Gg). The colour then happens around a steady line, as in the first example, where the melody touches one bent note and returns.
  2. Follow a colour chord by a diatonic chord that contains its bent notes' Western originals, or by the original itself: the release undoes the bend.
  3. Use the circle pairs (Cg → vEg, Fg → vAg) as short golden excursions inside a Western phrase.
  4. Keep the arrival Western. Golden V (Gg) before I colours the dominant but weakens the leading tone, since B becomes vB; the first example risks it once, after ii, and the phrase still lands on C, which is heard as the release.
Western mirror. Borrowing iv into a major key changes one note of IV by a semitone (F A C → F A♭ C). A golden colour chord changes two notes, each by a third of a semitone, and keeps one. Modal mixture moves within 12-ET; golden mixture moves between its layers. Both are colour, and both are heard against the diatonic chord they replace.

Definition. π(X) is the golden triad at minimal voice-leading distance from X. For a major triad $M_r$, π$(M_r) = G_r$; for a minor triad $m_r$, π$(m_r) = G_{r-1}$; both at distance 2, uniquely. (Theorem with exhaustive check, Chapter 35.)

Lower bound. A golden triad has one note on each layer of 36-ET and a Western triad has all three on one layer, so at least two voices change layer, and the distance is at least 2. ∎

The six colour chords of a major key on $t$ have golden roots $t + \{0, 5, 11, 15, 21, 26\}$: the major triads on 0, 15, 21 keep their roots and the minor triads on 6, 12, 27 project to 5, 11, 26. The circle pairs are the roots differing by 11 (one golden-circle step): $(0, 11)$ and $(15, 26)$; $(21, 32)$ would need π(Bm) = vBg, outside the key. (Theorem.)

Exercise 38.1. Which two notes of C major does the golden Ionian bend, and which two golden triads does that create? Why is Fg impossible in the same scale?

Answer

E down to vE and G up to ^G, creating Cg = C vE ^G and vEg = vE ^G B. Fg = ^C F vA needs C raised to ^C, but Cg keeps C, so the two cannot share an inflection (the scale has one note per degree).

Exercise 38.2. Compute the centroid (Ionian + Ionian + Aeolian)/3 degree by degree and check that its third is a golden third.

Answer

Thirds $(12 + 12 + 9)/3 = 11$ (vE), sixths $(27 + 27 + 24)/3 = 26$ (vA), sevenths $(33 + 33 + 30)/3 = 32$ (vB); the other degrees are equal in both modes. So C D vE F G vA vB, with a golden third C–vE of 11 sixth-steps; the fifth stays G (21), so the tonic chord is C(v3), not Cg.

Exercise 38.3. Show that a scale containing both F and Fg needs at least five notes, and find one with F, Am, Fg and vAg in six notes.

Answer

F = F A C and Fg = ^C F vA share only F, so together they need 5 notes: C ^C F vA A. Adding E gives Am (A C E) and vAg (vA ^C E): C ^C E F vA A, six notes with F, Am, Fg, vAg. Adding D (for Dm) gives the (3, 2) hybrid of the text.

Exercise 38.4 (listening). In the colour-chord example, which colour chord sounds most like a coloured version of its original, and which most like a different chord? Is the measured roughness difference a guide?

Answer

A matter for the ear; there is no single right answer. The measured increase is nearly the same for all six (0.18 to 0.20), so roughness does not explain a difference. What differs is context: Fg follows I and keeps F under the melody, so it is heard as a veiled IV; Gg follows ii and replaces the dominant, so its bent leading tone vB makes it sound less like V.
