The 72 keys of 36-ET

36-ET has 36 tonics, each with a major and a minor key: 72 keys. Each 12-ET key exists on the plain layer, a sixth-step higher (^) and a sixth-step lower (v). Keys on one layer relate exactly as in 12-ET; keys on different layers share no notes, so changing layer needs golden pivots or voice leading by sixth-steps (§24.6, §32.1).

Signatures for the ^ and v layers. A key on the ^ or v layer raises or lowers every note of a 12-ET key by the same sixth-step, so it can carry that key's signature plus one up or down mark: ^D major has the signature of D major with an up (§38.1). For a v key, the letter-faithful reading takes the 12-ET key a semitone above: the standard name vC♯ is also vD♭, so vC♯ major can carry the signature of C♯ major (7 sharps) or of D♭ major (5 flats) with a down. This is the book's suggested convention, not an established standard. Golden scales do not fit key signatures; golden music is written with a plain signature and inline accidentals.

Common notes under transposition

How many notes a scale shares with its own transposition by $t$ sixth-steps, for the major scale (which shares nothing with any key off its layer), golden7, golden13 and silver13. The chain shift is the number of golden sixths that make the transposition ($t \equiv 25j$, so $j \equiv 13t \pmod{36}$). For golden scales the count is $\max(0, n - |j|)$, a theorem (§24.6): the nearest golden keys are a golden sixth or third away (^A♭ and vE), and keys a single sixth-step away share nothing. silver13 is not a chain, and its best transpositions (by a semitone or a bronze third) share ten notes.
